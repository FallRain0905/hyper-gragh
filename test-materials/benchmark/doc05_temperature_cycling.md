# Temperature-Dependent Cycling Stability and Capacity Fading Mechanisms in Vanadium Redox Flow Batteries

## Abstract

Understanding the temperature-dependent degradation behavior of vanadium redox flow batteries (VRFBs) is essential for optimizing long-term operational performance across diverse climatic conditions. This study systematically investigates the cycling stability and capacity fading mechanisms of a laboratory-scale VRFB system operated at 25°C, 40°C, and 60°C over extended cycle life. At a constant current density of 80 mA/cm², the battery achieved initial discharge capacities of 1420 mAh, 1415 mAh, and 1435 mAh at 25°C, 40°C, and 60°C respectively. After 500 charge-discharge cycles, the capacity retention decreased markedly with increasing temperature: 85% at 25°C, 74% at 40°C, and only 60% at 60°C. Coulombic efficiency exhibited a gradual decline from 97.2% to 94.8% at 25°C, from 97.0% to 92.1% at 40°C, and from 96.8% to 88.5% at 60°C over the same cycling period. Post-cycling analysis revealed that vanadium crossover rates increased exponentially with temperature, measuring 2.1 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s at 25°C, 3.8 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s at 40°C, and 6.5 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s at 60°C after 200 cycles. Electrolyte imbalance, quantified by the state-of-charge (SOC) difference between the positive and negative half-cells, diverged progressively: the SOC differential reached 8% at 25°C, 15% at 40°C, and 24% at 60°C after 500 cycles. Periodic electrolyte remixing was demonstrated to partially restore capacity, with recovery efficiencies of 62% at 25°C, 55% at 40°C, and 41% at 60°C relative to the faded capacity. These findings elucidate the coupled roles of thermal acceleration of vanadium ion transport through the ion exchange membrane and the resulting electrolyte imbalance as primary drivers of temperature-enhanced capacity fading in VRFBs.

## 1. Introduction

Vanadium redox flow batteries have emerged as one of the most promising electrochemical energy storage technologies for grid-scale applications due to their decoupled energy and power ratings, long cycle life potential, and the unique advantage of employing the same vanadium element in both half-cell electrolytes. However, the long-term operational stability of VRFBs remains a critical challenge that limits widespread commercial deployment, particularly in applications subject to variable thermal environments. Temperature exerts profound effects on virtually every aspect of VRFB performance: electrochemical kinetics, mass transport, membrane permeability, and the thermodynamic stability of vanadium species in sulfuric acid supporting electrolyte.

While considerable research has focused on individual degradation modes in VRFBs at ambient or single-temperature conditions, systematic investigations spanning a broad temperature range with quantified capacity fading trajectories remain relatively scarce. The existing literature suggests that elevated temperatures accelerate capacity fade through multiple parallel pathways, yet the relative contributions of each mechanism at different temperatures and the quantitative relationships between thermal conditions, cycling duration, and performance metrics are not fully established. This knowledge gap hinders the development of accurate predictive degradation models and temperature-adaptive battery management strategies.

The present work addresses this gap by conducting controlled long-term cycling experiments at three representative temperatures: 25°C representing standard room-temperature operation, 40°C corresponding to moderate thermal stress typical of warm-climate installations without active cooling, and 60°C representing severe thermal stress that may occur during fault conditions or in poorly ventilated enclosures. By maintaining consistent electrolyte compositions, membrane materials, and current density protocols across all temperature conditions, this study isolates the thermal contribution to capacity fading and provides quantitative benchmarks for temperature-dependent degradation rates. The investigation further examines the effectiveness of periodic electrolyte remixing as a mitigation strategy for temperature-accelerated capacity loss.

## 2. Experimental Methods

All cycling experiments were conducted using a laboratory-scale VRFB single cell with an active electrode area of 25 cm². The cell employed graphite felt electrodes (SGL GFD4.6, 4.6 mm thickness) compressed to 80% of their original thickness, separated by a Nafion 115 proton exchange membrane. The electrolyte consisted of 1.6 M V³⁺ in 4.5 M total sulfate (prepared from VOSO₄ and subsequent electrochemical reduction) for the negative half-cell and 1.6 M VO²⁺ in 4.5 M total sulfate for the positive half-cell, with 60 mL of electrolyte in each reservoir.

Charge-discharge cycling was performed at a constant current density of 80 mA/cm² between voltage limits of 0.9 V and 1.65 V using a Bio-Logic VMP-3 potentiostat/galvanostat. Three identical cells were assembled and operated in temperature-controlled environmental chambers maintained at 25°C, 40°C, and 60°C (±0.5°C) respectively. Electrolyte circulation was driven by peristaltic pumps at a constant flow rate of 40 mL/min. Each cycling test was continued until the discharge capacity fell below 45% of the initial value or until 500 cycles were completed, whichever occurred first.

Capacity retention was calculated as the ratio of the discharge capacity at cycle N to the initial discharge capacity at cycle 1, expressed as a percentage. Coulombic efficiency (CE) was determined as the ratio of discharge capacity to charge capacity for each cycle. Electrolyte crossover was assessed by periodically sampling 0.5 mL aliquots from each half-cell reservoir and analyzing vanadium concentrations using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES). The state-of-charge (SOC) of each half-cell was estimated from the ratio of V³⁺ to total vanadium in the negative electrolyte and VO₂⁺ to total vanadium in the positive electrolyte, measured by UV-Vis spectrophotometry. Electrolyte remixing was performed at 100-cycle intervals by transferring the entire electrolyte volume from both reservoirs into a single vessel, mixing thoroughly for 30 minutes under nitrogen atmosphere, and redistributing equally back to the respective half-cells.

## 3. Results and Discussion

### 3.1 Temperature-Dependent Capacity Fading Trajectories

The capacity fading behavior exhibited strong and systematic dependence on operating temperature throughout the 500-cycle test duration. At 25°C, the battery demonstrated relatively stable performance for the initial 200 cycles, with capacity declining gradually from an initial 1420 mAh to 1310 mAh (92% retention). Beyond cycle 200, the fade rate accelerated modestly, reaching 85% capacity retention at cycle 500. The fading trajectory at 40°C followed a qualitatively similar pattern but with noticeably steeper decline rates: capacity retention stood at 88% after 100 cycles, 82% after 200 cycles, and progressively decreased to 74% by cycle 500. The most severe fading was observed at 60°C, where the capacity dropped to 78% of its initial value within the first 100 cycles, to 70% after 200 cycles, and reached only 60% retention at cycle 500. Notably, the cell at 60°C would have fallen below the 45% termination threshold had the test been extended to approximately 850 cycles, based on the observed linear fade rate in the later cycling stages.

The capacity fade rates, calculated as the average percentage capacity loss per 100 cycles, were 3.0% per 100 cycles at 25°C, 5.2% per 100 cycles at 40°C, and 8.0% per 100 cycles at 60°C. These values reveal a non-linear acceleration of fading with temperature: increasing from 25°C to 40°C (a 15°C rise) increased the fade rate by a factor of 1.73, while increasing from 40°C to 60°C (a 20°C rise) increased it by a factor of 1.54. This non-linearity suggests that the temperature dependence of capacity fading cannot be captured by a simple Arrhenius-type single-process model and instead reflects the combined activation of multiple degradation pathways with differing temperature sensitivities.

The voltage efficiency (VE) also exhibited temperature-dependent behavior, though the trends differed from capacity retention. VE values were 85.3%, 87.1%, and 89.2% at 25°C, 40°C, and 60°C respectively during the initial 50 cycles, reflecting the beneficial effect of temperature on electrochemical kinetics and mass transport. However, VE degraded faster at elevated temperatures due to increased shunt currents and parasitic reactions, such that by cycle 500, the VE values had converged to 82.5% at 25°C, 81.8% at 40°C, and 79.4% at 60°C. Energy efficiency followed the combined trends of CE and VE, starting at 82.8% at 25°C and declining to 78.2% at cycle 500, while the 60°C cell started at 86.4% but dropped more sharply to 71.5% by cycle 500.

### 3.2 Coulombic Efficiency Degradation and Vanadium Crossover

Coulombic efficiency provides critical insight into the extent of parasitic processes that consume charge without contributing to reversible energy storage. The initial CE values were remarkably consistent across temperatures: 97.2% at 25°C, 97.0% at 40°C, and 96.8% at 60°C. These values indicate that even at the beginning of life, elevated temperatures introduce measurable increases in charge inefficiency, primarily through accelerated vanadium crossover and enhanced side reaction kinetics.

The CE degradation over 500 cycles followed distinctly different trajectories at each temperature. At 25°C, CE declined gradually to 94.8%, representing a total loss of 2.4 percentage points. At 40°C, the decline was more pronounced, with CE reaching 92.1% at cycle 500 (a 4.9 percentage point loss). The most dramatic CE degradation occurred at 60°C, where CE plummeted to 88.5% by cycle 500, corresponding to a total loss of 8.3 percentage points. Importantly, the rate of CE degradation itself accelerated over the cycling duration at all temperatures, suggesting a positive feedback mechanism in which early-stage degradation creates conditions that accelerate subsequent degradation.

Vanadium crossover measurements conducted at 50-cycle intervals revealed the underlying transport mechanism driving CE loss. The effective vanadium crossover diffusion coefficients, calculated from the measured concentration changes in each half-cell, increased with both temperature and cycling duration. At cycle 50, the crossover rates were 1.2 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s at 25°C, 2.0 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s at 40°C, and 3.4 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s at 60°C. By cycle 200, these values had risen to 2.1 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s, 3.8 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s, and 6.5 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s respectively. The cycling-induced increase in crossover rates (a 75% increase from cycle 50 to cycle 200 at 25°C, 90% at 40°C, and 91% at 60°C) indicates that membrane degradation and chemical changes in the membrane structure compound the purely thermal acceleration of diffusion.

The crossover data further revealed an asymmetric transport behavior: vanadium ions migrated predominantly from the positive half-cell (VO²⁺/VO₂⁺) to the negative half-cell (V²⁺/V³⁺) at all temperatures. This directionality arises from the concentration gradients established by the differential stability of vanadium species: the V(V) species (VO₂⁺) exhibits higher membrane permeability due to its smaller hydrated ionic radius and lower charge density compared to V(II) species. At 60°C, the cumulative vanadium accumulation in the negative electrolyte reached 0.18 M after 200 cycles, compared to 0.06 M at 25°C, directly contributing to the larger capacity losses observed at elevated temperatures.

### 3.3 Electrolyte Imbalance and SOC Divergence

A direct consequence of asymmetric vanadium crossover is the progressive development of electrolyte imbalance, manifested as a growing divergence in the state-of-charge (SOC) between the positive and negative half-cells. Under ideal balanced operation, both half-cells maintain identical SOC values throughout cycling. However, as vanadium species accumulate asymmetrically and side reactions consume active species at different rates, the SOC profiles diverge.

At 25°C, the SOC difference between half-cells remained below 3% for the first 100 cycles, then gradually increased to 8% by cycle 500. This modest imbalance indicates that the battery at room temperature maintains reasonably balanced operation over extended cycling, with sufficient self-regulating mechanisms to prevent runaway divergence. At 40°C, the SOC divergence progressed more rapidly: the differential reached 6% after 100 cycles, 11% after 200 cycles, and 15% after 500 cycles. The accelerated SOC divergence at 40°C correlates with the higher vanadium crossover rates and the increased prevalence of water transfer and side reactions that asymmetrically alter the active species inventory in each half-cell.

The most severe electrolyte imbalance developed at 60°C, where the SOC differential reached 10% after only 50 cycles, 17% after 100 cycles, and 24% after 500 cycles. This substantial imbalance directly limits the usable capacity of the battery because the half-cell with the lower effective SOC determines the capacity-limiting electrode. The relationship between SOC divergence and capacity retention follows an approximately linear trend across all temperatures: each 1% increase in SOC differential corresponds to a capacity loss of approximately 0.6-0.8% relative to the theoretical balanced capacity. At 60°C, the 24% SOC differential accounts for roughly 60% of the total 40% capacity loss, with the remainder attributed to irreversible vanadium precipitation and active species depletion through parasitic reactions.

The chemical origin of the SOC divergence lies in the differential rates of the parasitic oxygen and hydrogen evolution reactions at the positive and negative electrodes respectively. At elevated temperatures, water oxidation at the positive electrode becomes increasingly favorable, converting VO₂⁺ back to VO²⁺ while evolving oxygen, which effectively reduces the SOC of the positive half-cell. Concurrently, hydrogen evolution at the negative electrode during charging consumes protons and reduces V³⁺ to V²⁺, altering the negative half-cell SOC in the opposite direction. These side reactions create a compounding effect: as the SOC diverges, the overpotentials at each electrode increase, further driving the parasitic reactions and accelerating the imbalance.

### 3.4 Effectiveness of Electrolyte Remixing as Mitigation Strategy

Given the central role of electrolyte imbalance in capacity fading, periodic remixing of the positive and negative electrolytes represents a straightforward mitigation approach that can restore species inventory balance without replacing the electrolyte. In this study, remixing was performed at 100-cycle intervals by combining and thoroughly mixing the electrolytes from both half-cells before redistributing them equally.

The capacity recovery achieved through remixing showed clear temperature dependence, consistent with the extent of imbalance present at each condition. At 25°C, where the SOC divergence was modest, the first remixing event at cycle 100 restored 89% of the faded capacity, recovering the system from 96% retention back to 99.2% of the initial capacity. Subsequent remixing events at cycles 200, 300, 400, and 500 achieved progressively lower recovery fractions as irreversible degradation accumulated, with the cycle 500 remixing restoring 62% of the accumulated fade. Over the full 500-cycle test, periodic remixing improved the net capacity retention from 85% to 92.5% at 25°C.

At 40°C, the effectiveness of remixing was moderately reduced. The first remixing at cycle 100 recovered 82% of the faded capacity, but by cycle 500, only 55% of the accumulated fade could be reversed. The net capacity retention with periodic remixing improved from 74% to 84% over 500 cycles. The reduced recovery efficiency at 40°C reflects the larger contribution of irreversible degradation mechanisms, including some vanadium precipitation and membrane chemical degradation, which remixing cannot address.

At 60°C, remixing provided only partial relief from capacity fading. The first remixing at cycle 100 recovered 71% of the faded capacity, but the recovery efficiency declined sharply with subsequent cycling. By cycle 500, only 41% of the accumulated fade was recoverable through remixing, yielding a net retention improvement from 60% to only 70.5%. The substantially diminished effectiveness of remixing at 60°C indicates that at this temperature, irreversible degradation mechanisms dominate the capacity loss. Post-mortem analysis of the membrane from the 60°C cell revealed significant chemical degradation including sulfonic acid group loss and polymer backbone cleavage, which increased membrane permeability permanently and could not be reversed by electrolyte remixing. Additionally, visible vanadium precipitate was observed in the positive electrolyte reservoir at 60°C after 300 cycles, representing an irreversible loss of active material from the dissolved electrolyte phase.

### 3.5 Degradation Mechanism Analysis and Comparative Assessment

The experimental results collectively support a degradation mechanism framework in which temperature acts as an accelerator of multiple coupled degradation pathways rather than activating a single dominant fade mechanism. At the foundation of the degradation hierarchy lies vanadium crossover through the ion exchange membrane, which is thermally activated with an effective activation energy of approximately 28-32 kJ/mol based on the measured diffusion coefficients. The crossover creates a primary electrolyte imbalance that directly reduces usable capacity by desynchronizing the SOC of the two half-cells.

Superimposed on the crossover-driven imbalance are temperature-accelerated side reactions. Oxygen evolution at the positive electrode and hydrogen evolution at the negative electrode become increasingly significant above 40°C, contributing to net coulombic inefficiency and further exacerbating the electrolyte imbalance. Water transport through the membrane, driven by electro-osmotic drag and hydraulic pressure gradients, compounds the imbalance by altering the concentration and volume of electrolyte in each half-cell asymmetrically.

The positive feedback nature of the degradation emerges from the interaction between these mechanisms. Increased crossover leads to higher concentrations of foreign vanadium species in each half-cell, which raises the ionic strength and modifies the local pH, both of which accelerate the precipitation of V(V) compounds from the positive electrolyte at elevated temperature. Membrane chemical degradation, in turn, increases the effective pore size and reduces the selectivity, permitting even faster crossover in later cycles. This compounding effect explains why the capacity fade rate accelerates over the cycling duration and why the temperature dependence of fading exceeds what would be predicted from the Arrhenius behavior of diffusion alone.

Comparing the relative importance of different mechanisms across temperatures, the analysis suggests that at 25°C, vanadium crossover accounts for approximately 70% of the total capacity fade, with side reactions and minor precipitation contributing the remaining 30%. At 40°C, crossover contributes approximately 55%, side reactions 30%, and precipitation 15% of the total fade. At 60°C, the picture shifts dramatically: crossover contributes only 35%, while side reactions and precipitation together account for 65% of the capacity loss. This shift in the dominant degradation mode has important implications for mitigation strategy selection: whereas crossover-reduction strategies such as membrane modification and remixing are effective at lower temperatures, higher-temperature operation demands strategies that also address side reaction suppression and precipitation inhibition, such as electrolyte additives and improved thermal management.

## 4. Conclusion

This study has provided a comprehensive quantitative characterization of temperature-dependent cycling stability and capacity fading in vanadium redox flow batteries through controlled long-term experiments at 25°C, 40°C, and 60°C. The principal findings establish that capacity fading accelerates non-linearly with temperature, with fade rates of 3.0%, 5.2%, and 8.0% per 100 cycles at the three test temperatures respectively. Coulombic efficiency degradation paralleled the capacity fading, declining by 2.4, 4.9, and 8.3 percentage points over 500 cycles at 25°C, 40°C, and 60°C.

Vanadium crossover through the Nafion 115 membrane was identified as the foundational degradation driver, with effective diffusion coefficients increasing from 2.1 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s at 25°C to 6.5 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s at 60°C after 200 cycles. The resulting electrolyte imbalance, quantified by SOC divergence between half-cells, reached 8% at 25°C, 15% at 40°C, and 24% at 60°C after 500 cycles. Periodic electrolyte remixing proved effective at mitigating crossover-driven capacity loss, improving net 500-cycle capacity retention from 85% to 92.5% at 25°C, from 74% to 84% at 40°C, and from 60% to 70.5% at 60°C.

The degradation mechanism analysis revealed a shift in the dominant fade mode with increasing temperature: from crossover-dominated at 25°C to a regime where side reactions and vanadium precipitation dominate at 60°C. This mechanistic shift explains the declining effectiveness of remixing at elevated temperatures and underscores the critical importance of active thermal management for VRFB systems operating in warm climates. For installations where passive cooling cannot maintain electrolyte temperature below 35°C, the incorporation of both periodic remixing protocols and electrolyte stabilization additives is recommended to achieve acceptable cycle life. Future work should focus on developing membranes with reduced temperature sensitivity of vanadium permeability and on quantifying the synergistic benefits of combining remixing with other degradation mitigation strategies under realistic variable-temperature operating profiles.
