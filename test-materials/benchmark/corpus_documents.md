# Baseline Performance Mapping of Vanadium Redox Flow Cells Assembled with Commercial Nafion Membranes: From Nafion 117 to Nafion 212

## Abstract

The vanadium redox flow battery (VRFB) continues to serve as the benchmark chemistry against which emerging redox flow architectures are evaluated. A critical yet often underreported source of performance variation in published VRFB datasets stems from the indiscriminate labeling of "Nafion" without specifying whether the membrane refers to the perfluorinated sulfonic acid family generically or to a specific grade such as Nafion 117 or Nafion 212. This study presents a systematic, side-by-side comparison of cell-level metrics obtained with Nafion 117 (183 um dry thickness) and Nafion 212 (50.8 um dry thickness) under rigorously controlled test protocols. At a current density of 100 mA/cm2 and 25 C, the Nafion 117 cell delivered a coulombic efficiency (CE) of 96.2%, a voltage efficiency (VE) of 84.1%, and an energy efficiency (EE) of 80.9%. Reducing membrane thickness by switching to Nafion 212 raised VE to 88.3% because of lower area resistance (0.45 ohm cm2 versus 1.85 ohm cm2 for Nafion 117), but CE dropped to 92.5% owing to increased vanadium crossover. When current density was increased from 40 to 200 mA/cm2, the Nafion 117 cell exhibited a monotonic increase in CE from 91.4% to 97.8%, while VE decreased from 89.6% to 76.2%; consequently, EE peaked at 83.6% near 80 mA/cm2 before declining to 74.5% at 200 mA/cm2. Elevating temperature to 40 C aggravated vanadium crossover for both membranes, reducing CE by 1.8-2.4 percentage points relative to 25 C operation. Long-term cycling over 200 charge-discharge cycles confirmed that the Nafion 212 cell suffered faster capacity fading, with a capacity retention of 78.3% compared to 86.7% for Nafion 117. These results highlight that membrane selection within the Nafion family is not a trivial substitute but a deliberate design choice that trades ohmic losses against crossover-induced Coulombic penalties and long-term capacity stability.

## Introduction

Vanadium redox flow batteries have garnered substantial research attention for grid-scale stationary energy storage because of their decoupled power and energy ratings, deep discharge capability, and avoidance of cross-contamination through the use of a single electroactive element in four oxidation states. The all-vanadium chemistry eliminates the irreversible capacity loss observed in zinc-bromine and iron-chromium systems where active species from opposite half-cells react to form insoluble or inert products. Nevertheless, the practical deployment of VRFBs depends critically on the membrane separator that must simultaneously conduct protons to complete the internal circuit and block vanadium ion migration between the positive and negative electrolytes.

Among the dozens of alternative membranes reported in the literature, sulfonated poly(ether ether ketone) (SPEEK), polybenzimidazole (PBI), and composite membranes incorporating inorganic fillers have been proposed to reduce vanadium permeability and material cost. Yet the vast majority of academic VRFB studies continue to employ commercial Nafion membranes as a baseline, precisely because the perfluorinated backbone confers exceptional chemical stability in the strongly acidic and oxidizing vanadium electrolyte environment. The term "Nafion," however, masks significant property differences across product grades. Nafion 117, with an equivalent weight of 1100 g/mol and a dry thickness of 183 um, has been the historical default. Nafion 212, featuring the same equivalent weight but a substantially thinner profile of 50.8 um, offers lower area resistance at the expense of reduced barrier thickness. Confounding matters further, some authors simply state that a "Nafion membrane" was used without clarifying the grade, rendering quantitative comparisons across studies problematic.

The present work addresses this ambiguity by establishing a rigorous, internally consistent performance baseline for VRFB single cells assembled with Nafion 117 and Nafion 212. We report coulombic efficiency, voltage efficiency, and energy efficiency measured across a broad current-density window (40-200 mA/cm2) at two operating temperatures (25 C and 40 C). Area resistance, vanadium crossover rates, and 200-cycle capacity retention are additionally quantified to link the observed efficiency trends to underlying transport phenomena. The dataset is intended to serve as a reference against which novel membranes can be benchmarked and to caution against the conflation of "Nafion" as a universal descriptor in VRFB literature.

## Section 1: Baseline Cell Performance with Nafion 117 at 25 C

Single cells with an active area of 25 cm2 were assembled with Nafion 117 membranes pretreated according to a standard three-step protocol involving boiling in hydrogen peroxide, deionized water, and sulfuric acid sequentially. The electrolyte consisted of 1.5 M VOSO4 in 3.0 M H2SO4 that was electrolytically precharged to yield approximately 50% state-of-charge in both half-cells. Graphite felt electrodes (SGL GFD4.6, 4.6 mm uncompressed thickness) served as the porous electrode material without additional thermal or chemical activation. All baseline measurements in this section were conducted at 25 C with an electrolyte flow rate of 40 mL/min per half-cell.

At a current density of 40 mA/cm2, the Nafion 117 cell delivered a CE of 91.4%, a VE of 89.6%, and an EE of 81.9%. The CE value reflects that not all charge passed during charge transfer is recoverable during discharge, primarily because vanadium ions diffuse across the membrane down their concentration gradients. The relatively high VE at this low current density indicates modest ohmic and activation overpotentials, consistent with the 1.85 ohm cm2 area resistance measured for Nafion 117 under fully hydrated conditions in 3.0 M H2SO4 at 25 C.

Increasing current density to 80 mA/cm2 produced a CE of 94.7% and a VE of 88.3%, yielding an EE of 83.6%. The CE increase arises because the duration of each charge-discharge cycle shortens as current density rises, thereby reducing the time available for diffusive vanadium crossover. The modest decrease in VE from 89.6% to 88.3% reflects growing ohmic losses that scale linearly with current density according to the relation eta_ohmic = i * R_area. At this operating point, Nafion 117 offers an attractive balance between crossover suppression and ohmic penalty.

At the standard reporting current density of 100 mA/cm2, the CE rose further to 96.2%, while VE declined to 84.1%. The resulting EE of 80.9% aligns with values commonly cited in the VRFB literature for Nafion 117 under comparable conditions, though direct comparison is often hampered by differences in electrode compression, flow field geometry, and electrolyte composition. The 3.9 percentage point gap between CE at 80 mA/cm2 and CE at 100 mA/cm2 demonstrates the sensitivity of coulombic metrics to current density, underscoring the necessity of reporting the test condition alongside any single-point efficiency claim.

At 160 mA/cm2, the cell achieved a CE of 97.3% and a VE of 79.4%, giving an EE of 77.3%. Pushing to 200 mA/cm2 further elevated CE to 97.8% but depressed VE to 76.2%, with EE falling to 74.5%. The monotonic VE decrease with increasing current density is expected because the ohmic overpotential across the Nafion 117 membrane alone reaches approximately 370 mV at 200 mA/cm2, a substantial fraction of the open-circuit voltage differential between the V(II)/V(III) and V(IV)/V(V) redox couples. These results establish that Nafion 117 performs optimally in the 60-100 mA/cm2 range when energy efficiency is the primary figure of merit, whereas operation above 160 mA/cm2 prioritizes Coulombic integrity at the expense of voltage yield.

## Section 2: Performance Comparison Between Nafion 117 and Nafion 212

To isolate the effect of membrane thickness, identical cell hardware, electrodes, and electrolytes were employed with Nafion 212 membranes substituted for Nafion 117. The area resistance of Nafion 212 measured under the same hydration and electrolyte conditions was 0.45 ohm cm2, representing a 75.7% reduction relative to Nafion 117. This difference originates from the thinner transport path for proton migration; because the specific conductivity of the sulfonic acid phase is comparable in both grades, the through-plane resistance scales approximately with thickness.

At 100 mA/cm2 and 25 C, the Nafion 212 cell exhibited a CE of 92.5%, a VE of 88.3%, and an EE of 81.7%. The comparison with Nafion 117 at the same condition reveals a clear trade-off: the thinner membrane improves VE by 4.2 percentage points (from 84.1% to 88.3%) owing to reduced ohmic loss, but CE decreases by 3.7 percentage points (from 96.2% to 92.5%) because the diminished barrier thickness facilitates greater vanadium ion transport. The EE values are remarkably similar, 80.9% for Nafion 117 versus 81.7% for Nafion 212, indicating that at this specific current density the two effects nearly compensate.

The VE advantage of Nafion 212 becomes more pronounced at higher current densities where ohmic losses dominate. At 160 mA/cm2, the Nafion 212 cell achieved a VE of 84.6% compared to 79.4% for Nafion 117, a 5.2 percentage point differential. However, the CE penalty also widened: Nafion 212 recorded 94.1% versus 97.3% for Nafion 117. The resulting EE values were 79.5% and 77.3% respectively, giving Nafion 212 a marginal 2.2 percentage point advantage at this higher loading. At 200 mA/cm2, the Nafion 212 cell still maintained a VE of 82.1% while its CE declined to 95.3%, producing an EE of 78.3% that exceeded the Nafion 117 value of 74.5% by 3.8 percentage points.

At lower current densities where crossover time per cycle is longer, the CE penalty for Nafion 212 becomes more severe. At 40 mA/cm2, the Nafion 212 cell delivered a CE of only 85.3% compared to 91.4% for Nafion 117, a 6.1 percentage point deficit. Combined with a modest VE advantage of 2.1 percentage points (91.7% versus 89.6%), the Nafion 212 EE of 78.2% actually fell below the Nafion 117 value of 81.9%. This inversion demonstrates that the relative performance ranking between membrane grades depends on the operating current density and cannot be generalized from a single test point.

The vanadium crossover rate was quantified using an ex-situ diffusion cell in which the receiving half-cell initially contained 1.5 M MgSO4 as a supporting electrolyte and the donor half-cell contained 1.5 M V(IV) in 3.0 M H2SO4. After 24 hours at 25 C, the V(IV) concentration in the receiving compartment reached 2.84 mM for Nafion 212 versus 0.97 mM for Nafion 117, corresponding to permeability coefficients of 1.72 x 10^-6 cm2/s and 0.59 x 10^-6 cm2/s respectively. The ratio of 2.92 is consistent with the geometric thickness ratio of 3.60 when accounting for the non-linear dependence of tortuosity on thickness in the perfluorinated matrix.

## Section 3: Temperature Dependence of Efficiency and Crossover

Temperature exerts opposing influences on the two primary loss mechanisms in VRFB membranes. Higher temperatures increase proton conductivity and reduce kinetic overpotentials at the electrode-electrolyte interface, both of which favor improved VE. Simultaneously, elevated temperature accelerates vanadium ion diffusion according to an Arrhenius relationship, thereby degrading CE. The net effect on EE and the crossover rate therefore depends on the activation energies of proton conduction and vanadium permeation.

For the Nafion 117 cell operated at 100 mA/cm2, raising the temperature from 25 C to 40 C improved VE from 84.1% to 86.5% while reducing CE from 96.2% to 94.4%. The VE gain of 2.4 percentage points reflects the enhanced membrane conductivity and faster electrode kinetics at the higher temperature. The CE loss of 1.8 percentage points confirms that vanadium crossover accelerates with temperature, though the magnitude is modest compared to the effect of switching membrane grades. EE changed from 80.9% to 81.7%, showing a slight improvement because the VE gain marginally exceeded the CE penalty.

The Nafion 212 cell showed a stronger temperature sensitivity because the thinner barrier offers less resistance to thermally activated diffusion. At 100 mA/cm2, increasing temperature to 40 C raised VE from 88.3% to 90.4% but depressed CE from 92.5% to 90.1%. The 2.4 percentage point CE reduction for Nafion 212 exceeds the 1.8 point loss for Nafion 117, confirming that thinner membranes are more vulnerable to temperature-induced crossover. EE remained nearly constant, changing from 81.7% to 81.5%. The crossover rate measured in the ex-situ diffusion cell at 40 C increased to 4.31 mM after 24 hours for Nafion 212 and 1.56 mM for Nafion 117, corresponding to increases of 51.8% and 60.8% respectively relative to the 25 C values.

The area resistance of both membranes decreased with temperature. Nafion 117 exhibited an area resistance of 1.52 ohm cm2 at 40 C compared to 1.85 ohm cm2 at 25 C, while Nafion 212 decreased from 0.45 ohm cm2 to 0.38 ohm cm2 over the same range. The apparent activation energy for proton conduction extracted from these two points is approximately 8.5 kJ/mol, consistent with the Grotthuss mechanism of proton hopping between sulfonic acid groups and water molecules. In contrast, the vanadium diffusion coefficients show a stronger temperature dependence corresponding to an activation energy near 22 kJ/mol, indicating that the crossover penalty will increasingly dominate at elevated temperatures unless membrane designs incorporate additional barriers.

## Section 4: Long-Term Cycling Stability and Capacity Retention

Short-term efficiency snapshots at a single current density do not capture the cumulative effect of vanadium crossover on electrolyte imbalance and capacity fading over extended operation. To assess this dimension, 200 consecutive charge-discharge cycles were performed at 100 mA/cm2 with a voltage window of 0.8 V to 1.65 V and a 10-minute constant-voltage hold at the charge cutoff. The capacity retention at cycle 200 relative to cycle 10 (after initial conditioning) was 86.7% for Nafion 117 and 78.3% for Nafion 212.

The faster capacity fade for Nafion 212 is directly attributable to the higher net vanadium transport during each cycle. Over 200 cycles at 100 mA/cm2, the cumulative vanadium crossover drives differential state-of-charge evolution in the positive and negative half-cells. Because the V(V) species at the positive electrode is more permeable than V(II) at the negative electrode, the net transport creates an electrolyte imbalance in which the positive electrolyte becomes progressively depleted of active vanadium while the negative electrolyte accumulates an excess. This imbalance manifests as a shrinking accessible capacity window because the limiting half-cell reaches full charge or discharge earlier in each cycle.

The average CE over the 200-cycle test was 95.8% for Nafion 117 and 91.6% for Nafion 212, values that are slightly lower than the initial single-point measurements due to the progressive accumulation of side reactions and electrolyte oxidation at the positive electrode over extended cycling. The capacity fade rate for Nafion 212, calculated as the linear regression slope of discharge capacity versus cycle number after the first 50 cycles, was approximately 0.11% per cycle compared to 0.07% per cycle for Nafion 117. Extrapolated to 1000 cycles, these rates predict capacity retentions of approximately 65% and 78% respectively, a difference that could significantly impact the levelized cost of storage for long-duration applications.

Periodic measurement of the electrolyte vanadium concentration in both half-cells confirmed the imbalance mechanism. After 200 cycles, the positive electrolyte vanadium concentration had decreased by 8.4% for Nafion 117 and 14.2% for Nafion 212 relative to their initial values, while the negative electrolyte showed corresponding increases of 6.1% and 11.8%. The sum of positive and negative deviations from the initial concentration is less than 100% in both cases because a fraction of vanadium is lost to precipitation of V(V) compounds and to slow air oxidation at the electrolyte reservoir surface, both of which represent irreversible capacity loss pathways.

## Discussion

The comprehensive dataset presented in the preceding sections reveals that the selection between Nafion 117 and Nafion 212 is a nuanced design decision governed by the intended operating regime of the VRFB system. The 4.1-fold difference in membrane thickness translates into a 3.1-fold difference in area resistance and an approximately 2.9-fold difference in vanadium permeability. These disparities produce quantitatively different efficiency landscapes across the current density and temperature design space.

At low current densities (below 60 mA/cm2), the dominant loss mechanism is vanadium crossover rather than ohmic polarization. In this regime, the thicker Nafion 117 membrane is unequivocally superior, delivering higher CE and EE. Nafion 212 should therefore be avoided for systems targeting prolonged charge-discharge durations at gentle current densities, such as solar time-shifting applications with 6-8 hour discharge durations. At moderate current densities (80-120 mA/cm2), the two membranes yield comparable EE values because the ohmic advantage of Nafion 212 is nearly offset by its crossover disadvantage. The near-identical EE values at 100 mA/cm2 (80.9% versus 81.7%) suggest that other factors such as membrane cost, mechanical durability during stack assembly, and water transport behavior should guide the selection in this intermediate regime.

At high current densities (above 160 mA/cm2), the ohmic penalty of Nafion 117 becomes the dominant loss mechanism, and Nafion 212 emerges as the preferred option despite its inferior CE. Power-sensitive applications such as grid frequency regulation, where short-duration high-power output is required, would benefit from the thinner membrane. The 3.8 percentage point EE advantage of Nafion 212 at 200 mA/cm2 translates into a proportional reduction in stack size and electrolyte volume for a given power rating, potentially offsetting the faster capacity fade through reduced upfront capital cost.

The temperature data add a further dimension to this analysis. While both membranes show improved VE at 40 C relative to 25 C, the thinner Nafion 212 membrane suffers a disproportionately larger CE penalty. For systems operating in warm climates or enclosed enclosures where passive cooling is insufficient, Nafion 117 offers superior thermal robustness. The activation energy disparity between proton conduction (8.5 kJ/mol) and vanadium diffusion (22 kJ/mol) implies that the crossover problem intensifies more rapidly than the ohmic problem as temperature increases. This thermodynamic asymmetry favors thicker membranes or modified membranes with additional selective barriers for high-temperature operation.

The 200-cycle capacity retention results corroborate the crossover-driven degradation mechanism. The 8.4 percentage point gap in capacity retention between Nafion 117 (86.7%) and Nafion 212 (78.3%) after 200 cycles underscores that short-term efficiency parity does not imply long-term durability parity. Electrolyte rebalancing systems, periodic remixing of positive and negative electrolytes, or the inclusion of catalytic rebalancing cells could mitigate this issue for Nafion 212, but each of these remedies adds system complexity and operating cost that must be weighed against the power-density benefit of the thinner membrane.

It is worth emphasizing that the term "Nafion" without qualification provides insufficient information for performance benchmarking. A study reporting "VRFB with Nafion membrane, CE = 95%, EE = 82% at 100 mA/cm2" could correspond to either Nafion 117 at 25 C or Nafion 212 at 40 C, among other combinations. The ambiguity impedes reproducibility and hinders fair comparison against alternative membranes. Future publications should specify the Nafion grade, pretreatment protocol, and cell operating conditions with the same rigor applied to reporting current density and electrolyte composition.

## Conclusion

This study has established an internally consistent performance baseline for vanadium redox flow battery single cells assembled with two widely used commercial Nafion membranes. At 100 mA/cm2 and 25 C, Nafion 117 delivered a CE of 96.2%, a VE of 84.1%, and an EE of 80.9%, while Nafion 212 achieved a CE of 92.5%, a VE of 88.3%, and an EE of 81.7%. The 75.7% reduction in area resistance offered by the thinner Nafion 212 membrane (0.45 ohm cm2 versus 1.85 ohm cm2) was counterbalanced by a 2.9-fold increase in vanadium permeability. Over 200 charge-discharge cycles, the Nafion 117 cell retained 86.7% of its initial capacity compared to 78.3% for Nafion 212, confirming that the reduced barrier thickness of the latter accelerates electrolyte imbalance.

The relative performance ranking between the two membranes depends on the operating current density. Nafion 117 is preferable below 60 mA/cm2 where crossover dominates, while Nafion 212 becomes advantageous above 160 mA/cm2 where ohmic losses govern. At the intermediate 80-120 mA/cm2 range, EE values converge and secondary considerations should guide selection. Temperature elevation from 25 C to 40 C improved VE for both membranes but degraded CE disproportionately for Nafion 212, revealing a thermal sensitivity that must be accounted for in warm-climate deployments.

These findings reinforce the necessity of specifying the exact Nafion grade in VRFB publications rather than relying on the generic membrane label. The dataset provided herein is intended to serve as a reference benchmark for subsequent studies proposing novel membrane materials, and to facilitate meaningful cross-study comparisons that are currently impeded by incomplete membrane identification.

# Polybenzimidazole-Derived and Non-Fluorinated Hydrocarbon Membranes for Vanadium Redox Flow Batteries: Structure–Property–Performance Relationships

## Abstract

The development of cost-effective, chemically stable proton exchange membranes represents a critical challenge for the widespread deployment of vanadium redox flow batteries (VRFBs). While perfluorinated sulfonic acid membranes such as Nafion exhibit excellent proton conductivity, their high vanadium permeability and substantial cost have motivated extensive research into non-fluorinated hydrocarbon alternatives. Among these, polybenzimidazole (PBI)-derived materials and their structural variants have attracted considerable attention due to their intrinsic dense chain packing and exceptional oxidative stability in concentrated vanadium electrolytes. This study systematically investigates the electrochemical performance of four representative membrane platforms—unmodified PBI, sulfonated naphthyl-substituted PBI with an ion exchange capacity of 1.42 mmol/g (SNPBI-1.42), a composite SPEEK/APK blend membrane, and a densely sulfonated triphenyl poly(arylene ether) (SPTPC-2.59) with an ion exchange capacity of 2.59 mmol/g—evaluated under identical VRFB operating conditions. Through comprehensive characterization of ion exchange capacity, area resistance, membrane swelling, vanadium crossover, and long-term charge–discharge cycling, we establish quantitative structure–property–performance relationships that elucidate the design trade-offs governing proton transport efficiency and vanadium blocking capability. Our findings demonstrate that increasing ion exchange capacity from 1.42 to 2.59 mmol/g reduces area resistance from 1.42 to 0.31 Ω·cm² but simultaneously increases the vanadium permeability from 1.2 × 10⁻⁷ to 8.5 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s. The SNPBI-1.42 membrane achieves the highest energy efficiency of 91.2% at 80 mA/cm², outperforming both the pristine PBI (82.4%) and the SPTPC-2.59 (87.6%) at the same current density. Over 500 continuous charge–discharge cycles at 80 mA/cm², the SNPBI-1.42 membrane exhibits a capacity fade rate of 0.012% per cycle, compared to 0.018% per cycle for the SPEEK/APK composite and 0.031% per cycle for the SPTPC-2.59 membrane. These results highlight that moderate sulfonation levels combined with rigid aromatic backbones offer an optimal compromise between proton conductivity and vanadium selectivity for non-fluorinated VRFB membranes.

## 1. Introduction

Vanadium redox flow batteries have emerged as one of the most promising electrochemical energy storage technologies for grid-scale applications due to their design flexibility, long cycle life, and decoupled energy–power configuration. The membrane separator, which simultaneously facilitates proton transport while preventing the mixing of positive and negative electrolytes, constitutes one of the most critical and cost-determining components in the VRFB stack. State-of-the-art VRFB systems predominantly employ perfluorinated sulfonic acid membranes, which offer high proton conductivity and excellent chemical durability. However, the inherently large ionic clusters within these perfluorinated materials result in substantial vanadium ion crossover, leading to capacity decay, self-discharge, and Coulombic efficiency penalties that compromise long-term operational stability.

Non-fluorinated hydrocarbon membranes represent an attractive alternative pathway toward reducing membrane costs and improving vanadium selectivity. Among the diverse classes of hydrocarbon-based materials, polybenzimidazole (PBI) and its chemically modified derivatives occupy a unique position due to their exceptional thermal stability, mechanical strength, and resistance to oxidative degradation by VO₂⁺ species in the charged positive electrolyte. The rigid aromatic backbone of PBI, characterized by interchain hydrogen bonding between imidazole groups, creates a densely packed morphological structure that inherently impedes the transport of large vanadium ions while permitting moderate proton migration through the Grotthuss mechanism.

The proton conductivity of pristine PBI, however, remains insufficient for high-power VRFB operation without additional acid doping or structural modification. Two principal strategies have been pursued to enhance the proton transport properties of PBI-based materials. The first approach involves sulfonation of the aromatic backbone to introduce pendant sulfonic acid groups that serve as additional proton-conducting sites, yielding sulfonated PBI derivatives with tunable ion exchange capacity values. The second approach focuses on blending PBI or its analogues with other sulfonated polymers to create composite membranes that combine the vanadium-blocking characteristics of PBI with the enhanced conductivity of highly sulfonated components.

Despite significant progress in the synthesis and characterization of PBI-derived membranes, a systematic comparative evaluation of structurally related but compositionally distinct membrane platforms under rigorously controlled VRFB operating conditions remains limited in scope. The complex interplay between ion exchange capacity, membrane swelling behavior, area resistance, vanadium crossover, and long-term cycling stability has not been fully elucidated for this important class of non-fluorinated materials. Furthermore, the question of whether maximum ion exchange capacity necessarily translates to optimal VRFB performance remains contested, with competing effects of enhanced proton transport and increased vanadium permeability creating a nuanced optimization landscape.

In this work, we report a comprehensive comparative study of four non-fluorinated hydrocarbon membranes—unmodified PBI, sulfonated naphthyl-substituted PBI (SNPBI-1.42), a SPEEK/APK composite blend, and a densely sulfonated triphenyl poly(arylene ether) (SPTPC-2.59)—for VRFB application. These membranes were selected to span a broad range of ion exchange capacity values (from 1.2 to 2.59 mmol/g) and structural architectures, enabling systematic investigation of the relationships between chemical composition, transport properties, and battery-level performance metrics. All membranes were characterized using a standardized experimental protocol, and their VRFB performance was evaluated across multiple current densities (40, 80, 120, 160, and 200 mA/cm²) to probe the influence of operating conditions on the efficiency–transport trade-offs. Our results provide quantitative design guidelines for the development of next-generation non-fluorinated membranes that balance proton conductivity and vanadium selectivity in practical VRFB systems.

### 2.2 Single Cell VRFB Performance at Varied Current Densities

The charge–discharge characteristics of the four membranes were evaluated in a single VRFB cell with an active area of 5 cm × 5 cm, employing 1.5 M V³⁺/V²⁺ in 3.0 M H₂SO₄ as the negative electrolyte and 1.5 M VO²⁺/VO₂⁺ in 3.0 M H₂SO₄ as the positive electrolyte. Each membrane was tested at current densities of 40, 80, 120, 160, and 200 mA/cm² to establish the dependence of Coulombic efficiency (CE), voltage efficiency (VE), and energy efficiency (EE) on the operating conditions.

At a current density of 80 mA/cm², the pristine PBI membrane achieved a Coulombic efficiency of 98.6%, a voltage efficiency of 83.6%, and an energy efficiency of 82.4%. The exceptionally high Coulombic efficiency reflects the superior vanadium-blocking properties of the unmodified PBI, while the relatively modest voltage efficiency is consistent with its high area resistance of 2.85 Ω·cm². The SNPBI-1.42 membrane demonstrated a Coulombic efficiency of 97.8%, a voltage efficiency of 93.3%, and an energy efficiency of 91.2% at the same 80 mA/cm² current density. The slight decrease in Coulombic efficiency relative to pristine PBI (by 0.8 percentage points) was accompanied by a substantial improvement in voltage efficiency (by 9.7 percentage points), resulting in a net gain of 8.8 percentage points in energy efficiency. The SPEEK/APK composite membrane achieved a Coulombic efficiency of 95.4%, a voltage efficiency of 94.8%, and an energy efficiency of 90.4% at 80 mA/cm². The SPTPC-2.59 membrane, despite possessing the lowest area resistance, exhibited the lowest Coulombic efficiency of 92.1% and a voltage efficiency of 95.1%, yielding an energy efficiency of 87.6% at 80 mA/cm².

The current density dependence of energy efficiency revealed distinct optimization behaviors for the four membranes. At 40 mA/cm², the energy efficiency values were 85.1%, 93.6%, 93.2%, and 91.4% for PBI, SNPBI-1.42, SPEEK/APK, and SPTPC-2.59, respectively. As the current density increased to 120 mA/cm², the energy efficiency values decreased to 80.3%, 89.4%, 87.1%, and 84.2%, respectively. At 160 mA/cm², the corresponding values further declined to 76.8%, 86.7%, 82.9%, and 80.1%, respectively. At the highest tested current density of 200 mA/cm², the energy efficiency values reached 72.5%, 83.2%, 78.6%, and 75.8% for PBI, SNPBI-1.42, SPEEK/APK, and SPTPC-2.59, respectively. The SNPBI-1.42 membrane consistently maintained the highest energy efficiency across all tested current densities, with the performance advantage becoming more pronounced at intermediate to high current densities (80–160 mA/cm²).

The rate of efficiency decline with increasing current density provides insight into the relative contributions of ohmic and kinetic losses in each membrane system. The pristine PBI exhibited the steepest decline in energy efficiency, decreasing by 12.6 percentage points from 40 to 200 mA/cm², primarily due to the dominant ohmic losses associated with its high area resistance. The SPTPC-2.59 membrane showed a decline of 15.6 percentage points over the same current density range, with the efficiency drop driven primarily by enhanced vanadium crossover and associated Coulombic efficiency penalties at longer charge–discharge durations. The SNPBI-1.42 membrane displayed the most gradual decline, with energy efficiency decreasing by only 10.4 percentage points from 40 to 200 mA/cm², indicating a well-balanced combination of low resistance and moderate vanadium permeability.

### 2.3 Long-Term Cycling Stability and Capacity Retention

Long-term charge–discharge cycling tests were conducted at a constant current density of 80 mA/cm² over 500 continuous cycles to assess the operational durability and capacity retention characteristics of each membrane. The pristine PBI membrane exhibited an initial discharge capacity of 1250 mAh, which gradually declined to 1075 mAh over 500 cycles, corresponding to a capacity retention of 86.0% and a fade rate of 0.028% per cycle. The SNPBI-1.42 membrane demonstrated superior capacity retention, with the initial discharge capacity of 1320 mAh decreasing to 1221 mAh after 500 cycles, yielding a capacity retention of 92.5% and the lowest fade rate among all tested membranes at 0.012% per cycle. The SPEEK/APK composite membrane showed an initial discharge capacity of 1285 mAh declining to 1172 mAh after 500 cycles, corresponding to a capacity retention of 91.2% and a fade rate of 0.018% per cycle. The SPTPC-2.59 membrane exhibited the poorest long-term capacity retention, with the initial discharge capacity of 1340 mAh falling to 1132 mAh over 500 cycles, representing a capacity retention of only 84.5% and the highest fade rate of 0.031% per cycle.

The capacity fade mechanisms operating in the four membrane systems differ substantially and correlate with the distinct transport property profiles. In the SPTPC-2.59 membrane, the rapid capacity fade of 0.031% per cycle is attributed primarily to the substantial vanadium crossover (8.5 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s), which leads to progressive electrolyte imbalance, accumulation of vanadium species in the opposing half-cell, and accelerated self-discharge reactions that compromise the long-term capacity. The elevated swelling ratio of 18.7% in this membrane may also contribute to gradual mechanical degradation and the formation of microscopic defects that further exacerbate vanadium permeation over extended cycling. In the pristine PBI membrane, the capacity fade rate of 0.028% per cycle, despite the lowest vanadium permeability, is associated with the progressive oxidative degradation of the imidazole rings by VO₂⁺ species, which gradually reduces the proton conductivity and increases the polarization losses over hundreds of cycles. The SNPBI-1.42 membrane combines the oxidative stability benefits of the PBI backbone with the enhanced proton transport provided by the sulfonic acid groups, resulting in the most stable capacity retention profile. The moderate vanadium permeability (1.2 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s) and constrained swelling ratio (5.8%) work synergistically to minimize both crossover-induced and degradation-driven capacity losses.

Post-cycling characterization of the membranes after 500 cycles revealed additional insights into the degradation mechanisms. The SNPBI-1.42 membrane retained 96.3% of its initial ion exchange capacity after cycling, compared to 89.7% for the pristine PBI, 93.1% for the SPEEK/APK composite, and 84.2% for the SPTPC-2.59 membrane. The superior IEC retention of the SNPBI-1.42 membrane confirms that the sulfonic acid groups are chemically stable under the VRFB operating conditions and that the moderate sulfonation level does not compromise the overall oxidative resistance of the aromatic backbone. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy of the cycled membranes indicated the appearance of new carbonyl absorption bands at 1720 cm⁻¹ in the pristine PBI and SPTPC-2.59 samples, consistent with oxidative cleavage of the aromatic ether linkages, while the SNPBI-1.42 membrane showed minimal changes in its infrared signature after 500 cycles.

## 3. Discussion

### 3.1 The IEC–Transport–Performance Optimization Landscape

The comprehensive dataset generated in this study enables a quantitative analysis of the optimization landscape governing non-fluorinated hydrocarbon membranes for VRFB application. The results reveal that ion exchange capacity serves as a useful but insufficient predictor of membrane performance, and that the relationships between IEC, transport properties, and battery-level metrics are substantially nonlinear with important trade-offs that must be carefully balanced.

The data demonstrate a clear inverse relationship between ion exchange capacity and area resistance: increasing IEC from 1.20 to 2.59 mmol/g reduces area resistance by a factor of 9.2, from 2.85 to 0.31 Ω·cm². This relationship reflects the well-established role of sulfonic acid groups as proton-conducting sites that facilitate Grothuss and vehicular proton transport mechanisms. However, the same increase in ion exchange capacity produces a more than tenfold increase in vanadium permeability, from 0.8 × 10⁻⁷ to 8.5 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s, indicating that the enhanced ionic pathways created by sulfonation are non-selective and permit the transport of both protons and vanadium ions. The approximately linear scaling of vanadium permeability with IEC across the membrane series suggests that the dominant transport mechanism for vanadium ions shifts from restricted diffusion through the dense polymer matrix at low IEC values to facilitated transport through the hydrated ionic channels at high IEC values.

The energy efficiency data at 80 mA/cm² reveal that the maximum value is not achieved by the membrane with either the lowest or the highest ion exchange capacity, but rather by the SNPBI-1.42 membrane with an intermediate IEC of 1.42 mmol/g. This observation supports the existence of an optimal IEC range for VRFB membranes, where the benefits of reduced ohmic losses outweigh the penalties associated with vanadium crossover. The pristine PBI suffers from excessive voltage losses due to its high resistance (2.85 Ω·cm²), while the SPTPC-2.59 membrane is penalized by Coulombic efficiency losses arising from its high vanadium permeability (8.5 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s). The SNPBI-1.42 membrane occupies a favorable position in this trade-off space, combining a moderate area resistance of 1.42 Ω·cm² with a low vanadium permeability of 1.2 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s.

The relationship between membrane swelling and long-term cycling stability provides an additional dimension for membrane design optimization. The SPTPC-2.59 membrane, with its swelling ratio of 18.7%, exhibited the highest capacity fade rate of 0.031% per cycle, suggesting that excessive dimensional changes during hydration–dehydration cycles contribute to mechanical fatigue and the formation of transport-facilitating defects. In contrast, the SNPBI-1.42 membrane, with a modest swelling ratio of 5.8%, achieved the lowest fade rate of 0.012% per cycle, indicating that maintaining structural dimensional stability is at least as important as optimizing ionic transport for achieving long VRFB operational lifetimes.

### 3.2 Comparative Assessment of Membrane Architectures

The four membrane platforms investigated in this study represent three distinct architectural approaches to non-fluorinated VRFB membrane design: rigid backbone modification (PBI → SNPBI-1.42), polymer blending (SPEEK/APK), and high-degree sulfonation of flexible backbones (SPTPC-2.59). The comparative performance data provide guidance on the relative merits of each approach.

The rigid backbone modification strategy exemplified by the SNPBI-1.42 membrane represents the most successful approach among those tested. By introducing sulfonic acid groups on naphthyl substituents attached to the rigid PBI backbone, this membrane achieves enhanced proton conductivity without substantially compromising the chain packing density or the vanadium-blocking characteristics of the parent PBI. The naphthyl substituents appear to act as spatially separated proton-conducting pendants that create localized hydrophilic domains within the overall hydrophobic aromatic matrix, generating a phase-separated morphology that supports efficient proton transport while maintaining size-selective exclusion of vanadium ions. The energy efficiency of 91.2% at 80 mA/cm² and the capacity fade rate of 0.012% per cycle demonstrate that this architectural approach effectively addresses the primary limitations of both pristine PBI (high resistance) and highly sulfonated materials (high vanadium crossover).

The SPEEK/APK composite membrane, while achieving competitive energy efficiency of 90.4% at 80 mA/cm², showed a higher capacity fade rate of 0.018% per cycle compared to the SNPBI-1.42 membrane. The blend architecture introduces interfacial regions between the SPEEK and APK components that may serve as preferential pathways for vanadium transport or sites for chemical degradation. The intermediate swelling ratio of 12.4% and vanadium permeability of 3.6 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s suggest that the blend morphology does not achieve the same level of ion selectivity as the rigid backbone modification approach. However, the SPEEK/APK membrane remains a viable candidate for applications where manufacturing simplicity and material cost are prioritized over maximum cycling stability.

The SPTPC-2.59 membrane, despite possessing the highest proton conductivity and the lowest area resistance, underperformed in long-term cycling tests due to its excessive vanadium permeability. This result highlights an important design principle for VRFB membranes: proton conductivity should not be maximized independently of selectivity. The densely sulfonated flexible backbone creates extensive interconnected ionic channels that effectively transport protons but simultaneously permit significant vanadium crossover. The low Coulombic efficiency of 92.1% at 80 mA/cm², while still acceptable for some applications, translates into accelerated capacity fade that limits the practical operational lifetime of VRFB cells employing this membrane.

### 3.3 Implications for Non-Fluorinated Membrane Design

The findings of this study carry several implications for the rational design of next-generation non-fluorinated hydrocarbon membranes for VRFB applications. First, the optimal ion exchange capacity range for non-fluorinated membranes appears to fall between 1.3 and 1.6 mmol/g, where the balance between proton transport efficiency and vanadium selectivity is most favorable. Membranes with IEC values below this range suffer from excessive ohmic losses that reduce voltage efficiency, while membranes with IEC values above this range experience unacceptable vanadium crossover that degrades Coulombic efficiency and long-term capacity retention.

Second, the backbone rigidity and chain packing density emerge as critical structural parameters that are at least as important as the ion exchange capacity in determining membrane performance. The comparison between SNPBI-1.42 and SPTPC-2.59 membranes, which possess similar proton transport properties but markedly different vanadium permeabilities, demonstrates that maintaining a rigid, densely packed polymer structure is essential for achieving high ion selectivity. Flexible backbones with high degrees of sulfonation may achieve low area resistance but fail to provide adequate vanadium blocking, regardless of the specific chemical structure.

Third, the swelling ratio serves as a practical indicator of long-term mechanical stability and should be maintained below approximately 8% for membranes intended for extended VRFB operation. The correlation between high swelling ratios and accelerated capacity fade observed in the SPTPC-2.59 membrane suggests that dimensional stability during wet–dry cycling is a prerequisite for maintaining the morphological integrity required for selective ion transport over thousands of operational hours.

Finally, the superior oxidative stability of the SNPBI-1.42 membrane, as evidenced by its 96.3% IEC retention after 500 cycles, demonstrates that moderate sulfonation of PBI-type backbones does not compromise the inherent chemical resistance of the parent material. This finding is significant because it validates the approach of structural modification as a viable pathway for enhancing the performance of PBI-based membranes without sacrificing their primary advantage of exceptional durability in the aggressive vanadium electrolyte environment.

## 4. Conclusion

This study presents a systematic comparative evaluation of four non-fluorinated hydrocarbon membranes—PBI, SNPBI-1.42, SPEEK/APK, and SPTPC-2.59—for vanadium redox flow battery application. Through detailed characterization of physicochemical properties and comprehensive electrochemical testing across multiple current densities, we establish quantitative structure–property–performance relationships that inform the rational design of next-generation membrane materials.

The key findings are as follows: (1) The SNPBI-1.42 membrane, with an ion exchange capacity of 1.42 mmol/g, achieves the highest energy efficiency of 91.2% at 80 mA/cm² and the lowest capacity fade rate of 0.012% per cycle over 500 continuous cycles, outperforming all other tested membranes across the full range of operating conditions. (2) Increasing ion exchange capacity from 1.42 to 2.59 mmol/g reduces area resistance from 1.42 to 0.31 Ω·cm² but simultaneously increases vanadium permeability from 1.2 × 10⁻⁷ to 8.5 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s, demonstrating a fundamental conductivity–selectivity trade-off. (3) The pristine PBI membrane, despite exceptional vanadium-blocking capability (0.8 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s) and high Coulombic efficiency (98.6%), is limited by its high area resistance (2.85 Ω·cm²) that results in a relatively low energy efficiency of 82.4% at 80 mA/cm². (4) Long-term cycling stability correlates more strongly with membrane swelling behavior than with any single transport parameter, with the SNPBI-1.42 membrane (swelling ratio 5.8%) achieving superior durability compared to the highly swollen SPTPC-2.59 membrane (swelling ratio 18.7%).

These results demonstrate that moderate sulfonation levels combined with rigid aromatic backbones offer an optimal compromise between proton conductivity and vanadium selectivity for non-fluorinated VRFB membranes. The SNPBI-1.42 membrane represents a promising candidate for practical VRFB systems, combining high energy efficiency, excellent cycling stability, and the cost advantages of non-fluorinated hydrocarbon materials. Future work should focus on scale-up manufacturing of sulfonated PBI derivatives and extended field testing under realistic grid-storage operational profiles to validate the long-term performance advantages identified in this laboratory study.

# Surface-Modified Carbon Felt Electrodes with Tunable Wettability for Enhanced Vanadium Redox Flow Battery Performance

## Abstract

The electrochemical performance of carbon felt electrodes in vanadium redox flow batteries (VRFBs) is critically governed by surface functionalization strategies that modulate wettability, active surface area, and charge-transfer kinetics. In this study, we systematically investigate three electrode architectures: pristine carbon-carbon composite (C-C), modified carbon-carbon composite (MC-C) treated via acid oxidation and thermal activation, and boron-doped modified carbon-carbon composite (BMC-C) prepared through a two-step functionalization involving oxygen plasma etching followed by boronation at 850°C. Through cyclic voltammetry (CV), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and single-cell charge-discharge testing across current densities of 50, 100, 200, and 500 mA cm⁻², we demonstrate that the BMC-C electrode achieves a contact angle of 92.8°, compared to 130.4° for C-C and 102° for MC-C, reflecting substantially enhanced hydrophilicity. The corresponding charge-transfer resistance decreases from 3.82 Ω cm² (C-C) to 2.17 Ω cm² (MC-C) and further to 1.34 Ω cm² (BMC-C) for the V(IV)/V(V) redox couple in 2.0 M VOSO₄ + 3.0 M H₂SO₄ electrolyte. The electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) increases by 2.8-fold from C-C to BMC-C, as estimated from the Randles-Sevcik analysis of peak current densities. Single-cell testing reveals that the BMC-C-based VRFB delivers an energy efficiency (EE) of 82.4% at 100 mA cm⁻², surpassing 74.1% for MC-C and 66.3% for C-C, while maintaining a Coulombic efficiency (CE) above 96% across all tested current densities. The voltage efficiency (VE) exhibits a pronounced dependence on current density, declining from 89.2% at 50 mA cm⁻² to 71.5% at 200 mA cm⁻² for the BMC-C cell. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) analysis confirm that surface roughness increases from an arithmetic mean roughness (Ra) of 8.4 μm for C-C to 14.7 μm for BMC-C, contributing to enhanced electrolyte-electrode contact and reduced mass transport limitations. These findings elucidate the mechanistic interplay between surface chemistry, wettability, and electrochemical kinetics in carbon felt electrodes for VRFB applications.

## 1. Introduction

Vanadium redox flow batteries (VRFBs) have emerged as one of the most promising electrochemical energy storage technologies for grid-scale applications due to their decoupled power and energy capacity, long cycle life, and inherent safety advantages. The positive half-cell reaction involving the V(IV)/V(V) couple (VO²⁺/VO₂⁺) is widely recognized as the rate-limiting step in conventional VRFB systems, primarily due to the sluggish charge-transfer kinetics and the significant activation polarization at the graphite-based electrode surface. Carbon felt electrodes, typically manufactured from polyacrylonitrile (PAN) precursor fibers, constitute the most widely adopted electrode material in VRFB stacks owing to their high electrical conductivity, three-dimensional porous architecture, chemical stability in strongly acidic electrolytes, and favorable cost structure. However, pristine carbon felts suffer from several critical limitations that impede their practical performance: inherently hydrophobic surfaces with contact angles exceeding 130°, relatively low electrochemically active surface area due to the absence of functional groups, and significant charge-transfer resistance at the electrode-electrolyte interface.

To address these challenges, a diverse array of surface modification strategies has been investigated, including thermal activation in air or carbon dioxide atmospheres, acid treatment with concentrated HNO₃ or H₂SO₄, electrochemical oxidation, plasma treatment, and heteroatom doping with nitrogen, boron, or sulfur. Among these approaches, boron doping has attracted considerable attention because the introduction of electron-deficient boron sites adjacent to carbon atoms can polarize the surrounding electronic structure, thereby lowering the adsorption energy of vanadium intermediates and promoting the catalytic turnover of the V(IV)/V(V) reaction. Furthermore, combined treatment strategies that first introduce oxygen-containing functional groups to increase wettability and subsequently incorporate heteroatoms to enhance intrinsic electrocatalytic activity have demonstrated synergistic effects that exceed the performance gains achievable by either modification alone.

In this work, we report a systematic investigation of three carbon felt electrode variants—C-C (pristine), MC-C (acid-oxidized and thermally activated), and BMC-C (boron-doped modified)—to elucidate how sequential surface engineering influences wettability, roughness, charge-transfer characteristics, and full-cell performance. We employ water contact angle goniometry, SEM, BET analysis, CV, EIS, and galvanostatic charge-discharge cycling at 50–500 mA cm⁻², providing a mechanistic framework linking electrochemical enhancements to quantifiable surface property changes.

## 2. Materials and Electrode Preparation

The starting material for all electrode variants was commercial soft carbon felt with a nominal thickness of 5.0 mm, bulk density of 0.09 g cm⁻³, and average fiber diameter of 12 μm. The pristine material (C-C) was used as-received without further modification, following ultrasonic cleaning in deionized water and ethanol to remove surface contaminants.

The MC-C electrode was prepared through a two-step activation procedure. First, rectangular specimens (50 mm × 50 mm × 5 mm) were immersed in a 6.0 M HNO₃ solution and refluxed at 80°C for 12 hours to introduce oxygen-containing functional groups including carboxyl, hydroxyl, and carbonyl moieties. Following acid treatment, the samples were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water until the filtrate reached neutral pH, then thermally activated at 450°C in a flowing air atmosphere for 3 hours to enhance surface porosity and increase the density of acidic functional groups.

The BMC-C electrode was fabricated by subjecting MC-C substrates to boronation at 850°C under argon flow (200 mL min⁻¹). Boric acid (H₃BO₃) vapor, generated by upstream sublimation, reacted with the activated carbon surface for 2 hours. This temperature promotes covalent B-C bond formation while avoiding excessive fiber degradation. The furnace was cooled under continuous argon flow, and BMC-C electrodes were stored in a desiccator prior to use.

The activated carbon felt reference sample used for comparative benchmarking was prepared by thermal activation of pristine carbon felt at 700°C in a CO₂ atmosphere for 4 hours, a condition known to produce high surface area activated carbon structures with enhanced porosity.

## 3. Physicochemical Characterization

### 3.1 Surface Wettability and Morphology

Water contact angle measurements reveal the wettability of each electrode, a property that critically influences electrolyte penetration and ion accessibility. The pristine C-C electrode exhibited a contact angle of 130.4°, characteristic of hydrophobic graphitic surfaces without polar functional groups. The MC-C electrode showed a substantially reduced contact angle of 102°, indicating successful introduction of oxygen-containing groups. The BMC-C electrode achieved 92.8°, representing a transition from hydrophobic to near-hydrophilic behavior attributed to boron's Lewis acid-base interactions with water molecules, complementing the hydrogen bonding of oxygen functional groups.

Scanning electron microscopy images revealed notable differences in surface morphology across the three electrode variants. The pristine C-C fibers presented relatively smooth surfaces with an arithmetic mean roughness (Ra) of 8.4 μm, exhibiting occasional surface striations but minimal topographical complexity. In contrast, the MC-C fibers showed a visibly roughened surface texture with Ra increasing to 11.2 μm, reflecting the etching action of acid oxidation and thermal activation that creates microscale surface defects and opens closed micropores. The BMC-C fibers displayed the highest degree of surface roughness with Ra reaching 14.7 μm, as the high-temperature boronation process introduces additional nanoscale texturing while preserving the beneficial features of the MC-C substrate. This progressive increase in surface roughness from C-C through MC-C to BMC-C correlates directly with the measured increases in electrochemically active surface area.

BET nitrogen adsorption-desorption measurements yielded specific surface area values of 1.42 m² g⁻¹ for C-C, 3.15 m² g⁻¹ for MC-C, and 4.08 m² g⁻¹ for BMC-C. While these absolute values are modest compared to highly activated carbon materials, the BET surface area increases by 2.87-fold from C-C to BMC-C, paralleling the roughness trend and indicating that the functionalization protocols effectively generate accessible surface area within the fiber matrix without compromising the macroporous architecture required for convective electrolyte flow.

### 3.2 Electrochemical Characterization

Cyclic voltammograms were recorded for the V(IV)/V(V) redox couple in a three-electrode configuration using 2.0 M VOSO₄ + 3.0 M H₂SO₄ as the supporting electrolyte, with Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) reference electrode and platinum wire counter electrode. The working electrode consisted of a 10 mm × 10 mm specimen mounted on a glassy carbon electrode holder. CV scans were performed at potential sweep rates ranging from 5 to 100 mV s⁻¹ within the potential window of 0.0 V to +1.2 V vs. Ag/AgCl.

For the C-C electrode, the anodic peak current density (jpa) at 50 mV s⁻¹ was 12.4 mA cm⁻², while the cathodic peak current density (jpc) was 8.7 mA cm⁻², yielding a peak current ratio (jpa/jpc) of 1.43 and a peak separation (ΔEp) of 268 mV. The MC-C electrode exhibited enhanced peak currents of jpa = 21.6 mA cm⁻² and jpc = 16.3 mA cm⁻² at the same sweep rate, with a reduced ΔEp of 204 mV and an improved peak current ratio of 1.32. The BMC-C electrode demonstrated the highest electrochemical activity, achieving jpa = 31.8 mA cm⁻² and jpc = 26.5 mA cm⁻², with a ΔEp of 158 mV and jpa/jpc = 1.20. The systematic decrease in peak separation from 268 mV (C-C) to 158 mV (BMC-C) indicates progressively faster charge-transfer kinetics, while the approach of the peak current ratio toward unity suggests improved electrochemical reversibility.

The Randles-Sevcik analysis of peak current density versus square root of sweep rate yielded linear correlations (R² > 0.995) for all three electrodes, confirming diffusion-controlled electrochemical behavior. The slopes of these linear fits were used to estimate the effective electrochemical surface area (ECSA), which increased from 4.6 cm² for C-C to 8.9 cm² for MC-C and further to 12.9 cm² for BMC-C, representing a 2.80-fold enhancement. This ECSA progression closely mirrors the BET surface area and roughness trends, validating the consistency of the morphological and electrochemical analyses.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy was conducted under the same electrolyte conditions at the formal potential of the V(IV)/V(V) couple (approximately +0.72 V vs. Ag/AgCl). The Nyquist plots displayed a characteristic semicircular feature in the high-to-medium frequency region associated with the charge-transfer process, followed by a low-frequency Warburg tail indicative of mass transport limitations. The equivalent circuit fitting employed a Randles-type model consisting of solution resistance (Rs) in series with a parallel combination of charge-transfer resistance (Rct) and constant phase element (CPE), followed by a Warburg impedance element (W).

The charge-transfer resistance values extracted from the EIS data revealed dramatic differences between the three electrode variants. The C-C electrode exhibited the highest Rct of 3.82 Ω cm², consistent with the inherently slow electron transfer kinetics at the unmodified carbon surface. The MC-C electrode achieved a substantially lower Rct of 2.17 Ω cm², reflecting the catalytic contribution of oxygen functional groups—particularly quinone and hydroxyl moieties—that serve as redox mediators facilitating electron shuttling between the carbon surface and the vanadium species. The BMC-C electrode recorded the lowest Rct of 1.34 Ω cm², representing a 64.9% reduction relative to C-C and a 38.2% improvement over MC-C. This superior charge-transfer characteristic is attributed to the dual catalytic role of boron dopants, which create electron-deficient sites that stabilize the transition state of the V(IV)/V(V) electron transfer reaction while simultaneously increasing the density of states near the Fermi level.

## 4. Single-Cell Battery Performance

Galvanostatic charge-discharge cycling tests were performed in a laboratory-scale VRFB single cell with an active electrode area of 25 cm², separated by a Nafion 117 membrane. The positive electrolyte comprised 1.6 M VOSO₄ in 4.5 M total sulfate, while the negative electrolyte contained 1.6 M V₃⁺ in equivalent sulfate support. Both electrolyte reservoirs held 60 mL of solution, and the electrolyte flow rate was maintained at 60 mL min⁻¹ using peristaltic pumps. The state of charge (SOC) window was set to 20-80% for all cycling tests, with voltage cutoff limits of 1.65 V (charge) and 0.8 V (discharge). All cell tests were conducted at ambient temperature (25 ± 2°C).

Charge-discharge curves were recorded at current densities of 50, 100, 200, and 500 mA cm⁻² for each electrode variant. A key diagnostic parameter derived from these measurements is the polarization voltage delta (ΔVpol), defined as the difference between the average charge voltage and average discharge voltage at a given current density. For the C-C electrode, ΔVpol increased steeply from 0.18 V at 50 mA cm⁻² to 0.52 V at 200 mA cm⁻² and reached 0.91 V at 500 mA cm⁻², indicating severe kinetic and ohmic limitations at elevated current densities. The MC-C electrode demonstrated improved polarization characteristics, with ΔVpol values of 0.14 V, 0.28 V, 0.41 V, and 0.72 V at 50, 100, 200, and 500 mA cm⁻², respectively. The BMC-C electrode exhibited the smallest polarization voltage delta across all current densities, recording 0.11 V at 50 mA cm⁻², 0.22 V at 100 mA cm⁻², 0.33 V at 200 mA cm⁻², and 0.58 V at 500 mA cm⁻².

The Coulombic efficiency (CE) remained high for all electrode variants, exceeding 96% across the entire current density range from 50 to 500 mA cm⁻². Specifically, at 100 mA cm⁻², the CE values were 96.8% for C-C, 97.2% for MC-C, and 97.5% for BMC-C. The slight increases in CE with surface modification are attributed to enhanced electrolyte wetting and reduced parasitic side reactions at the electrode surface, which minimize self-discharge and vanadium ion crossover through the membrane. At higher current densities, CE increased marginally for all variants due to the shorter residence time during charge and discharge phases, which reduces the opportunity for crossover-related capacity loss.

The voltage efficiency (VE) exhibited strong dependence on both electrode type and current density. For the C-C electrode, VE declined from 77.5% at 50 mA cm⁻² to 65.1% at 100 mA cm⁻², 54.8% at 200 mA cm⁻², and 41.2% at 500 mA cm⁻². The MC-C electrode showed substantial improvements with VE values of 84.3% at 50 mA cm⁻², 76.3% at 100 mA cm⁻², 66.7% at 200 mA cm⁻², and 50.4% at 500 mA cm⁻². The BMC-C electrode achieved the highest voltage efficiency across all conditions, with VE = 89.2% at 50 mA cm⁻², 84.5% at 100 mA cm⁻², 71.5% at 200 mA cm⁻², and 54.1% at 500 mA cm⁻². The 18.3 percentage-point difference in VE between BMC-C and C-C at 200 mA cm⁻² is particularly significant, as it demonstrates that surface engineering can substantially extend the practical operating window of VRFB systems to higher power densities.

The energy efficiency (EE), calculated as the product of CE and VE, followed trends consistent with the individual efficiency components. At 100 mA cm⁻², the C-C electrode delivered an EE of 66.3%, the MC-C electrode achieved 74.1%, and the BMC-C electrode reached 82.4%. At 200 mA cm⁻², these values decreased to 53.1% for C-C, 64.8% for MC-C, and 69.7% for BMC-C. The ability of the BMC-C electrode to maintain energy efficiency above 80% at 100 mA cm⁻² while the C-C electrode falls below 67% represents a critical performance advantage for practical grid-scale applications where capital cost minimization requires operation at elevated current densities to reduce stack size.

Long-term cycling stability was evaluated over 200 consecutive charge-discharge cycles at 100 mA cm⁻². The BMC-C electrode demonstrated excellent capacity retention with a fade rate of 0.03% per cycle, compared to 0.05% per cycle for MC-C and 0.08% per cycle for C-C. The enhanced durability of the BMC-C electrode is attributed to the high-temperature boronation process that creates stable B-C bonds resistant to electrochemical degradation in the aggressive acidic environment of the VRFB electrolyte, in contrast to the oxygen functional groups on MC-C which are more susceptible to reduction or dissolution during prolonged cycling.

## 5. Discussion

The results presented in this study establish a clear correlation between the surface modification protocol, the resulting physicochemical properties, and the electrochemical performance of carbon felt electrodes in VRFB applications. The systematic progression from C-C to MC-C to BMC-C demonstrates that each successive functionalization step introduces beneficial changes that are reflected across multiple length scales and measurement modalities.

At the molecular level, the water contact angle data provide unambiguous evidence of surface chemistry evolution. The reduction from 130.4° to 102° upon acid/thermal treatment (C-C to MC-C) is consistent with the introduction of oxygen-containing functional groups that increase the surface free energy and promote hydrophilicity. The further reduction to 92.8° for BMC-C indicates that boron incorporation contributes additional polar character to the surface, facilitating electrolyte wetting. The contact angle alone, however, does not fully explain the performance differences observed. While wettability enhancement ensures better electrolyte penetration into the porous electrode structure, the 64.9% reduction in charge-transfer resistance from C-C (3.82 Ω cm²) to BMC-C (1.34 Ω cm²) reflects intrinsic catalytic effects that go beyond purely morphological considerations.

The mechanistic role of boron dopants in enhancing V(IV)/V(V) kinetics can be understood through electronic structure effects. Boron atoms incorporated into the carbon lattice create localized electron-deficient sites due to boron's three valence electrons versus carbon's four. These sites polarize the surrounding carbon framework, increasing the density of states at the Fermi level and modifying the adsorption energy of vanadium intermediates. For the V(IV)/V(V) reaction, which proceeds through an inner-sphere electron transfer mechanism involving adsorbed VO₂⁺ and VO₂⁺ species, optimal binding energy at the electrode surface is critical. The boron-doped carbon surface appears to provide a more favorable adsorption configuration that stabilizes the transition state without overly strong binding that would impede product desorption, consistent with the observed reduction in peak separation from 268 mV to 158 mV.

The interplay between surface roughness and electrochemically active surface area is another critical factor. Ra increases from 8.4 μm (C-C) to 14.7 μm (BMC-C), a 75% enhancement that corresponds to the 2.80-fold ECSA increase and 2.87-fold BET increase. This multi-scale roughening creates additional active sites while maintaining the open pore structure for convective electrolyte delivery. The steeper ECSA increase relative to Ra indicates that chemical functionalization contributes to effective surface area through nanoscale porosity and catalytic site creation.

The single-cell efficiency data provide the ultimate validation of the surface engineering strategy under realistic operating conditions. The energy efficiency of 82.4% achieved by BMC-C at 100 mA cm⁻² represents a substantial improvement over the 66.3% obtained with C-C, with the majority of this gain arising from enhanced voltage efficiency (84.5% vs. 65.1%) rather than Coulombic efficiency improvements. This pattern indicates that the surface modifications primarily reduce activation and ohmic overpotentials at the positive electrode rather than mitigating membrane crossover or side reactions. The polarization voltage delta data further support this interpretation: the difference in ΔVpol between BMC-C and C-C increases from 0.07 V at 50 mA cm⁻² to 0.33 V at 500 mA cm⁻², demonstrating that the benefits of surface functionalization become increasingly pronounced as current density rises.

A comparison with the activated carbon felt reference prepared by CO₂ activation at 700°C provides additional context. The activated carbon felt exhibited a contact angle of 98.5°, a BET surface area of 6.42 m² g⁻¹, and Rct of 2.68 Ω cm². While its surface area exceeded that of BMC-C, its charge-transfer resistance was higher and its energy efficiency at 100 mA cm⁻² was only 78.6%. This finding highlights an important design consideration: maximizing surface area through aggressive activation does not necessarily translate to optimal electrochemical performance if the surface lacks catalytically active functional groups. The BMC-C electrode, despite its lower absolute surface area compared to the activated carbon felt, achieves superior kinetics because the boronation process creates specific active sites tailored for the V(IV)/V(V) reaction rather than merely increasing physical surface area.

Long-term cycling data reveal that BMC-C maintains its advantage over 200 cycles with the lowest fade rate (0.03% per cycle). This durability is critical for VRFB deployment, where electrode replacement is a major operational cost. The stability of B-C bonds formed at 850°C contrasts favorably with the less robust oxygen functional groups on MC-C, which undergo reduction or dissolution during cycling. This durability advantage, combined with higher initial efficiency, positions BMC-C functionalization as a compelling pathway for commercial VRFB electrodes.

## 6. Conclusion

This study has demonstrated that sequential surface functionalization of carbon felt electrodes—combining acid oxidation, thermal activation, and high-temperature boronation—produces a boron-doped modified carbon-carbon composite (BMC-C) electrode with substantially enhanced performance for vanadium redox flow battery applications. The comprehensive characterization across three electrode variants (C-C, MC-C, and BMC-C) enables the establishment of quantitative structure-property-performance relationships.

Key findings include: (1) the BMC-C electrode achieves a water contact angle of 92.8°, compared to 130.4° for pristine C-C, reflecting a transition from hydrophobic to near-hydrophilic surface character; (2) the charge-transfer resistance for the V(IV)/V(V) couple decreases progressively from 3.82 Ω cm² (C-C) to 2.17 Ω cm² (MC-C) to 1.34 Ω cm² (BMC-C), demonstrating synergistic kinetic enhancement from combined oxygen functionalization and boron doping; (3) the electrochemically active surface area increases by 2.80-fold from C-C to BMC-C, paralleling the 75% increase in surface roughness (Ra from 8.4 μm to 14.7 μm); and (4) in single-cell testing at 100 mA cm⁻², the BMC-C electrode delivers an energy efficiency of 82.4%, surpassing MC-C (74.1%) and C-C (66.3%) while maintaining Coulombic efficiency above 96% and exhibiting a low capacity fade rate of 0.03% per cycle over 200 cycles.

The mechanistic analysis reveals that the performance gains arise from a combination of improved electrolyte wettability, increased active surface area, and intrinsic catalytic enhancement of the V(IV)/V(V) electron transfer reaction at boron-modified carbon sites. The BMC-C electrode maintains energy efficiency above 69% even at 200 mA cm⁻², where the C-C electrode drops below 54%, demonstrating the practical significance of the surface engineering approach for high-power VRFB operation. These results support the continued development of heteroatom-doped carbon felt electrodes as a pathway to improved cost-effectiveness and performance in grid-scale vanadium redox flow battery systems.

# Influence of Electrolyte Concentration and Flow Rate on Performance Metrics in Vanadium Redox Flow Batteries

## Abstract

The energy efficiency and practical energy density of vanadium redox flow batteries (VRFBs) are governed by a complex interplay between electrolyte composition, hydrodynamic conditions, and electrochemical kinetics. This study systematically investigates how vanadium concentration, sulfuric acid concentration, and electrolyte flow rate affect coulombic efficiency (CE), voltage efficiency (VE), energy efficiency (EE), concentration polarization, and net system-level energy density. Single-cell tests were conducted at current densities of 50, 100, and 200 mA/cm² using electrolytes containing 1.2–2.0 mol/L total vanadium dissolved in 2.0–5.0 mol/L H₂SO₄ support solution, with flow rates ranging from 10 to 100 mL/min. Increasing vanadium concentration from 1.2 to 1.6 mol/L raised the theoretical volumetric energy density from 19.3 to 25.7 Wh/L and improved practical EE at 100 mA/cm² from 78.2% to 83.5%, though further increases to 2.0 mol/L were accompanied by elevated viscosity and diminished mass-transfer coefficients. Sulfuric acid concentration exhibited a non-monotonic effect: raising H₂SO₄ from 2.0 to 4.0 mol/L enhanced VE by stabilizing ionic conductivity and suppressing precipitation, whereas 5.0 mol/L acid promoted excessive hydrogen evolution and reduced CE from 97.1% to 93.8%. Flow rate optimization revealed that increasing from 10 to 40 mL/min mitigated concentration polarization and raised VE by 4.2 percentage points, but beyond 60 mL/min the incremental gain was outweighed by parasitic pumping losses, causing net system efficiency to decline. These findings demonstrate that maximizing energy density requires simultaneous optimization of all three parameters rather than isolated maximization of any single variable.

## 1. Introduction

Vanadium redox flow batteries have attracted considerable research attention as a promising technology for grid-scale energy storage due to their decoupled power and energy ratings, long cycle life, and flexible capacity scaling. The active material in a VRFB consists of vanadium ions at four different oxidation states (V²⁺, V³⁺, VO²⁺, VO₂⁺) dissolved in an aqueous sulfuric acid solution, with the electrochemical reactions occurring at porous carbon felt electrodes separated by an ion-exchange membrane. Despite these inherent advantages, the relatively low energy density of VRFBs compared to lithium-ion systems remains a persistent barrier to broader deployment in space-constrained applications.

The theoretical volumetric energy density of a VRFB is determined primarily by the concentration of the active vanadium species and the cell voltage, which depends on the standard electrode potentials of the V²⁺/V³⁺ and VO²⁺/VO₂⁺ redox couples. In principle, increasing the vanadium concentration should directly increase the energy density; however, the solubility and stability of vanadium species in sulfuric acid impose practical upper limits. At concentrations above approximately 1.8–2.0 mol/L and temperatures below 15°C, V(II) and V(V) species are prone to precipitation as VOSO₄·5H₂O and V₂O₅ respectively, restricting the usable concentration window and necessitating careful thermal management.

The supporting electrolyte concentration, specifically the H₂SO₄ concentration, plays an equally critical though sometimes underappreciated role. Sulfuric acid provides the proton conductivity required for charge compensation during cell operation, and its concentration affects not only the solution conductivity but also the speciation and stability of vanadium ions. At low acid concentrations, the limited proton availability increases ohmic resistance and can lead to lower voltage efficiency, particularly at elevated current densities. Conversely, excessively high acid concentrations promote competitive hydrogen evolution at the negative electrode, reducing coulombic efficiency and potentially destabilizing the vanadium ion equilibrium.

Beyond electrolyte chemistry, the flow rate of electrolyte through the cell stack represents a key operational parameter that governs the trade-off between electrochemical performance and auxiliary energy consumption. At low flow rates, insufficient replenishment of reactants at the electrode surface leads to concentration polarization, causing premature voltage decay during discharge and reduced voltage efficiency. Increasing the flow rate enhances mass transfer and reduces the diffusion boundary layer thickness, but simultaneously raises pumping power requirements. The net effect on system-level energy efficiency depends on the balance between these competing factors, and the optimal flow rate is expected to vary with current density and electrolyte composition.

While numerous studies have examined individual aspects of VRFB electrolyte formulation or flow-field design, relatively few have systematically quantified the coupled effects of vanadium concentration, sulfuric acid concentration, and flow rate on a consistent set of performance metrics including CE, VE, EE, concentration polarization overpotential, and practical energy density. The present work addresses this gap through an integrated parametric study, with the aim of establishing experimentally grounded design guidelines for electrolyte composition and flow conditions that maximize deliverable energy density while maintaining high round-trip energy efficiency.

## 2. Experimental Methods

All electrochemical tests were performed using a single-cell VRFB with an active area of 25 cm². The cell comprised graphite plate current collectors with serpentine flow fields, carbon felt electrodes (SGL GFD 4.6 EA) compressed to 20% thickness, and a Nafion 117 membrane pretreated according to standard procedures. Electrolytes were prepared by dissolving VOSO₄·xH₂O in sulfuric acid solutions of specified concentration, followed by electrolytic pre-charging to produce equal volumes of V²⁺/V³⁺ negative electrolyte and VO²⁺/VO₂⁺ positive electrolyte. Unless otherwise specified, each half-cell electrolyte volume was 50 mL.

Vanadium concentrations of 1.2, 1.5, 1.6, 1.8, and 2.0 mol/L were evaluated, with a selected set of experiments extended to 3.0 mol/L for stability assessment. Sulfuric acid concentrations ranged from 2.0 to 5.0 mol/L total sulfate, encompassing the typical operational window reported in the literature. Electrolyte flow rates of 10, 20, 40, 60, and 100 mL/min were controlled using peristaltic pumps with calibrated flow meters. Cell testing was conducted at constant current densities of 50, 100, and 200 mA/cm² within a voltage window of 0.8–1.65 V using a battery cycler. Coulombic efficiency was calculated as the ratio of discharge capacity to charge capacity, voltage efficiency as the ratio of average discharge voltage to average charge voltage, and energy efficiency as the product of CE and VE. Concentration polarization overpotential was estimated from the difference between the measured cell voltage at a given state of charge and the theoretical equilibrium voltage corrected for ohmic losses. Pumping power was measured directly using in-line pressure sensors and flow meters, and system-level energy efficiency was computed by subtracting the integrated pumping energy from the gross electrical energy output.

## 3. Results and Discussion

### 3.1 Effect of Vanadium Concentration on Energy Density and Efficiency

The theoretical volumetric energy density, calculated from the Nernst equation and the total vanadium concentration assuming complete utilization, increased linearly from 19.3 Wh/L at 1.2 mol/L to 25.7 Wh/L at 1.6 mol/L and further to 32.1 Wh/L at 2.0 mol/L. However, the practical energy density measured at 80% state-of-charge utilization was substantially lower, accounting for incomplete reactant conversion, ohmic losses, and concentration polarization. At 100 mA/cm² and a flow rate of 40 mL/min with 3.0 mol/L H₂SO₄, the practical energy density rose from 14.8 Wh/L at 1.2 mol/L vanadium to 20.3 Wh/L at 1.6 mol/L, representing only 66–79% of the theoretical values. The gap between theoretical and practical energy density widened at higher concentrations: at 2.0 mol/L vanadium, the practical energy density reached 22.1 Wh/L, merely 69% of the theoretical 32.1 Wh/L, indicating that transport and kinetic limitations become increasingly significant as the concentration increases.

Coulombic efficiency remained consistently high across the tested vanadium concentrations, varying between 96.2% and 97.5%, with the slight decrease at higher concentrations attributable to enhanced vanadium crossover driven by larger concentration gradients across the membrane. Voltage efficiency, in contrast, showed a more pronounced dependence on vanadium concentration. At 100 mA/cm², VE increased from 81.3% at 1.2 mol/L to 85.6% at 1.6 mol/L, primarily due to the reduced relative impact of fixed overpotential losses at higher operating voltages associated with greater Nernstian potential differences. Beyond 1.6 mol/L, however, VE declined slightly to 84.9% at 2.0 mol/L, as the elevated viscosity of the concentrated electrolyte—rising from 3.2 mPa·s at 1.2 mol/L to 7.8 mPa·s at 2.0 mol/L at 25°C—impeded mass transfer and increased the concentration polarization overpotential.

Energy efficiency followed the combined trends of CE and VE, peaking at 83.5% for 1.6 mol/L vanadium at 100 mA/cm² and 40 mL/min, compared to 78.2% for 1.2 mol/L and 82.8% for 2.0 mol/L. At the higher current density of 200 mA/cm², the optimal concentration shifted to 1.5 mol/L, where an EE of 76.4% was recorded, surpassing the 72.1% obtained at 1.6 mol/L and the 68.9% at 2.0 mol/L. This shift reflects the growing importance of mass-transfer limitations at elevated current densities, where the higher viscosity of concentrated electrolytes disproportionately penalizes performance. At 50 mA/cm², the lower current density minimized concentration polarization effects, and the highest tested concentration of 2.0 mol/L yielded the maximum EE of 86.1%, suggesting that the optimal vanadium concentration is current-density-dependent and that system designers must match electrolyte formulation to anticipated operating conditions.

Long-term stability testing at 3.0 mol/L vanadium in 3.0 mol/L H₂SO₄ revealed gradual precipitation of V(V) species at the positive electrode after approximately 72 hours of continuous cycling at 25°C, with CE declining from an initial 95.8% to 89.3% over 50 cycles. This observation confirms that while higher vanadium concentrations offer attractive theoretical energy density gains, the practical stability window is constrained by precipitation kinetics and operating temperature.

### 3.2 Role of Sulfuric Acid Concentration in Determining Efficiency and Stability

The sulfuric acid concentration profoundly influenced both the ionic conductivity of the electrolyte and the side reaction behavior at the negative electrode. At a fixed vanadium concentration of 1.6 mol/L and flow rate of 40 mL/min, increasing H₂SO₄ concentration from 2.0 to 4.0 mol/L raised the solution conductivity from 320 to 480 mS/cm at 25°C and improved VE at 100 mA/cm² from 82.1% to 86.2%. This improvement stems from enhanced proton transport, which reduces ohmic resistance and facilitates charge compensation during the V²⁺/V³⁺ redox reaction. The reduced ohmic overpotential at 4.0 mol/L H₂SO₄ corresponded to a cell resistance decrease from 1.85 Ω·cm² to 1.42 Ω·cm², as determined by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy.

Further increasing the acid concentration to 5.0 mol/L, however, produced diminishing and ultimately negative returns. While conductivity continued to increase marginally to 510 mS/cm, VE actually decreased to 85.1%, and CE dropped sharply from 97.1% to 93.8%. The decline in CE was accompanied by visible gas evolution at the negative electrode during charging, consistent with enhanced hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) kinetics in strongly acidic media. The competitive HER not only represents a direct loss of charge efficiency but also alters the electrolyte pH balance over extended cycling, potentially destabilizing vanadium speciation and promoting hydrolysis precipitation.

The non-monotonic dependence of energy efficiency on H₂SO₄ concentration resulted in a clear optimum at 4.0 mol/L, where EE reached 83.7% at 100 mA/cm², compared to 79.8% at 2.0 mol/L, 83.5% at 3.0 mol/L, and 79.7% at 5.0 mol/L. Interestingly, the relative penalty of low acid concentration was more severe at elevated current densities: at 200 mA/cm², reducing H₂SO₄ from 4.0 to 2.0 mol/L caused VE to fall from 79.8% to 71.4%, a decrement of 8.4 percentage points, whereas at 50 mA/cm² the corresponding decrease was only 3.1 percentage points (from 90.2% to 87.1%). This current-density sensitivity arises because ohmic overpotential scales linearly with current density, making high-conductivity electrolytes progressively more important as the operating power density increases.

Temperature-dependent precipitation tests showed that the stability of 1.6 mol/L vanadium electrolytes against V(V) precipitation improved with increasing acid concentration up to 4.0 mol/L, with the onset temperature for visible precipitation decreasing from 28°C at 2.0 mol/L H₂SO₄ to 12°C at 4.0 mol/L. At 5.0 mol/L, the precipitation temperature remained similar to 4.0 mol/L, but the HER-induced pH drift over cycling reintroduced stability risks, suggesting that 4.0 mol/L represents a practical upper bound for H₂SO₄ concentration in long-term operation. These results underscore that electrolyte composition optimization requires balancing conductivity enhancement against side reaction suppression, and that the frequently cited "higher acid is better" guideline must be qualified by HER considerations.

### 3.3 Flow Rate Optimization: Trade-offs Between Mass Transfer and Pumping Loss

The electrolyte flow rate governed the balance between electrochemical performance and parasitic energy consumption in a manner that varied substantially with current density and electrolyte concentration. At 1.6 mol/L vanadium, 3.0 mol/L H₂SO₄, and 100 mA/cm², increasing the flow rate from 10 to 20 mL/min raised VE from 81.4% to 84.1%, and further to 85.6% at 40 mL/min. This improvement reflects enhanced convective mass transfer of vanadium ions to the electrode surface, which reduces the concentration difference between the bulk electrolyte and the electrode–electrolyte interface. The concentration polarization overpotential, estimated from voltage decay analysis, decreased from 68 mV at 10 mL/min to 31 mV at 40 mL/min, indicating that the low-flow condition imposed a significant transport limitation.

Between 40 and 60 mL/min, the incremental gain in VE diminished: at 60 mL/min, VE reached 86.3%, only 0.7 percentage points higher than at 40 mL/min. By 100 mL/min, VE had essentially plateaued at 86.5%, suggesting that above 40 mL/min the electrode surface concentration approaches the bulk concentration and further flow increases yield negligible electrochemical benefit. The mass-transfer coefficient, calculated from the limiting current behavior, increased from 2.1 × 10⁻⁵ m/s at 10 mL/min to 4.3 × 10⁻⁵ m/s at 40 mL/min, but only to 4.7 × 10⁻⁵ m/s at 60 mL/min and 4.9 × 10⁻⁵ m/s at 100 mL/min, confirming the asymptotic approach to a mass-transfer limit imposed by the porous electrode structure rather than the external flow rate alone.

Pumping power, however, increased approximately with the square of flow rate due to the dominant pressure-drop contribution from the porous carbon felt and serpentine flow channels. The measured pumping power rose from 12 mW at 10 mL/min to 48 mW at 40 mL/min, 108 mW at 60 mL/min, and 300 mW at 100 mL/min. When expressed as a fraction of the gross electrical power output, the parasitic pumping loss increased from 0.6% at 10 mL/min to 2.4% at 40 mL/min, 5.4% at 60 mL/min, and 15.0% at 100 mL/min. The net system energy efficiency, defined as gross EE minus the pumping energy penalty, exhibited a clear maximum at 40 mL/min, where the net EE was 81.5%, compared to 78.0% at 10 mL/min, 80.9% at 60 mL/min, and 71.5% at 100 mL/min.

This optimum shifted with current density. At 200 mA/cm², the higher reactant flux requirement moved the optimal flow rate to 60 mL/min, where net EE peaked at 73.2%, compared to 68.9% at 40 mL/min and 64.1% at 100 mL/min. At 50 mA/cm², in contrast, the lower polarization sensitivity meant that even 20 mL/min was sufficient, yielding a net EE of 84.7% that was only marginally below the 85.0% at 40 mL/min, making the lower flow rate attractive for low-power applications where minimizing auxiliary energy is paramount. These results demonstrate that flow rate optimization cannot be performed independently of the intended operating regime and that the frequently recommended "high flow rate" strategy must be evaluated against its pumping cost in the context of specific current density targets.

### 3.4 Coupled Parameter Interactions and System-Level Implications

The individual parameter studies revealed that vanadium concentration, sulfuric acid concentration, and flow rate interact in non-trivial ways that preclude simple additive optimization. For instance, the viscosity increase associated with high vanadium concentrations amplifies the pressure drop and hence the pumping penalty at any given flow rate. At 2.0 mol/L vanadium and 100 mL/min, the pumping power reached 420 mW, 40% higher than the 300 mW measured at 1.6 mol/L under identical hydrodynamic conditions, because the viscosity rose from 5.1 mPa·s to 7.8 mPa·s. This viscosity-mediated coupling shifts the net EE optimum to lower flow rates for more concentrated electrolytes: at 2.0 mol/L, the optimal flow rate was 40 mL/min rather than 60 mL/min, with a corresponding net EE of 78.9% compared to 82.8% at 1.6 mol/L.

Similarly, the sulfuric acid concentration affects the optimal flow rate through its influence on conductivity and the relative importance of ohmic versus concentration polarization losses. At 2.0 mol/L H₂SO₄ and 100 mA/cm², the lower conductivity rendered ohmic losses dominant, and the flow-rate-mediated mass-transfer improvement contributed less to overall VE than at 4.0 mol/L H₂SO₄ where ohmic losses were already minimal. Consequently, the VE improvement from increasing flow rate from 10 to 40 mL/min was 3.2 percentage points at 2.0 mol/L H₂SO₄ but only 2.1 percentage points at 4.0 mol/L H₂SO₄, implying that high-acid electrolytes are less sensitive to flow optimization.

A comprehensive assessment of practical energy density versus net system efficiency reveals a Pareto-optimal front that system designers must navigate. The combination of 1.6 mol/L vanadium, 4.0 mol/L H₂SO₄, and 40 mL/min at 100 mA/cm² yielded a practical energy density of 21.5 Wh/L with a net EE of 82.1%, representing the best balanced performance in the tested parameter space. Increasing vanadium to 2.0 mol/L raised the practical energy density to 23.8 Wh/L but reduced net EE to 78.9% due to viscosity-enhanced pumping losses and mass-transfer limitations. Conversely, reducing to 1.5 mol/L vanadium lowered the practical energy density to 19.1 Wh/L but allowed a net EE of 83.2%, suggesting that this composition may be preferable for efficiency-critical applications where space constraints are secondary.

The current density choice further complicates this optimization landscape. At 50 mA/cm², the combination of 1.6 mol/L vanadium, 4.0 mol/L H₂SO₄, and 20 mL/min delivered a practical energy density of 21.2 Wh/L with a net EE of 84.7%, whereas at 200 mA/cm² the same electrolyte composition required 60 mL/min to achieve acceptable polarization behavior, yielding a practical energy density of 20.8 Wh/L but a reduced net EE of 74.5%. The relatively modest energy density penalty at 200 mA/cm²—only 4 Wh/L lower than at 50 mA/cm²—may be acceptable in applications where high power density is required, but the 10.2 percentage point efficiency reduction has significant implications for thermal management and operating cost over the system lifetime.

## 4. Conclusion

This systematic investigation of electrolyte concentration and flow rate effects in VRFBs has revealed several key findings with direct relevance to system design and optimization. First, increasing vanadium concentration from 1.2 to 1.6 mol/L improves both theoretical and practical energy density, with EE peaking at 83.5% at 100 mA/cm²; however, further increases to 2.0 mol/L introduce viscosity-driven mass-transfer penalties that partially offset the concentration benefit, and 3.0 mol/L electrolytes exhibit precipitation instability within 72 hours at 25°C. Second, sulfuric acid concentration exhibits a non-monotonic effect with an optimum at approximately 4.0 mol/L, where the balance between proton conductivity enhancement and hydrogen evolution suppression yields maximum EE of 83.7%. Deviations to 2.0 or 5.0 mol/L both reduce EE to approximately 80%, albeit through different mechanisms—ohmic losses in the former case and CE degradation from HER in the latter. Third, flow rate optimization follows a clear trade-off curve: increasing from 10 to 40 mL/min mitigates concentration polarization and raises net system EE from 78.0% to 81.5%, but further increases beyond 60 mL/min disproportionately increase pumping losses and reduce net efficiency. The optimal flow rate depends on current density, shifting from 20 mL/min at 50 mA/cm² to 40 mL/min at 100 mA/cm² and 60 mL/min at 200 mA/cm². These results demonstrate that maximizing VRFB performance requires integrated optimization of electrolyte composition and hydrodynamic conditions, and that isolated maximization of any single parameter—whether vanadium concentration, acid concentration, or flow rate—leads to suboptimal system-level outcomes. Future work should extend these findings to multi-cell stacks and explore temperature-dependent optimization to broaden the practical operating window for high-concentration electrolytes.

# Temperature-Dependent Cycling Stability and Capacity Fading Mechanisms in Vanadium Redox Flow Batteries

## Abstract

Understanding the temperature-dependent degradation behavior of vanadium redox flow batteries (VRFBs) is essential for optimizing long-term operational performance across diverse climatic conditions. This study systematically investigates the cycling stability and capacity fading mechanisms of a laboratory-scale VRFB system operated at 25°C, 40°C, and 60°C over extended cycle life. At a constant current density of 80 mA/cm², the battery achieved initial discharge capacities of 1420 mAh, 1415 mAh, and 1435 mAh at 25°C, 40°C, and 60°C respectively. After 500 charge-discharge cycles, the capacity retention decreased markedly with increasing temperature: 85% at 25°C, 74% at 40°C, and only 60% at 60°C. Coulombic efficiency exhibited a gradual decline from 97.2% to 94.8% at 25°C, from 97.0% to 92.1% at 40°C, and from 96.8% to 88.5% at 60°C over the same cycling period. Post-cycling analysis revealed that vanadium crossover rates increased exponentially with temperature, measuring 2.1 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s at 25°C, 3.8 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s at 40°C, and 6.5 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s at 60°C after 200 cycles. Electrolyte imbalance, quantified by the state-of-charge (SOC) difference between the positive and negative half-cells, diverged progressively: the SOC differential reached 8% at 25°C, 15% at 40°C, and 24% at 60°C after 500 cycles. Periodic electrolyte remixing was demonstrated to partially restore capacity, with recovery efficiencies of 62% at 25°C, 55% at 40°C, and 41% at 60°C relative to the faded capacity. These findings elucidate the coupled roles of thermal acceleration of vanadium ion transport through the ion exchange membrane and the resulting electrolyte imbalance as primary drivers of temperature-enhanced capacity fading in VRFBs.

## 1. Introduction

Vanadium redox flow batteries have emerged as one of the most promising electrochemical energy storage technologies for grid-scale applications due to their decoupled energy and power ratings, long cycle life potential, and the unique advantage of employing the same vanadium element in both half-cell electrolytes. However, the long-term operational stability of VRFBs remains a critical challenge that limits widespread commercial deployment, particularly in applications subject to variable thermal environments. Temperature exerts profound effects on virtually every aspect of VRFB performance: electrochemical kinetics, mass transport, membrane permeability, and the thermodynamic stability of vanadium species in sulfuric acid supporting electrolyte.

While considerable research has focused on individual degradation modes in VRFBs at ambient or single-temperature conditions, systematic investigations spanning a broad temperature range with quantified capacity fading trajectories remain relatively scarce. The existing literature suggests that elevated temperatures accelerate capacity fade through multiple parallel pathways, yet the relative contributions of each mechanism at different temperatures and the quantitative relationships between thermal conditions, cycling duration, and performance metrics are not fully established. This knowledge gap hinders the development of accurate predictive degradation models and temperature-adaptive battery management strategies.

The present work addresses this gap by conducting controlled long-term cycling experiments at three representative temperatures: 25°C representing standard room-temperature operation, 40°C corresponding to moderate thermal stress typical of warm-climate installations without active cooling, and 60°C representing severe thermal stress that may occur during fault conditions or in poorly ventilated enclosures. By maintaining consistent electrolyte compositions, membrane materials, and current density protocols across all temperature conditions, this study isolates the thermal contribution to capacity fading and provides quantitative benchmarks for temperature-dependent degradation rates. The investigation further examines the effectiveness of periodic electrolyte remixing as a mitigation strategy for temperature-accelerated capacity loss.

## 2. Experimental Methods

All cycling experiments were conducted using a laboratory-scale VRFB single cell with an active electrode area of 25 cm². The cell employed graphite felt electrodes (SGL GFD4.6, 4.6 mm thickness) compressed to 80% of their original thickness, separated by a Nafion 115 proton exchange membrane. The electrolyte consisted of 1.6 M V³⁺ in 4.5 M total sulfate (prepared from VOSO₄ and subsequent electrochemical reduction) for the negative half-cell and 1.6 M VO²⁺ in 4.5 M total sulfate for the positive half-cell, with 60 mL of electrolyte in each reservoir.

Charge-discharge cycling was performed at a constant current density of 80 mA/cm² between voltage limits of 0.9 V and 1.65 V using a Bio-Logic VMP-3 potentiostat/galvanostat. Three identical cells were assembled and operated in temperature-controlled environmental chambers maintained at 25°C, 40°C, and 60°C (±0.5°C) respectively. Electrolyte circulation was driven by peristaltic pumps at a constant flow rate of 40 mL/min. Each cycling test was continued until the discharge capacity fell below 45% of the initial value or until 500 cycles were completed, whichever occurred first.

Capacity retention was calculated as the ratio of the discharge capacity at cycle N to the initial discharge capacity at cycle 1, expressed as a percentage. Coulombic efficiency (CE) was determined as the ratio of discharge capacity to charge capacity for each cycle. Electrolyte crossover was assessed by periodically sampling 0.5 mL aliquots from each half-cell reservoir and analyzing vanadium concentrations using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES). The state-of-charge (SOC) of each half-cell was estimated from the ratio of V³⁺ to total vanadium in the negative electrolyte and VO₂⁺ to total vanadium in the positive electrolyte, measured by UV-Vis spectrophotometry. Electrolyte remixing was performed at 100-cycle intervals by transferring the entire electrolyte volume from both reservoirs into a single vessel, mixing thoroughly for 30 minutes under nitrogen atmosphere, and redistributing equally back to the respective half-cells.

## 3. Results and Discussion

### 3.1 Temperature-Dependent Capacity Fading Trajectories

The capacity fading behavior exhibited strong and systematic dependence on operating temperature throughout the 500-cycle test duration. At 25°C, the battery demonstrated relatively stable performance for the initial 200 cycles, with capacity declining gradually from an initial 1420 mAh to 1310 mAh (92% retention). Beyond cycle 200, the fade rate accelerated modestly, reaching 85% capacity retention at cycle 500. The fading trajectory at 40°C followed a qualitatively similar pattern but with noticeably steeper decline rates: capacity retention stood at 88% after 100 cycles, 82% after 200 cycles, and progressively decreased to 74% by cycle 500. The most severe fading was observed at 60°C, where the capacity dropped to 78% of its initial value within the first 100 cycles, to 70% after 200 cycles, and reached only 60% retention at cycle 500. Notably, the cell at 60°C would have fallen below the 45% termination threshold had the test been extended to approximately 850 cycles, based on the observed linear fade rate in the later cycling stages.

The capacity fade rates, calculated as the average percentage capacity loss per 100 cycles, were 3.0% per 100 cycles at 25°C, 5.2% per 100 cycles at 40°C, and 8.0% per 100 cycles at 60°C. These values reveal a non-linear acceleration of fading with temperature: increasing from 25°C to 40°C (a 15°C rise) increased the fade rate by a factor of 1.73, while increasing from 40°C to 60°C (a 20°C rise) increased it by a factor of 1.54. This non-linearity suggests that the temperature dependence of capacity fading cannot be captured by a simple Arrhenius-type single-process model and instead reflects the combined activation of multiple degradation pathways with differing temperature sensitivities.

The voltage efficiency (VE) also exhibited temperature-dependent behavior, though the trends differed from capacity retention. VE values were 85.3%, 87.1%, and 89.2% at 25°C, 40°C, and 60°C respectively during the initial 50 cycles, reflecting the beneficial effect of temperature on electrochemical kinetics and mass transport. However, VE degraded faster at elevated temperatures due to increased shunt currents and parasitic reactions, such that by cycle 500, the VE values had converged to 82.5% at 25°C, 81.8% at 40°C, and 79.4% at 60°C. Energy efficiency followed the combined trends of CE and VE, starting at 82.8% at 25°C and declining to 78.2% at cycle 500, while the 60°C cell started at 86.4% but dropped more sharply to 71.5% by cycle 500.

### 3.2 Coulombic Efficiency Degradation and Vanadium Crossover

Coulombic efficiency provides critical insight into the extent of parasitic processes that consume charge without contributing to reversible energy storage. The initial CE values were remarkably consistent across temperatures: 97.2% at 25°C, 97.0% at 40°C, and 96.8% at 60°C. These values indicate that even at the beginning of life, elevated temperatures introduce measurable increases in charge inefficiency, primarily through accelerated vanadium crossover and enhanced side reaction kinetics.

The CE degradation over 500 cycles followed distinctly different trajectories at each temperature. At 25°C, CE declined gradually to 94.8%, representing a total loss of 2.4 percentage points. At 40°C, the decline was more pronounced, with CE reaching 92.1% at cycle 500 (a 4.9 percentage point loss). The most dramatic CE degradation occurred at 60°C, where CE plummeted to 88.5% by cycle 500, corresponding to a total loss of 8.3 percentage points. Importantly, the rate of CE degradation itself accelerated over the cycling duration at all temperatures, suggesting a positive feedback mechanism in which early-stage degradation creates conditions that accelerate subsequent degradation.

Vanadium crossover measurements conducted at 50-cycle intervals revealed the underlying transport mechanism driving CE loss. The effective vanadium crossover diffusion coefficients, calculated from the measured concentration changes in each half-cell, increased with both temperature and cycling duration. At cycle 50, the crossover rates were 1.2 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s at 25°C, 2.0 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s at 40°C, and 3.4 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s at 60°C. By cycle 200, these values had risen to 2.1 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s, 3.8 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s, and 6.5 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s respectively. The cycling-induced increase in crossover rates (a 75% increase from cycle 50 to cycle 200 at 25°C, 90% at 40°C, and 91% at 60°C) indicates that membrane degradation and chemical changes in the membrane structure compound the purely thermal acceleration of diffusion.

The crossover data further revealed an asymmetric transport behavior: vanadium ions migrated predominantly from the positive half-cell (VO²⁺/VO₂⁺) to the negative half-cell (V²⁺/V³⁺) at all temperatures. This directionality arises from the concentration gradients established by the differential stability of vanadium species: the V(V) species (VO₂⁺) exhibits higher membrane permeability due to its smaller hydrated ionic radius and lower charge density compared to V(II) species. At 60°C, the cumulative vanadium accumulation in the negative electrolyte reached 0.18 M after 200 cycles, compared to 0.06 M at 25°C, directly contributing to the larger capacity losses observed at elevated temperatures.

### 3.3 Electrolyte Imbalance and SOC Divergence

A direct consequence of asymmetric vanadium crossover is the progressive development of electrolyte imbalance, manifested as a growing divergence in the state-of-charge (SOC) between the positive and negative half-cells. Under ideal balanced operation, both half-cells maintain identical SOC values throughout cycling. However, as vanadium species accumulate asymmetrically and side reactions consume active species at different rates, the SOC profiles diverge.

At 25°C, the SOC difference between half-cells remained below 3% for the first 100 cycles, then gradually increased to 8% by cycle 500. This modest imbalance indicates that the battery at room temperature maintains reasonably balanced operation over extended cycling, with sufficient self-regulating mechanisms to prevent runaway divergence. At 40°C, the SOC divergence progressed more rapidly: the differential reached 6% after 100 cycles, 11% after 200 cycles, and 15% after 500 cycles. The accelerated SOC divergence at 40°C correlates with the higher vanadium crossover rates and the increased prevalence of water transfer and side reactions that asymmetrically alter the active species inventory in each half-cell.

The most severe electrolyte imbalance developed at 60°C, where the SOC differential reached 10% after only 50 cycles, 17% after 100 cycles, and 24% after 500 cycles. This substantial imbalance directly limits the usable capacity of the battery because the half-cell with the lower effective SOC determines the capacity-limiting electrode. The relationship between SOC divergence and capacity retention follows an approximately linear trend across all temperatures: each 1% increase in SOC differential corresponds to a capacity loss of approximately 0.6-0.8% relative to the theoretical balanced capacity. At 60°C, the 24% SOC differential accounts for roughly 60% of the total 40% capacity loss, with the remainder attributed to irreversible vanadium precipitation and active species depletion through parasitic reactions.

The chemical origin of the SOC divergence lies in the differential rates of the parasitic oxygen and hydrogen evolution reactions at the positive and negative electrodes respectively. At elevated temperatures, water oxidation at the positive electrode becomes increasingly favorable, converting VO₂⁺ back to VO²⁺ while evolving oxygen, which effectively reduces the SOC of the positive half-cell. Concurrently, hydrogen evolution at the negative electrode during charging consumes protons and reduces V³⁺ to V²⁺, altering the negative half-cell SOC in the opposite direction. These side reactions create a compounding effect: as the SOC diverges, the overpotentials at each electrode increase, further driving the parasitic reactions and accelerating the imbalance.

### 3.4 Effectiveness of Electrolyte Remixing as Mitigation Strategy

Given the central role of electrolyte imbalance in capacity fading, periodic remixing of the positive and negative electrolytes represents a straightforward mitigation approach that can restore species inventory balance without replacing the electrolyte. In this study, remixing was performed at 100-cycle intervals by combining and thoroughly mixing the electrolytes from both half-cells before redistributing them equally.

The capacity recovery achieved through remixing showed clear temperature dependence, consistent with the extent of imbalance present at each condition. At 25°C, where the SOC divergence was modest, the first remixing event at cycle 100 restored 89% of the faded capacity, recovering the system from 96% retention back to 99.2% of the initial capacity. Subsequent remixing events at cycles 200, 300, 400, and 500 achieved progressively lower recovery fractions as irreversible degradation accumulated, with the cycle 500 remixing restoring 62% of the accumulated fade. Over the full 500-cycle test, periodic remixing improved the net capacity retention from 85% to 92.5% at 25°C.

At 40°C, the effectiveness of remixing was moderately reduced. The first remixing at cycle 100 recovered 82% of the faded capacity, but by cycle 500, only 55% of the accumulated fade could be reversed. The net capacity retention with periodic remixing improved from 74% to 84% over 500 cycles. The reduced recovery efficiency at 40°C reflects the larger contribution of irreversible degradation mechanisms, including some vanadium precipitation and membrane chemical degradation, which remixing cannot address.

At 60°C, remixing provided only partial relief from capacity fading. The first remixing at cycle 100 recovered 71% of the faded capacity, but the recovery efficiency declined sharply with subsequent cycling. By cycle 500, only 41% of the accumulated fade was recoverable through remixing, yielding a net retention improvement from 60% to only 70.5%. The substantially diminished effectiveness of remixing at 60°C indicates that at this temperature, irreversible degradation mechanisms dominate the capacity loss. Post-mortem analysis of the membrane from the 60°C cell revealed significant chemical degradation including sulfonic acid group loss and polymer backbone cleavage, which increased membrane permeability permanently and could not be reversed by electrolyte remixing. Additionally, visible vanadium precipitate was observed in the positive electrolyte reservoir at 60°C after 300 cycles, representing an irreversible loss of active material from the dissolved electrolyte phase.

### 3.5 Degradation Mechanism Analysis and Comparative Assessment

The experimental results collectively support a degradation mechanism framework in which temperature acts as an accelerator of multiple coupled degradation pathways rather than activating a single dominant fade mechanism. At the foundation of the degradation hierarchy lies vanadium crossover through the ion exchange membrane, which is thermally activated with an effective activation energy of approximately 28-32 kJ/mol based on the measured diffusion coefficients. The crossover creates a primary electrolyte imbalance that directly reduces usable capacity by desynchronizing the SOC of the two half-cells.

Superimposed on the crossover-driven imbalance are temperature-accelerated side reactions. Oxygen evolution at the positive electrode and hydrogen evolution at the negative electrode become increasingly significant above 40°C, contributing to net coulombic inefficiency and further exacerbating the electrolyte imbalance. Water transport through the membrane, driven by electro-osmotic drag and hydraulic pressure gradients, compounds the imbalance by altering the concentration and volume of electrolyte in each half-cell asymmetrically.

The positive feedback nature of the degradation emerges from the interaction between these mechanisms. Increased crossover leads to higher concentrations of foreign vanadium species in each half-cell, which raises the ionic strength and modifies the local pH, both of which accelerate the precipitation of V(V) compounds from the positive electrolyte at elevated temperature. Membrane chemical degradation, in turn, increases the effective pore size and reduces the selectivity, permitting even faster crossover in later cycles. This compounding effect explains why the capacity fade rate accelerates over the cycling duration and why the temperature dependence of fading exceeds what would be predicted from the Arrhenius behavior of diffusion alone.

Comparing the relative importance of different mechanisms across temperatures, the analysis suggests that at 25°C, vanadium crossover accounts for approximately 70% of the total capacity fade, with side reactions and minor precipitation contributing the remaining 30%. At 40°C, crossover contributes approximately 55%, side reactions 30%, and precipitation 15% of the total fade. At 60°C, the picture shifts dramatically: crossover contributes only 35%, while side reactions and precipitation together account for 65% of the capacity loss. This shift in the dominant degradation mode has important implications for mitigation strategy selection: whereas crossover-reduction strategies such as membrane modification and remixing are effective at lower temperatures, higher-temperature operation demands strategies that also address side reaction suppression and precipitation inhibition, such as electrolyte additives and improved thermal management.

## 4. Conclusion

This study has provided a comprehensive quantitative characterization of temperature-dependent cycling stability and capacity fading in vanadium redox flow batteries through controlled long-term experiments at 25°C, 40°C, and 60°C. The principal findings establish that capacity fading accelerates non-linearly with temperature, with fade rates of 3.0%, 5.2%, and 8.0% per 100 cycles at the three test temperatures respectively. Coulombic efficiency degradation paralleled the capacity fading, declining by 2.4, 4.9, and 8.3 percentage points over 500 cycles at 25°C, 40°C, and 60°C.

Vanadium crossover through the Nafion 115 membrane was identified as the foundational degradation driver, with effective diffusion coefficients increasing from 2.1 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s at 25°C to 6.5 × 10⁻⁷ cm²/s at 60°C after 200 cycles. The resulting electrolyte imbalance, quantified by SOC divergence between half-cells, reached 8% at 25°C, 15% at 40°C, and 24% at 60°C after 500 cycles. Periodic electrolyte remixing proved effective at mitigating crossover-driven capacity loss, improving net 500-cycle capacity retention from 85% to 92.5% at 25°C, from 74% to 84% at 40°C, and from 60% to 70.5% at 60°C.

The degradation mechanism analysis revealed a shift in the dominant fade mode with increasing temperature: from crossover-dominated at 25°C to a regime where side reactions and vanadium precipitation dominate at 60°C. This mechanistic shift explains the declining effectiveness of remixing at elevated temperatures and underscores the critical importance of active thermal management for VRFB systems operating in warm climates. For installations where passive cooling cannot maintain electrolyte temperature below 35°C, the incorporation of both periodic remixing protocols and electrolyte stabilization additives is recommended to achieve acceptable cycle life. Future work should focus on developing membranes with reduced temperature sensitivity of vanadium permeability and on quantifying the synergistic benefits of combining remixing with other degradation mitigation strategies under realistic variable-temperature operating profiles.

# Bismuth Ion as an Electrolyte Additive for Suppressing Hydrogen Evolution and Enhancing Chromium Redox Kinetics in Iron-Chromium Redox Flow Batteries

## Abstract

The iron-chromium redox flow battery (ICRFB) represents a promising candidate for large-scale electrochemical energy storage, yet its commercial viability is severely constrained by the competitive hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) on the negative electrode and the sluggish kinetics of the Cr(III)/Cr(II) redox couple. In this work, we investigate the use of bismuth ions (Bi3+) as a homogeneous electrolyte additive to simultaneously address both limitations. Cyclic voltammetry and rotating disk electrode measurements demonstrate that the addition of Bi3+ at concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 3.0 ppm substantially accelerates the Cr(III)/Cr(II) charge transfer reaction, with the standard rate constant increasing from 2.1 × 10^-3 cm/s in the pristine electrolyte to 4.8 × 10^-3 cm/s upon the introduction of 2.0 ppm Bi3+. Simultaneously, Bi3+ undergoes underpotential deposition on the graphite felt electrode surface, forming a bismuth adlayer that increases the HER overpotential by approximately 85 mV at 80 mA/cm^2. Single-cell tests conducted at current densities of 40, 80, 120, and 160 mA/cm^2 reveal that the optimal Bi3+ concentration of 2.0 ppm yields a coulombic efficiency of 94% and an energy efficiency of 87% at 80 mA/cm^2, compared to 87% and 72% respectively for the baseline cell without additive. Long-term cycling over 200 charge-discharge cycles at 80 mA/cm^2 confirms that the Bi3+-containing cell maintains stable performance with a capacity fade rate of 0.03% per cycle, whereas the baseline cell exhibits accelerated capacity degradation at 0.12% per cycle, predominantly attributable to hydrogen accumulation and subsequent electrolyte imbalance. These findings establish Bi3+ as an effective, low-cost additive strategy for improving the performance and durability of ICRFB systems.

## 1. Introduction

Redox flow batteries (RFBs) have garnered substantial research attention as grid-scale energy storage technologies capable of decoupling energy and power ratings, thereby enabling flexible system design for integrating intermittent renewable energy sources. Among the various RFB chemistries explored to date, the all-vanadium redox flow battery (VRFB) has achieved the highest degree of commercial maturity; however, the high cost of vanadium salts and the limited availability of vanadium resources have motivated continued exploration of alternative redox couples based on earth-abundant elements. The iron-chromium redox flow battery (ICRFB), which utilizes Fe(III)/Fe(II) and Cr(III)/Cr(II) redox couples in acidic aqueous electrolytes, offers a potentially attractive cost advantage owing to the natural abundance and low cost of both iron and chromium precursors.

Despite its economic appeal, the ICRFB faces two fundamental technical barriers that have impeded its widespread deployment. The first and most critical challenge arises from the thermodynamic favorability of the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) on the negative electrode, where the standard reduction potential of the Cr(III)/Cr(II) couple (-0.41 V vs. SHE) lies negative to the reversible hydrogen electrode potential under typical operating conditions. This potential overlap results in substantial parasitic hydrogen evolution during the charging process, leading to low coulombic efficiency (CE), rapid electrolyte imbalance, and accelerated capacity fade over extended cycling. The second challenge concerns the intrinsically slow kinetics of the Cr(III)/Cr(II) redox reaction, which is characterized by a large inner-sphere reorganization energy associated with the d-electron transfer process in octahedral chromium complexes. This kinetic limitation manifests as high activation overpotential, restricting the operating current density and thus reducing the power density and energy efficiency (EE) of the ICRFB system.

A diverse range of strategies has been pursued to mitigate HER and enhance chromium redox kinetics in ICRFBs. These include the development of catalytic electrode materials such as bismuth-modified carbon felts, gold nanoparticle-decorated electrodes, and nitrogen-doped carbon surfaces; the optimization of electrolyte compositions through the addition of catalytic metal ions, complexing agents, and supporting electrolytes; and the engineering of cell configurations including flow field design and membrane selection. Among these approaches, the use of bismuth-based additives has attracted particular interest due to the high hydrogen overpotential of metallic bismuth and its ability to form underpotential deposition layers on carbon substrates. Previous studies have primarily focused on electrodeposited bismuth coatings on solid electrodes, which require additional fabrication steps and may suffer from delamination during extended operation.

In contrast to solid-phase bismuth modification, the present work explores the direct addition of Bi3+ ions to the electrolyte as a homogeneous additive that can continuously replenish the bismuth adlayer on the electrode surface through dynamic adsorption-desorption equilibrium. This approach offers several potential advantages, including simplified cell assembly, uniform distribution of the bismuth species throughout the porous electrode structure, and self-healing of the catalytic surface during long-term cycling. Herein, we report a systematic investigation of Bi3+ additive effects on the electrochemical kinetics of the Cr(III)/Cr(II) couple, the HER suppression mechanism, and the overall performance of a laboratory-scale ICRFB single cell. The influence of Bi3+ concentration is examined across the range of 0.5 to 3.0 ppm, and the relationship between additive concentration, operating current density, and cell efficiency is rigorously quantified. Furthermore, the long-term cycling stability of Bi3+-containing ICRFB cells is evaluated and compared against baseline configurations to assess the practical viability of this additive strategy.

## 2. Electrochemical Characterization of Cr(III)/Cr(II) Redox Kinetics with Bi3+ Additive

### 2.1 Cyclic Voltammetry Analysis

The electrochemical behavior of the Cr(III)/Cr(II) redox couple in the presence of varying Bi3+ concentrations was first characterized by cyclic voltammetry (CV) on a glassy carbon electrode in 1.0 M HCl containing 0.5 M CrCl3. Figure 1a presents the CV curves recorded at a scan rate of 50 mV/s for electrolytes containing 0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, and 3.0 ppm Bi3+, added in the form of BiCl3. In the absence of Bi3+, the Cr(III) reduction peak appears at -0.58 V vs. Ag/AgCl with a peak current density of 3.2 mA/cm^2, while the Cr(II) oxidation peak is observed at -0.31 V vs. Ag/AgCl at 2.8 mA/cm^2. The large peak separation of 270 mV indicates quasi-reversible electron transfer kinetics consistent with the known inner-sphere character of the Cr(III)/Cr(II) couple.

Upon the addition of Bi3+, significant changes in the voltammetric response are observed. At 0.5 ppm Bi3+, the reduction peak shifts positively to -0.54 V vs. Ag/AgCl and the peak current density increases to 3.8 mA/cm^2, suggesting an onset of catalytic enhancement. As the Bi3+ concentration is progressively increased to 1.0 ppm and 1.5 ppm, the reduction peaks continue to shift toward less negative potentials, reaching -0.50 V and -0.47 V respectively, while the corresponding peak current densities rise to 4.3 mA/cm^2 and 4.7 mA/cm^2. The most pronounced catalytic effect is observed at 2.0 ppm Bi3+, where the reduction peak potential shifts to -0.44 V vs. Ag/AgCl and the peak current density reaches 5.1 mA/cm^2, representing a 59% increase in peak current relative to the pristine electrolyte. The peak separation narrows to 180 mV, reflecting significantly improved electron transfer kinetics. Interestingly, further increasing the Bi3+ concentration to 3.0 ppm results in a slight decrease in peak current density to 4.9 mA/cm^2 and a small negative shift in peak potential to -0.45 V, suggesting that the catalytic enhancement reaches an optimum at 2.0 ppm and diminishes marginally at higher concentrations, possibly due to competitive adsorption effects or partial blocking of active surface sites by excess bismuth species.

### 2.2 Rotating Disk Electrode Measurements

To quantitatively assess the electron transfer kinetics, rotating disk electrode (RDE) measurements were performed on the Cr(III)/Cr(II) system across the same range of Bi3+ concentrations. The Levich-Koutecky analysis of the RDE data at rotation rates from 400 to 2500 rpm yielded the standard heterogeneous electron transfer rate constants (k0) summarized in Table 1. The pristine electrolyte exhibits a k0 value of 2.1 × 10^-3 cm/s, which is consistent with literature values for the Cr(III)/Cr(II) couple in hydrochloric acid supporting electrolyte. With the addition of 0.5 ppm Bi3+, k0 increases to 2.7 × 10^-3 cm/s, corresponding to a 29% enhancement. At 1.0 ppm Bi3+, the rate constant reaches 3.4 × 10^-3 cm/s, and at 1.5 ppm it further increases to 4.2 × 10^-3 cm/s. The maximum rate constant of 4.8 × 10^-3 cm/s is obtained at 2.0 ppm Bi3+, representing a 2.3-fold acceleration of the electron transfer reaction relative to the baseline. This substantial kinetic enhancement translates to reduced activation overpotential during battery operation, particularly at elevated current densities. At 3.0 ppm Bi3+, the rate constant decreases slightly to 4.6 × 10^-3 cm/s, confirming the existence of an optimal concentration window.

The origin of the catalytic enhancement can be attributed to the formation of Cr(III)-Bi3+ surface complexes or adsorbed bismuth adatoms that facilitate the inner-sphere electron transfer pathway. Bismuth species adsorbed on the electrode surface may act as redox mediators, providing an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy compared to the direct electron transfer between Cr(III) and the carbon electrode. The optimal concentration at 2.0 ppm likely reflects a balance between maximizing the surface coverage of catalytically active bismuth species and avoiding excessive site blocking at higher concentrations.

## 3. Hydrogen Evolution Reaction Suppression Mechanism

### 3.1 HER Polarization Behavior

The suppression of parasitic hydrogen evolution represents the primary motivation for introducing Bi3+ into the ICRFB electrolyte. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was conducted on graphite felt electrodes in 1.0 M HCl with and without Bi3+ additive to evaluate the HER polarization characteristics. In the pristine electrolyte, the HER onset potential on the untreated graphite felt is approximately -0.38 V vs. Ag/AgCl, and the current density reaches 10 mA/cm^2 at an overpotential of -0.21 V, corresponding to the HER overpotential required to sustain this geometric current density.

Following the introduction of 2.0 ppm Bi3+ into the electrolyte, the HER polarization curve exhibits a marked shift toward more negative potentials. The HER onset potential shifts by approximately 65 mV in the negative direction, and the overpotential required to achieve 10 mA/cm^2 increases to -0.30 V, representing an 85 mV increase in HER overpotential compared to the baseline system. At 80 mA/cm^2, the HER overpotential in the Bi3+-containing electrolyte is 142 mV more negative than in the pristine electrolyte. This substantial increase in the kinetic barrier for hydrogen evolution is the direct consequence of bismuth underpotential deposition on the graphite felt surface, which creates a high-overpotential metallic adlayer that kinetically inhibits proton reduction.

The dependence of HER suppression on Bi3+ concentration was systematically investigated. At 0.5 ppm Bi3+, the HER overpotential at 10 mA/cm^2 increases by 25 mV relative to the baseline. As the Bi3+ concentration increases to 1.0 ppm and 1.5 ppm, the HER overpotential increases by 48 mV and 68 mV respectively. The maximum HER suppression is achieved at 2.0 ppm Bi3+, with the 85 mV overpotential increase noted above. At 3.0 ppm Bi3+, the HER overpotential increase is 82 mV, marginally lower than the optimum at 2.0 ppm, which mirrors the trend observed in the Cr(III)/Cr(II) kinetic measurements. This concentration dependence strongly suggests that the HER suppression and the chromium redox catalysis share a common mechanistic origin in the surface-adsorbed bismuth species.

### 3.2 In-Situ Hydrogen Evolution Monitoring

To quantify the parasitic hydrogen evolution under conditions representative of actual battery operation, in-situ hydrogen gas detection was performed during galvanostatic polarization of graphite felt electrodes at applied current densities of 40, 80, 120, and 160 mA/cm^2 in electrolytes containing 0.5 M CrCl3 and varying Bi3+ concentrations. The H2 evolution rate was determined by gas chromatographic analysis of the headspace gas collected during controlled-potential electrolysis.

In the absence of Bi3+, the H2 evolution rate at 80 mA/cm^2 corresponds to a current efficiency loss of approximately 13% to parasitic hydrogen evolution, meaning that 13% of the applied current is diverted to HER rather than the desired Cr(III) reduction. At 40 mA/cm^2, this parasitic loss is lower at 8%, while at 120 mA/cm^2 and 160 mA/cm^2 it increases to 17% and 21% respectively, reflecting the increasing overpotential-driven thermodynamic driving force for HER at higher current densities.

Upon addition of 2.0 ppm Bi3+, the H2 evolution rate at 80 mA/cm^2 decreases dramatically, reducing the parasitic current loss to 3.5%. This represents a 73% reduction in hydrogen evolution compared to the baseline system. At 40 mA/cm^2, the parasitic loss is further suppressed to 1.8%, while at 120 mA/cm^2 and 160 mA/cm^2 it remains at 5.2% and 7.8% respectively. The significantly lower parasitic losses at all current densities confirm that the Bi3+-derived surface adlayer provides effective kinetic inhibition of HER across the practical operating range of the ICRFB. The remaining residual hydrogen evolution at high current densities likely reflects incomplete coverage of the graphite felt surface by the bismuth adlayer, particularly in regions of the porous electrode where mass transport limitations may lead to locally enhanced proton concentrations.

### 3.3 Mechanistic Insights from Surface Analysis

Post-mortem analysis of the graphite felt electrodes by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) provides direct evidence for the formation of a bismuth-containing surface layer on electrodes cycled in Bi3+-enriched electrolyte. SEM images reveal sub-micron bismuth crystallites distributed across the carbon fiber surfaces of electrodes retrieved from cells operated with 2.0 ppm Bi3+, with an estimated surface coverage of 35-45% based on image analysis. No bismuth signals are detected on electrodes from the baseline cell, confirming that the bismuth deposition is additive-mediated rather than originating from impurities. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements further confirm the presence of metallic bismuth (Bi 4f7/2 binding energy of 157.0 eV) on the electrode surface, indicating that Bi3+ is reduced to Bi(0) during the underpotential deposition process. The combination of catalytically active metallic bismuth sites for Cr(III)/Cr(II) electron transfer and the high HER overpotential of the bismuth surface accounts for the dual functionality observed experimentally.

## 4. Single Cell Performance Evaluation

### 4.1 Coulombic and Energy Efficiency as Functions of Bi3+ Concentration

Single ICRFB cells were assembled using graphite felt electrodes, a Nafion 115 membrane, and electrolytes composed of 1.0 M FeCl2 in 2.0 M HCl for the positive half-cell and 1.0 M CrCl3 in 2.0 M HCl for the negative half-cell. Bi3+ was added exclusively to the negative electrolyte at concentrations of 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, and 3.0 ppm. All cell tests were conducted at ambient temperature (25 °C) with an electrolyte flow rate of 100 mL/min.

The coulombic efficiency (CE) and energy efficiency (EE) of the cells were measured at a fixed current density of 80 mA/cm^2 over 20 conditioning cycles, and the average steady-state values are reported. The baseline cell without Bi3+ additive achieves a CE of 87% and an EE of 72% at 80 mA/cm^2. The relatively low CE is a direct consequence of the parasitic HER competing with the Cr(III)/Cr(II) charge transfer reaction, while the EE is further penalized by the high activation overpotential associated with the sluggish chromium redox kinetics.

With the progressive addition of Bi3+, both CE and EE exhibit substantial improvements, as shown in Figure 3. At 0.5 ppm Bi3+, the CE increases to 90% and the EE rises to 76%. At 1.0 ppm Bi3+, the CE reaches 92% and the EE improves to 81%. Further increasing the Bi3+ concentration to 1.5 ppm yields a CE of 93.5% and an EE of 85%. The optimal performance is achieved at 2.0 ppm Bi3+, where the cell delivers a CE of 94% and an EE of 87%, representing improvements of 7 percentage points in CE and 15 percentage points in EE relative to the baseline. At 3.0 ppm Bi3+, a marginal decline is observed, with the CE decreasing slightly to 93.8% and the EE to 86.2%, consistent with the subtle catalytic inhibition observed in the RDE measurements at this elevated concentration.

The EE improvement of 15 percentage points at the optimal 2.0 ppm Bi3+ concentration arises from the combined effects of HER suppression, which increases the CE by reducing parasitic current losses, and the accelerated Cr(III)/Cr(II) kinetics, which reduces the voltage efficiency penalty associated with activation overpotential. The voltage efficiency at 80 mA/cm^2 increases from 83% for the baseline cell to 93% for the cell with 2.0 ppm Bi3+, accounting for the majority of the EE improvement beyond the CE gain.

### 4.2 Current Density Dependence of Cell Performance

To evaluate the performance characteristics across the practical operating window, the cell with 2.0 ppm Bi3+ was tested at current densities of 40, 80, 120, and 160 mA/cm^2, and the results were compared against the baseline cell at the same current densities. At 40 mA/cm^2, the baseline cell achieves a CE of 92% and an EE of 80%, while the Bi3+-containing cell delivers a CE of 96% and an EE of 90%. At 80 mA/cm^2, the respective values are 87% CE and 72% EE for the baseline versus 94% CE and 87% EE for the Bi3+-enhanced cell. As the current density is increased to 120 mA/cm^2, the baseline cell performance degrades to 80% CE and 62% EE, whereas the Bi3+-containing cell maintains 91% CE and 80% EE. At the highest current density of 160 mA/cm^2, the baseline cell exhibits only 72% CE and 51% EE, while the Bi3+-enhanced cell achieves 87% CE and 72% EE.

These results demonstrate that the Bi3+ additive provides performance benefits across the entire current density range, with particularly pronounced advantages at elevated current densities where both HER parasitic losses and kinetic overpotentials are most severe. The ability to maintain an EE of 72% at 160 mA/cm^2 in the Bi3+-containing cell, equivalent to the baseline EE at only 80 mA/cm^2, indicates that the additive effectively doubles the accessible power density range for comparable energy efficiency. This characteristic is of significant practical importance for reducing the stack cost of ICRFB systems, as higher operating current densities enable lower electrode area requirements for a given power rating.

### 4.3 Comparison with Alternative Additive Strategies

The performance of the Bi3+ additive approach was benchmarked against two alternative strategies: a gold nanoparticle (Au NP) modified graphite felt electrode and a lead ion (Pb2+) electrolyte additive at 5.0 ppm. The Au NP-modified electrode achieved a CE of 93% and an EE of 85% at 80 mA/cm^2, comparable to but slightly lower than the Bi3+ additive system. The Pb2+ additive system achieved 91% CE and 82% EE at the same current density. While both alternative approaches provide meaningful improvements over the baseline, the Bi3+ additive at 2.0 ppm offers a superior combination of CE and EE performance. Furthermore, the substantially lower concentration requirement of Bi3+ (2.0 ppm) compared to Pb2+ (5.0 ppm), combined with the avoidance of precious metal usage in the Au NP system, positions the Bi3+ strategy as a more cost-effective approach for large-scale deployment.

## 5. Long-Term Cycling Stability and Degradation Analysis

### 5.1 Extended Cycling Performance

The long-term durability of the Bi3+-enhanced ICRFB was assessed through 200 continuous charge-discharge cycles at 80 mA/cm^2 with a voltage window of 0.8-1.4 V. The baseline cell and the cell with 2.0 ppm Bi3+ were cycled under identical conditions for direct comparison. For the baseline cell, the initial discharge capacity of 280 mAh exhibits a steady decline over the cycling period, reaching 212 mAh after 200 cycles. This corresponds to a capacity retention of 75.7% and an average capacity fade rate of 0.12% per cycle. The capacity fade is accompanied by a progressive decrease in CE from the initial 87% to 81% by cycle 200, indicating accelerating parasitic HER losses as the cell degrades.

In contrast, the cell with 2.0 ppm Bi3+ demonstrates markedly improved cycling stability. The initial discharge capacity of 295 mAh gradually decreases to 277 mAh after 200 cycles, corresponding to a capacity retention of 93.9% and an average fade rate of only 0.03% per cycle. The CE remains stable at 94 ± 1% throughout the entire cycling period, and the EE decreases only modestly from 87% to 84% over 200 cycles. The higher initial capacity of the Bi3+-containing cell compared to the baseline (295 mAh versus 280 mAh) reflects the improved CE during the initial conditioning cycles, which results in more complete utilization of the active species.

### 5.2 Degradation Mechanism Analysis

Post-cycling electrolyte analysis reveals distinctly different degradation signatures for the baseline and Bi3+-enhanced cells. In the baseline cell, the negative electrolyte shows a significant decrease in Cr(III) concentration from the initial 1.0 M to 0.82 M after 200 cycles, accompanied by a pH increase from 0.3 to 0.6 due to proton consumption in the HER. Gas chromatographic analysis of the headspace confirms cumulative hydrogen evolution of 18.6 mmol over 200 cycles, corresponding to a total charge loss of 3578 mAh to parasitic HER. This hydrogen accumulation leads to progressive state-of-charge imbalance between the positive and negative half-cells, which is the primary driver of capacity fade in the baseline ICRFB.

For the Bi3+-enhanced cell, the Cr(III) concentration decreases only slightly from 1.0 M to 0.96 M over 200 cycles, and the pH remains essentially stable at 0.3 ± 0.05. The cumulative hydrogen evolution is reduced to 4.2 mmol, representing a 77% reduction in total parasitic hydrogen generation compared to the baseline. The residual capacity fade of 0.03% per cycle in the Bi3+-containing cell is attributed to a combination of minor membrane crossover of iron and chromium species and slow oxidative degradation of the graphite felt electrode, rather than HER-driven electrolyte imbalance. These results confirm that the Bi3+ additive effectively mitigates the dominant degradation mechanism in ICRFBs by suppressing parasitic hydrogen evolution and maintaining electrolyte compositional stability over extended cycling.

### 5.3 Bi3+ Consumption and Replenishment

The concentration of Bi3+ in the negative electrolyte was monitored periodically during the 200-cycle test by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES). The initial Bi3+ concentration of 2.0 ppm gradually decreases to 1.6 ppm after 200 cycles, corresponding to an average consumption rate of 0.002 ppm per cycle. This gradual depletion is attributed to the incorporation of bismuth into the electrode material and minor crossover to the positive half-cell through the membrane. The relatively slow consumption rate suggests that periodic replenishment of Bi3+ at intervals of several hundred cycles would be sufficient to maintain the additive concentration near the optimal level, adding minimal operational cost to the ICRFB system.

## 6. Discussion

The results presented in this study demonstrate that Bi3+ ions function as an effective dual-action electrolyte additive in ICRFBs, simultaneously addressing the two most critical limitations of this battery chemistry: parasitic hydrogen evolution and sluggish Cr(III)/Cr(II) redox kinetics. The mechanistic basis for both effects originates from the underpotential deposition of Bi3+ on the graphite felt electrode surface, where metallic bismuth adatoms create catalytically active sites that facilitate chromium redox electron transfer while presenting a high kinetic barrier for proton reduction.

The optimal Bi3+ concentration of 2.0 ppm identified in this work represents a compromise between maximizing surface coverage of catalytically active bismuth species and avoiding excessive site blocking at higher concentrations. The observation of slightly diminished performance at 3.0 ppm suggests that an excess of bismuth species may partially occlude the carbon surface, reducing the effective electrochemically active surface area available for the Cr(III)/Cr(II) reaction. This concentration-dependent behavior highlights the importance of precise additive dosage in achieving optimal performance.

The comparison between the ICRFB with Bi3+ additive and the all-vanadium RFB reveals competitive efficiency metrics. At 80 mA/cm^2, the Bi3+-enhanced ICRFB achieves a CE of 94% and an EE of 87%, which approach the typical performance of VRFB systems operated under comparable conditions. Given the substantially lower electrolyte cost of the ICRFB chemistry, these efficiency levels suggest that the cost-performance gap between ICRFBs and VRFBs can be significantly narrowed through effective additive strategies. However, it should be noted that the long-term cycling stability of the Bi3+-enhanced ICRFB, while substantially improved over the baseline, still falls short of the multi-thousand-cycle stability demonstrated by commercial VRFB systems, and further research is needed to address remaining degradation mechanisms including membrane crossover and electrode oxidation.

The HER suppression mechanism identified in this work differs fundamentally from approaches based on electrolyte pH modification or the use of organic complexing agents. Unlike pH adjustment, which inherently limits the thermodynamic driving force for chromium reduction, the Bi3+ additive operates through a purely kinetic mechanism that selectively inhibits HER without compromising the Cr(III)/Cr(II) redox potential. This selectivity is a critical advantage, as it enables the full utilization of the thermodynamic cell voltage while minimizing parasitic losses. The dynamic nature of the bismuth adlayer, which is continuously replenished from the electrolyte bulk, also provides superior durability compared to static electrode coatings that may degrade through delamination or dissolution over extended operation.

## 7. Conclusion

This study has established Bi3+ as a highly effective electrolyte additive for iron-chromium redox flow batteries, achieving simultaneous suppression of hydrogen evolution and acceleration of Cr(III)/Cr(II) redox kinetics through an underpotential deposition mechanism on the graphite felt electrode surface. Key findings include: (1) the addition of 2.0 ppm Bi3+ increases the Cr(III)/Cr(II) standard electron transfer rate constant by a factor of 2.3, from 2.1 × 10^-3 cm/s to 4.8 × 10^-3 cm/s; (2) the HER overpotential at 80 mA/cm^2 increases by 85 mV in the presence of 2.0 ppm Bi3+, reducing parasitic current losses from 13% to 3.5%; (3) single-cell testing at 80 mA/cm^2 demonstrates a coulombic efficiency of 94% and an energy efficiency of 87% with the optimal Bi3+ concentration, compared to 87% CE and 72% EE for the baseline cell; (4) long-term cycling over 200 cycles at 80 mA/cm^2 reveals a capacity fade rate of only 0.03% per cycle with Bi3+ additive, versus 0.12% per cycle for the baseline, corresponding to a fourfold improvement in cycling stability. These results position Bi3+ electrolyte additive as a promising, cost-effective strategy for advancing the commercial viability of ICRFB technology for grid-scale energy storage applications.

# Mitigation of Capacity Fade and Efficiency Loss in Zinc-Bromine Flow Batteries Through Separator Engineering and Complexing Agent Optimization

## Abstract

Zinc-bromine flow batteries (ZBFBs) have attracted considerable attention for stationary energy storage due to their high energy density, material abundance, and inherent safety. However, persistent challenges including bromine crossover, zinc dendrite proliferation, and incremental ohmic resistance continue to compromise long-term cycling stability. This study systematically investigates the interplay between bromine complexing agent concentration, porous separator architecture, and current density on the degradation behavior of laboratory-scale zinc-bromine cells. Electrochemical characterization was performed at current densities of 20, 40, 60, and 80 mA/cm² over 200 charge-discharge cycles using carbon felt electrodes and an aqueous electrolyte comprising zinc bromide and N-methyl-N-ethylpyrrolidinium bromide (MEP) as the bromine sequestration agent. Coulombic efficiency ranged from 85% at 80 mA/cm² to 92% at 20 mA/cm², with energy efficiency declining from 78% to 64% across the same operational window. Zinc utilization decreased from 72% to 55% as the cycle number increased from 50 to 200, accompanied by measurable acceleration in zinc dendrite growth rate from 0.8 μm/cycle to 2.4 μm/cycle. Bromine crossover rates of 0.12 mL/h were observed in cells employing unmodified porous separators without complexing agent, whereas incorporation of 0.5 M MEP reduced crossover to 0.03 mL/h. Microporous membranes with controlled pore size distribution (0.1–0.5 μm) exhibited area-specific resistance of 0.8 Ω·cm² compared to 1.4 Ω·cm² for conventional separators, while simultaneously suppressing dendrite penetration. These findings demonstrate that coordinated optimization of the bromine complexing agent and separator morphology can substantially retard degradation and extend operational lifetime in zinc-bromine flow battery systems.

## Introduction

The global transition toward renewable electricity generation has intensified demand for cost-effective, durable, and scalable energy storage technologies. Among the various electrochemical systems under investigation, zinc-bromine flow batteries occupy a distinctive niche by combining the high theoretical energy density of the zinc-bromine redox couple (430 Wh/kg) with the architectural flexibility of flow battery designs. The fundamental electrochemical reactions involve the deposition and dissolution of metallic zinc at the negative electrode during charge and discharge, respectively, alongside the reversible formation of polybromide species at the positive electrode. During charging, bromide ions are oxidized to elemental bromine (Br₂), which subsequently complexes with quaternary ammonium salts to form a dense, immiscible polybromide phase that prevents free bromine from migrating across the cell.

Despite these attractive features, the zinc-bromine chemistry suffers from several degradation pathways that collectively erode performance over extended cycling. The most prominent of these is zinc dendrite growth, wherein non-uniform zinc plating during charge leads to the formation of dendritic structures that can penetrate the separator and induce internal short circuits. Concurrently, molecular bromine and polybromide species exhibit finite solubility in the aqueous phase and can permeate through the separator toward the negative half-cell, a phenomenon termed bromine crossover. This crossover not only diminishes coulombic efficiency by consuming active material but also corrodes the zinc deposit, further destabilizing the negative electrode. Additionally, progressive separator clogging and electrolyte composition shifts contribute to rising ohmic resistance, which manifests as declining voltage efficiency and energy efficiency over time.

Previous efforts to address these challenges have explored electrode modifications, electrolyte additives, and separator replacement strategies. Carbon felt and graphite felt remain the predominant electrode materials due to their high surface area and compatibility with bromine environments. Bromine complexing agents such as MEP and N-methylpyrrolidinium bromide have been incorporated to sequester Br₂, while separators ranging from porous polyolefin to microporous composite structures have been evaluated for blocking bromine diffusion while permitting zinc ion transport. Nevertheless, a comprehensive understanding of how these variables interact across realistic operating conditions remains incomplete.

The present work addresses this gap by conducting a parametric study of zinc-bromine flow battery degradation. We examine the combined effects of current density (20–80 mA/cm²), MEP concentration (0–0.5 M), and separator type on coulombic efficiency, energy efficiency, zinc utilization, bromine crossover rate, ohmic resistance, and zinc dendrite growth rate. The results provide quantitative insights into the degradation hierarchy and identify actionable design targets for extending operational lifetime.

## Electrochemical Performance and Coulombic Efficiency Trends

The electrochemical performance of zinc-bromine cells was evaluated under galvanostatic charge-discharge conditions at current densities of 20, 40, 60, and 80 mA/cm², with a fixed electrolyte composition of 2 M ZnBr₂ and 0.3 M MEP at 25°C. Figure 1 presents the voltage profiles and efficiency metrics as a function of cycle number and current density. At 20 mA/cm², the cells achieved an average coulombic efficiency (CE) of 92% and an energy efficiency (EE) of 78% during the first 50 cycles. Increasing the current density to 40 mA/cm² yielded CE values of approximately 89% and EE of 72%, reflecting the greater polarization losses associated with higher electrochemical reaction rates. At 60 mA/cm², CE decreased further to 87% and EE to 68%, while the highest current density of 80 mA/cm² produced the lowest efficiencies observed in this study: CE of 85% and EE of 64%.

The reduction in coulombic efficiency at elevated current densities is attributable to the combined effects of enhanced bromine crossover and less uniform zinc plating. At higher charging rates, the rate of Br₂ generation at the positive electrode exceeds the complexation kinetics of MEP, resulting in a transient accumulation of free bromine in the electrolyte. This uncomplexed bromine exhibits higher mobility and diffuses more readily across the porous separator toward the negative electrode, where it chemically reacts with the plated zinc to form zinc bromide. This parasitic reaction directly consumes active material on both sides of the cell and represents an irreversible capacity loss mechanism. Simultaneously, the higher overpotentials associated with 80 mA/cm² operation promote localized nucleation at defect sites on the carbon felt surface, initiating dendrite growth earlier in the cycle life.

Voltage efficiency, calculated as the ratio of discharge voltage to charge voltage, followed a similar declining trend with increasing current density. The average charge voltage rose from 1.89 V at 20 mA/cm² to 2.12 V at 80 mA/cm², while the discharge voltage decreased from 1.71 V to 1.58 V over the same range. The resulting voltage polarization of 0.18 V at 20 mA/cm² expanded to 0.54 V at 80 mA/cm², contributing substantially to the observed EE decline. Ohmic resistance measurements conducted by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy after 100 cycles revealed area-specific resistance values of 0.9 Ω·cm² at 20 mA/cm² and 1.6 Ω·cm² at 80 mA/cm², confirming that elevated current densities accelerate resistive degradation pathways.

Zinc utilization, defined as the ratio of the practical discharge capacity to the theoretical zinc deposit capacity, also exhibited strong current density dependence. At 20 mA/cm², zinc utilization remained at 72% after 50 cycles but gradually decreased to 62% by cycle 200. In contrast, cells operated at 80 mA/cm² showed zinc utilization of only 55% at cycle 200, with the decline beginning within the first 50 cycles. The lower utilization at higher current densities reflects both incomplete zinc stripping during discharge—due to trapped metallic zinc within dendrite structures—and partial electrical isolation of zinc deposits from the carbon felt substrate as dendrites grow away from the electrode surface.

## Bromine Crossover and Complexing Agent Effects

Bromine crossover represents one of the most critical degradation mechanisms in zinc-bromine flow batteries because it simultaneously reduces coulombic efficiency and compromises the structural integrity of the negative electrode. To quantify the effect of bromine sequestration on crossover rates, cells were assembled with varying concentrations of MEP (0, 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5 M) and subjected to 100 charge-discharge cycles at a fixed current density of 40 mA/cm². The bromine crossover rate was determined by periodically sampling the negative electrolyte and measuring the concentration of bromine species that had permeated from the positive half-cell.

In the absence of MEP, the bromine crossover rate averaged 0.12 mL/h during the first 50 cycles and increased to 0.16 mL/h between cycles 100 and 200 as separator degradation created additional diffusion pathways. Under these conditions, the cell exhibited a coulombic efficiency of only 82% and visible corrosion of the zinc deposit was observed after 50 cycles. The addition of 0.1 M MEP reduced the crossover rate to 0.08 mL/h and improved CE to 86%, demonstrating that even modest complexing agent concentrations provide meaningful protection against bromine migration. At 0.3 M MEP, the crossover rate further decreased to 0.05 mL/h with a corresponding CE of 89%, which was maintained consistently through 200 cycles. The highest MEP concentration of 0.5 M achieved the lowest crossover rate of 0.03 mL/h and a CE of 91%, representing a 75% reduction in bromine crossover compared to the baseline electrolyte without complexing agent.

The mechanism underlying this improvement involves the formation of a polybromide-MEP complex phase that is immiscible with the aqueous electrolyte and effectively traps molecular bromine. When MEP is present at sufficient concentration, the equilibrium between free Br₂ and complexed polybromide shifts strongly toward the complexed form, reducing the thermodynamic activity of bromine in the aqueous phase. This reduced activity directly lowers the chemical potential gradient driving bromine diffusion across the separator. Additionally, the dense polybromide phase that accumulates in the positive electrolyte tank during charging acts as a secondary reservoir, releasing bromide ions back into the circuit during discharge while maintaining low free bromine concentrations throughout the cycle.

It is noteworthy, however, that excessive MEP concentrations can introduce secondary complications. At 0.5 M, the increased viscosity of the positive electrolyte raised the pumping power requirements by approximately 15% and marginally increased the ohmic resistance of the positive half-cell from 0.6 Ω·cm² to 0.7 Ω·cm². Furthermore, the higher concentration of quaternary ammonium cations slightly reduced zinc ion mobility, as evidenced by a 3% decrease in the limiting current density for zinc deposition. These trade-offs suggest that while 0.5 M MEP provides the best crossover suppression, 0.3 M may represent a more balanced compromise between bromine containment and overall cell performance when pumping energy penalties are considered.

Comparative experiments replacing MEP with N-methylpyrrolidinium bromide (NMP-Br) at equivalent molar concentrations revealed that MEP forms a more stable polybromide complex with lower aqueous solubility, explaining its superior performance in crossover reduction. Cells with NMP-Br at 0.3 M exhibited a crossover rate of 0.07 mL/h and CE of 87%, both inferior to the MEP-containing counterpart. The structural difference between the two complexing agents—the ethyl substituent on MEP versus the methyl group on NMP-Br—appears to enhance hydrophobicity and reduce the partition coefficient of the polybromide complex in the aqueous phase.

## Zinc Dendrite Growth and Ohmic Resistance Evolution

Zinc dendrite formation at the negative electrode constitutes a second major degradation pathway that limits the cycle life of zinc-bromine flow batteries. In this study, zinc dendrite growth was monitored using in-situ optical microscopy and post-mortem scanning electron microscopy after predefined cycle numbers. The zinc dendrite growth rate, defined as the average increase in dendrite height per cycle, was measured for cells operated at different current densities and separator configurations.

At a current density of 20 mA/cm² with a conventional porous separator, the initial dendrite growth rate during the first 50 cycles was 0.8 μm/cycle. This rate accelerated to 1.3 μm/cycle between cycles 50 and 100, and further to 1.8 μm/cycle between cycles 100 and 200. The accelerating growth rate reflects a positive feedback mechanism: as dendrites protrude from the electrode surface, the local current density at the dendrite tips increases due to geometric focusing effects, which in turn drives faster zinc deposition at these locations. At 40 mA/cm², the corresponding growth rates were 1.1 μm/cycle (0–50 cycles), 1.7 μm/cycle (50–100 cycles), and 2.4 μm/cycle (100–200 cycles). The highest current density of 80 mA/cm² produced dendrite growth rates exceeding 3.0 μm/cycle beyond cycle 100, leading to premature cell failure through separator penetration at approximately cycle 175.

The evolution of ohmic resistance showed a strong correlation with dendrite development. Initial area-specific resistance values measured by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy were similar across all test conditions, ranging from 0.7 to 0.8 Ω·cm² for fresh cells. After 100 cycles, however, significant divergence was observed. Cells at 20 mA/cm² exhibited ohmic resistance of 0.9 Ω·cm², while those at 80 mA/cm² reached 1.6 Ω·cm². Post-cycling analysis indicated that the resistance increase stems from multiple sources: physical displacement of electrolyte by dendrite structures at the separator interface, partial clogging of separator pores by detached zinc particles, and increased ionic path length as dendrites create non-uniform current distributions. The contribution of separator clogging was particularly pronounced in cells with conventional porous separators, where zinc particle accumulation within the pore network increased the effective tortuosity of the ion transport pathway.

To decouple the effects of current density and separator properties, additional experiments were conducted using microporous membranes with a narrow pore size distribution of 0.1–0.5 μm. When paired with operation at 40 mA/cm², the microporous membrane reduced the dendrite growth rate to 0.6 μm/cycle during the first 100 cycles—a 65% reduction compared to the conventional porous separator under identical conditions. The microporous membrane simultaneously maintained a stable ohmic resistance of 0.8 Ω·cm² through 200 cycles, whereas the porous separator showed resistance increasing from 0.8 Ω·cm² to 1.4 Ω·cm² over the same period. The superior performance of the microporous membrane is attributed to its smaller, more uniform pores, which physically obstruct dendrite penetration and reduce the probability of pore clogging by zinc particulates. Additionally, the more uniform pore structure supports a more homogeneous current distribution across the electrode surface, reducing the localized hotspots that nucleate dendrite formation.

## Separator Morphology and Long-Term Cycling Stability

The morphology and transport properties of the separator exert profound influence on the long-term cycling stability of zinc-bromine flow batteries by governing both bromine crossover and zinc dendrite penetration resistance. In this section, we compare the performance of three separator types over extended cycling: a conventional porous separator with average pore size of 5 μm and porosity of 55%, a microporous membrane with pore size of 0.1–0.5 μm and porosity of 42%, and a modified microporous membrane with asymmetric pore structure featuring 0.3 μm pores on the bromine-facing side and 0.1 μm pores on the zinc-facing side.

Long-term cycling tests were performed at 40 mA/cm² with 0.3 M MEP over 200 cycles. The conventional porous separator exhibited the most rapid performance degradation. Coulombic efficiency began at 89% but declined steadily to 81% by cycle 200, while energy efficiency dropped from 72% to 58%. Post-mortem examination revealed significant bromine staining on the zinc-facing side of the separator, confirming substantial crossover, together with visible dendrite penetration marks at multiple locations. The ohmic resistance increased from an initial 0.8 Ω·cm² to 1.4 Ω·cm², consistent with progressive pore clogging and electrolyte degradation.

The microporous membrane demonstrated substantially improved retention of efficiency metrics. Coulombic efficiency remained stable at 89–90% throughout 200 cycles, with energy efficiency declining only modestly from 72% to 68%. Bromine crossover was reduced to 0.04 mL/h, compared to 0.09 mL/h for the porous separator. The area-specific resistance remained nearly constant at 0.8 Ω·cm², indicating minimal pore clogging and stable electrolyte composition. Dendrite penetration was not observed, and the zinc dendrite growth rate was limited to 0.6 μm/cycle through the full test duration.

The asymmetric microporous membrane provided further incremental benefits by orienting the finer pore structure toward the zinc electrode. This configuration achieved CE of 90–91% and EE of 70% at cycle 200, with bromine crossover of 0.035 mL/h and stable ohmic resistance of 0.75 Ω·cm². The zinc-facing 0.1 μm pores offered enhanced resistance to dendrite penetration while the larger 0.3 μm bromine-facing pores allowed sufficient bromide ion transport without excessive flow resistance. The asymmetric design also facilitated more uniform zinc plating by distributing the current density more evenly across the electrode-separator interface.

These results demonstrate that separator engineering represents a high-leverage strategy for extending zinc-bromine flow battery lifetime. The 65% reduction in zinc dendrite growth rate and 56% reduction in bromine crossover achieved by substituting a microporous membrane for a conventional porous separator translate directly into improved efficiency retention and delayed failure modes. When combined with optimized MEP concentration of 0.3 M, the microporous membrane configuration enables over 200 cycles of stable operation at 40 mA/cm² without signs of imminent failure, suggesting a realistic path toward multi-year operational lifetime in appropriately scaled systems.

## Discussion

The experimental results reveal a complex, interdependent degradation landscape in zinc-bromine flow batteries where bromine crossover, zinc dendrite growth, and ohmic resistance evolution interact through positive and negative feedback loops. The hierarchy of degradation drivers shifts with operating conditions. At low current density (20 mA/cm²), bromine crossover dominates capacity fade because the slow zinc plating rate produces uniform deposits. Under these conditions, bromine crossover accounts for approximately 60% of total capacity loss, while zinc-related degradation contributes 40%. As current density increases to 80 mA/cm², zinc dendrite growth becomes the primary failure mode, responsible for over 70% of capacity fade.

The effectiveness of MEP as a bromine complexing agent depends critically on its concentration relative to the bromine generation rate. At 20 mA/cm², 0.3 M MEP is sufficient to complex virtually all generated bromine. At 80 mA/cm², however, the same concentration proves inadequate, resulting in transient free bromine accumulation. This concentration-rate interplay suggests that optimal MEP loading should scale with the designed current density. For high-power applications targeting 80 mA/cm², MEP concentrations of 0.5 M or higher may be necessary, albeit with associated viscosity and conductivity penalties.

The separator comparison highlights the dual function this component must fulfill: blocking bromine diffusion while permitting zinc ion transport and resisting dendrite penetration. Conventional porous separators fail because their large pore networks (average 5 μm) offer minimal resistance to bromine diffusion and provide pathways for dendrite propagation. Microporous membranes address both limitations through tortuous, size-selective transport pathways. The reduction in porosity from 55% to 42% does not significantly impede zinc ion transport, but smaller pores dramatically reduce the effective diffusion coefficient of bromine species.

The asymmetric microporous membrane tailors the pore structure to each side of the cell: 0.1 μm zinc-facing pores provide dendrite penetration resistance, while 0.3 μm bromine-facing pores reduce hydraulic resistance. This design philosophy offers a generalizable strategy for other flow battery chemistries facing asymmetric degradation challenges.

The ohmic resistance evolution couples electrochemical performance to system-level energy efficiency. The 75% increase in area-specific resistance for porous separators at 80 mA/cm² (from 0.8 to 1.4 Ω·cm²) directly reduces voltage efficiency. Microporous membranes that suppress dendrite growth to 0.6 μm/cycle simultaneously stabilize ohmic resistance, creating a dual benefit that compounds over extended cycling.

From a practical perspective, a zinc-bromine flow battery with 0.3 M MEP and a microporous membrane operated at 40 mA/cm² can achieve coulombic efficiency of 89%, energy efficiency of approximately 70%, zinc utilization above 65%, and stable ohmic resistance below 0.85 Ω·cm² over 200 cycles. Further improvements may be realized through electrode surface treatments that promote uniform zinc nucleation and advanced separator coatings providing chemical resistance to bromine attack.

## Conclusion

This study provides a systematic characterization of degradation mechanisms in zinc-bromine flow batteries and quantifies the effectiveness of engineering strategies for their mitigation. The principal findings are as follows.

Coulombic efficiency decreases monotonically with increasing current density, from 92% at 20 mA/cm² to 85% at 80 mA/cm², driven primarily by enhanced bromine crossover and less uniform zinc plating at higher rates. Energy efficiency follows a parallel trend, declining from 78% to 64%, with voltage polarization contributing substantially to the losses at elevated current densities.

Bromine crossover is effectively suppressed by the addition of MEP as a complexing agent. At 40 mA/cm², increasing MEP concentration from 0 to 0.5 M reduces the bromine crossover rate from 0.12 mL/h to 0.03 mL/h and improves coulombic efficiency from 82% to 91%. The optimal concentration of 0.3 M MEP represents a practical compromise, achieving 89% CE and 0.05 mL/h crossover while avoiding excessive viscosity increases.

Zinc dendrite growth accelerates with both cycle number and current density. Dendrite growth rates increased from 0.8 μm/cycle during the first 50 cycles at 20 mA/cm² to 2.4 μm/cycle during cycles 100–200 at 40 mA/cm², with premature failure occurring at approximately cycle 175 under 80 mA/cm² operation with conventional separators.

Separator morphology exerts decisive influence on both dendrite suppression and resistance stability. Microporous membranes with 0.1–0.5 μm pores reduced the zinc dendrite growth rate by 65% compared to conventional porous separators, maintained stable ohmic resistance of 0.8 Ω·cm² over 200 cycles, and reduced bromine crossover by 56%. Asymmetric microporous membranes provided further incremental improvements, achieving the most stable long-term performance observed in this study.

These findings establish that coordinated optimization of the bromine complexing agent concentration and separator architecture can substantially extend the operational lifetime of zinc-bromine flow batteries. The quantitative relationships between current density, efficiency metrics, degradation rates, and materials properties reported here provide design guidelines for the development of next-generation zinc-bromine systems targeting multi-year cycling stability in grid-scale energy storage applications.

# Degradation Mechanisms in Soluble Lead-Acid Flow Batteries: Coupled Effects of Lead Ion Speciation, Surface Morphology Evolution, and Positive Electrode Passivation

## Abstract

Soluble lead-acid flow batteries (SLAFBs) represent a promising candidate for grid-scale energy storage due to their unified electrolyte design and elimination of ion-selective membranes. However, capacity fading during extended cycling remains the primary barrier to commercial deployment. This study systematically investigates the coupled degradation mechanisms governing performance loss in SLAFBs operating with methanesulfonic acid (MSA) based electrolytes. Through controlled cycling experiments at current densities ranging from 10 to 40 mA/cm², Pb²⁺ concentrations of 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 mol/L, and H₂SO₄ concentrations of 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 mol/L, we quantified the individual and synergistic contributions of PbO₂ passivation, lead dendrite formation, electrolyte stratification, and surface roughness evolution to overall capacity degradation. The battery achieved an initial coulombic efficiency of 90% at 20 mA/cm² with 1.0 mol/L Pb²⁺ and 1.5 mol/L H₂SO₄, which declined to 78% after 200 cycles concurrently with a voltage efficiency drop from 82% to 71% and an energy efficiency decrease from 74% to 64%. Ex situ profilometry revealed that the lead electrode surface roughness (Ra) increased from 0.8 μm to 4.2 μm over 200 cycles, directly correlating with localized current density hotspots and non-uniform Pb deposition. Simultaneously, the positive electrode developed a PbO₂ passivation layer with thickness increasing from 15 nm at cycle 50 to 120 nm at cycle 200, contributing to a cumulative voltage loss of 340 mV. Mechanistic analysis indicates that local polarization at the positive electrode interface, driven by Pb²⁺ depletion and accumulated PbO₂ insulation, constitutes the rate-limiting degradation pathway, accounting for approximately 60% of the total capacity fade. These findings establish a quantitative framework for linking electrode morphology, interfacial chemistry, and electrochemical performance loss in membraneless flow battery systems.

## 1. Introduction

The increasing penetration of intermittent renewable energy sources into electrical grids has created an urgent demand for cost-effective, scalable energy storage technologies. Among the various electrochemical energy storage systems under investigation, soluble lead-acid flow batteries (SLAFBs) have attracted considerable research attention owing to their unique architecture featuring a single electrolyte stream containing dissolved lead species, thereby eliminating the need for expensive ion-exchange membranes. In the SLAFB chemistry, lead ions exist as Pb²⁺ in the methanesulfonic acid (MSA) or fluoboric acid supporting electrolyte, undergoing deposition as metallic lead (Pb⁰) at the negative electrode during charge and as lead dioxide (PbO₂) at the positive electrode.

Despite these architectural advantages, SLAFBs suffer from progressive capacity degradation that limits their cycle life to typically fewer than 300 cycles. Multiple degradation pathways contribute to this decline. At the negative electrode, non-uniform Pb deposition from the Pb²⁺ electrolyte leads to dendritic growth that can cause internal short circuits. More insidiously, the evolving surface morphology characterized by increasing surface roughness creates localized electric field enhancements that promote further non-uniform deposition, establishing a positive feedback loop of morphological degradation.

At the positive electrode, the formation of a partially insulating PbO₂ layer on the electrode surface—a phenomenon termed PbO₂ passivation—progressively blocks active surface sites and increases interfacial resistance. The PbO₂ layer formed during charge does not fully dissolve during discharge, particularly at lower acid concentrations and higher current densities, leading to cumulative layer growth and associated voltage loss. This positive electrode passivation is further complicated by local polarization effects arising from Pb²⁺ concentration gradients, which drive the formation of non-stoichiometric lead oxide phases with inferior electrical conductivity.

Additionally, density-driven electrolyte stratification creates non-uniform reactant distribution, leading to local polarization and uneven current distribution. The interplay between Pb deposition morphology, PbO₂ passivation, and electrolyte stratification creates a complex degradation landscape that has not been fully quantified. This work decouples and quantifies these pathways by systematically varying current density (10–40 mA/cm²), Pb²⁺ concentration (0.5–1.5 mol/L), and H₂SO₄ concentration (1.0–2.0 mol/L), establishing quantitative relationships between operating conditions, electrode surface evolution, and performance metrics.

## 2. Experimental Methods

Electrochemical experiments were conducted in a laboratory-scale flow cell with an active electrode area of 10 cm² and an inter-electrode gap of 5 mm. The electrolyte consisted of methanesulfonic acid (MSA) as the supporting electrolyte, with lead methanesulfonate at Pb²⁺ concentrations of 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 mol/L, and H₂SO₄ at 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 mol/L. The electrolyte flow rate was maintained at 100 mL/min. Lead foil electrodes (99.99% purity, 1.0 mm thickness) served as substrates. Cycling tests were performed galvanostatically at 10, 20, 30, and 40 mA/cm² between voltage limits of 1.4 V and 2.4 V. Coulombic efficiency, voltage efficiency, and energy efficiency were calculated from charge-discharge data. Capacity retention was tracked as the ratio of discharge capacity at cycle N to that at cycle 5. Surface roughness (Ra) was measured by contact profilometry. PbO₂ layer thickness was determined from cross-sectional SEM-EDS. EIS was conducted from 100 kHz to 10 mHz to monitor interfacial resistance evolution.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Electrochemical Performance and Efficiency Trends

At the reference condition of 20 mA/cm², 1.0 mol/L Pb²⁺, and 1.5 mol/L H₂SO₄, the battery delivered an initial coulombic efficiency of 90%, a voltage efficiency of 82%, and an energy efficiency of 74%. The coulombic efficiency remained above 88% for the first 50 cycles but then declined steadily, reaching 85% at cycle 100 and 78% at cycle 200. The voltage efficiency exhibited a more pronounced decay, decreasing from 82% at cycle 1 to 71% at cycle 200, translating into an energy efficiency decline from 74% to 64%.

The effect of current density was investigated at constant 1.0 mol/L Pb²⁺ and 1.5 mol/L H₂SO₄. At 10 mA/cm², the initial coulombic efficiency was 92%, the voltage efficiency was 85%, and the energy efficiency was 78%. At 30 mA/cm², these values decreased to 87%, 76%, and 66% respectively. At 40 mA/cm², the coulombic efficiency dropped to 83%, the voltage efficiency to 70%, and the energy efficiency to 58%. This monotonic decrease reflects greater overpotential losses, more non-uniform current distribution, and enhanced PbO₂ passivation at higher reaction rates.

Electrolyte composition also significantly influenced performance. Reducing Pb²⁺ concentration from 1.0 to 0.5 mol/L at 20 mA/cm² decreased the initial coulombic efficiency from 90% to 85% and the voltage efficiency from 82% to 77%, yielding an energy efficiency of 70%. Increasing Pb²⁺ to 1.5 mol/L improved the coulombic efficiency to 92% and the voltage efficiency to 84%, with energy efficiency of 77%. The H₂SO₄ concentration exhibited a non-monotonic effect: at 1.0 mol/L, the initial coulombic efficiency was 86%, which increased to 90% at 1.5 mol/L, but then decreased slightly to 89% at 2.0 mol/L, suggesting that moderate acid concentrations enhance Pb²⁺ stability while excessively high acid promotes hydrogen evolution.

### 3.2 Capacity Retention and Degradation Rates

At the reference condition, the discharge capacity decreased from 18.5 mAh/cm² to 14.8 mAh/cm² after 200 cycles, corresponding to a capacity retention of 80% and an average degradation rate of 0.10% per cycle. The fade accelerated during later stages: retention was 94% at cycle 50, 88% at cycle 100, and 80% at cycle 200, indicating the degradation rate increased from 0.06% per cycle during the first 50 cycles to 0.16% per cycle during the final 50 cycles.

Higher current densities substantially exacerbated fading. At 30 mA/cm², capacity retention dropped to 72% after 200 cycles, while at 40 mA/cm² it reached only 63%. At 10 mA/cm², retention improved to 88%. Lower Pb²⁺ concentrations also accelerated degradation: at 0.5 mol/L and 20 mA/cm², retention fell to 74%, compared to 80% at 1.0 mol/L and 85% at 1.5 mol/L. The effect of H₂SO₄ concentration was comparatively modest, with retention values of 79%, 80%, and 81% at 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 mol/L respectively after 200 cycles at 20 mA/cm².

The accelerated fade at higher current densities and lower Pb²⁺ concentrations is consistent with a mechanism dominated by positive electrode passivation. Under these conditions, the rate of PbO₂ formation during charge exceeds its dissolution rate during discharge, leading to cumulative passivation layer growth and progressive voltage loss.

### 3.3 Negative Electrode Surface Evolution and Lead Deposition Morphology

Contact profilometry revealed that the arithmetic mean surface roughness (Ra) increased from 0.8 μm on pristine lead foil to 1.5 μm after 50 cycles, 2.8 μm after 100 cycles, and 4.2 μm after 200 cycles at the reference condition. This progressive roughening was accompanied by nodular Pb deposition patterns that became increasingly pronounced.

The surface roughness evolution depended strongly on current density. At 10 mA/cm², Ra reached only 2.4 μm after 200 cycles, whereas at 30 mA/cm² it increased to 5.8 μm. At 40 mA/cm², severe dendritic growth occurred after 150 cycles, with localized roughness exceeding 8 μm. Cross-sectional imaging confirmed dendrites grew preferentially at surface scratches or defects, indicating that surface inhomogeneity acts as a nucleation catalyst for non-uniform lead plating.

The relationship between surface roughness and electrochemical performance was quantified through EIS. The charge-transfer resistance at the negative electrode increased from 0.15 Ω·cm² on the pristine electrode to 0.28 Ω·cm² after 200 cycles at 20 mA/cm². This 87% increase correlates with reduced active surface area for uniform deposition, as growing roughness creates regions electrically shielded from the bulk electrolyte. The increasing surface area paradoxically promotes greater non-uniformity because enhanced local electric fields at sharp features drive preferential deposition at protrusions, amplifying morphological instability.

### 3.4 Positive Electrode Passivation and PbO₂ Layer Growth

Cross-sectional SEM-EDS revealed a compact PbO₂ layer with initial thickness of approximately 15 nm after 50 cycles at the reference condition, growing to 45 nm after 100 cycles and 120 nm after 200 cycles, following an approximately linear trend after an initial nucleation period. The layer accumulated preferentially at regions of highest local current density, creating patchy, heterogeneous coverage.

The PbO₂ passivation layer thickness showed strong dependence on operating conditions. At 10 mA/cm², the layer reached only 70 nm after 200 cycles, while at 30 mA/cm² it grew to 180 nm. At 40 mA/cm², the thickness exceeded 250 nm after 200 cycles. The growth rate was also sensitive to H₂SO₄ concentration: at 1.0 mol/L H₂SO₄, the layer reached 150 nm after 200 cycles at 20 mA/cm², compared to 120 nm at 1.5 mol/L and 95 nm at 2.0 mol/L. This inverse relationship suggests higher proton availability promotes more complete PbO₂ dissolution during discharge, mitigating cumulative growth.

The PbO₂ passivation layer contributed substantially to cell resistance and voltage loss. The positive electrode interfacial resistance increased from 0.22 Ω·cm² at cycle 5 to 0.61 Ω·cm² at cycle 200, with the PbO₂ layer accounting for approximately 65% of this increase. The cumulative voltage loss attributed to positive electrode degradation reached 340 mV at cycle 200, representing roughly 55% of the total cell voltage increase. Of this 340 mV, approximately 200 mV was associated with ohmic drop across the PbO₂ layer itself, while 140 mV arose from increased charge-transfer overpotential due to reduced active surface area.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Coupling Between Negative Electrode Morphology and Positive Electrode Passivation

The two primary degradation mechanisms are coupled through the overall cell electrochemistry and electrolyte composition. As negative electrode surface roughness increases, non-uniform current distribution creates temporal fluctuations in local Pb²⁺ concentration near the electrode surface. These fluctuations propagate through the shared electrolyte and affect Pb²⁺ availability at the positive electrode, where local depletion exacerbates formation of poorly conducting oxide phases that accelerate passivation.

This coupling explains the experimentally observed acceleration of capacity fade during later stages. During the initial 50 cycles, surface roughness increase from 0.8 μm to 1.5 μm is modest, and the PbO₂ layer remains thin (15 nm), resulting in limited interaction and a slow degradation rate of 0.06% per cycle. However, as roughness exceeds 2 μm after 100 cycles, the fluctuation amplitude in local Pb²⁺ concentration becomes significant, driving faster PbO₂ accumulation and creating a positive feedback loop. During cycles 150–200, this coupled degradation yields an accelerated fade rate of 0.16% per cycle.

### 4.2 Relative Contributions of Degradation Pathways to Capacity Fade

The total capacity loss of 20% after 200 cycles at the reference condition was deconvoluted into constituent contributions. Local polarization at the positive electrode interface, encompassing ohmic voltage loss across the PbO₂ layer and associated charge-transfer overpotential increase, accounts for approximately 60% of total capacity fade. This dominance arises because PbO₂ layer growth is only partially reversible and creates a cumulative, non-recoverable increase in cell resistance.

Negative electrode morphology degradation contributes approximately 25% of total capacity fade. While Pb deposition remains fundamentally reversible, practical reversibility is compromised by growing surface irregularity, which leads to electrically isolated deposits and reduced coulombic efficiency. Electrolyte stratification accounts for the remaining 15%, estimated by comparing horizontal and vertical cell orientations, with the vertical orientation showing approximately 3% additional capacity loss after 200 cycles due to gravity-driven stratification.

The relative contributions shift with operating conditions. At 40 mA/cm², the passivation contribution increases to approximately 70% due to accelerated PbO₂ growth. At 10 mA/cm², the passivation contribution decreases to 45%, while stratification increases to 25% because longer residence times allow greater concentration gradient development. At 0.5 mol/L Pb²⁺, stratification contribution increases to 22% because lower bulk concentration makes the system more sensitive to depletion-driven gradients.

### 4.3 Mechanistic Origins of Local Polarization and Voltage Loss

The total voltage loss of approximately 620 mV between cycle 5 and cycle 200 at the reference condition comprises roughly 280 mV from ohmic contributions, 200 mV from activation overpotential (primarily at the positive electrode), and 140 mV from concentration overpotential. The dominance of positive electrode-related voltage loss can be attributed to the specific electrochemistry of the PbO₂/Pb²⁺ couple. When the PbO₂ passivation layer forms, it blocks surface sites required for the adsorption of intermediate Pb²⁺ species, thereby increasing activation overpotential. Simultaneously, the semiconducting PbO₂ layer introduces an ohmic voltage drop that scales linearly with layer thickness. At 120 nm thickness, the calculated ohmic contribution is approximately 200 mV.

Local polarization at the negative electrode plays a critical role in initiating morphological degradation. Current density enhancement at surface asperities creates localized Pb²⁺ depletion regions. The diffusion-limited current density for Pb²⁺ reduction at 1.0 mol/L is approximately 85 mA/cm², meaning the system operates at 24% of the limiting current at 20 mA/cm². However, at protrusions where local current density is enhanced 2–3 fold, the local Pb²⁺ concentration drops to 30–40% of bulk, creating conditions for unstable dendritic growth.

### 4.4 Implications for Electrolyte Design and Operating Protocols

The strong dependence of PbO₂ passivation rate on H₂SO₄ concentration suggests maintaining acid concentrations at or above 1.5 mol/L is beneficial. However, hydrogen evolution at high acid concentrations limits the practical upper bound, as evidenced by the coulombic efficiency decrease from 90% to 89% when increasing H₂SO₄ from 1.5 to 2.0 mol/L. An optimized concentration of 1.5–1.8 mol/L offers the best compromise.

Higher Pb²⁺ concentrations improve cycle life: capacity retention improves from 74% to 85% when increasing Pb²⁺ from 0.5 to 1.5 mol/L at 20 mA/cm², suggesting concentrations above 1.0 mol/L are necessary for cycle life targets exceeding 500 cycles. Current density selection is particularly impactful: reducing from 40 to 20 mA/cm² improves capacity retention from 63% to 80%, though at lower power density. For applications prioritizing cycle life, operation at or below 20 mA/cm² is recommended.

The surface roughness data suggest that electrode pretreatment to achieve ultra-smooth initial surfaces (Ra < 0.5 μm) could delay morphological degradation by reducing nucleation sites for non-uniform deposition. Additionally, periodic current reversal or pulse charging protocols merit investigation as methods for dissolving incipient PbO₂ layers before they reach critical thicknesses.

## 5. Conclusion

This study provided comprehensive, quantitative characterization of degradation mechanisms in soluble lead-acid flow batteries. Four primary pathways were identified and quantified: positive electrode PbO₂ passivation, negative electrode surface roughness evolution and non-uniform Pb deposition, and electrolyte stratification.

The key findings are: First, PbO₂ passivation at the positive electrode is the dominant degradation mechanism, accounting for approximately 60% of total capacity fade and producing a cumulative voltage loss of 340 mV after 200 cycles through a passivation layer growing from 15 nm to 120 nm. Second, negative electrode surface roughness increases from Ra of 0.8 μm to 4.2 μm over 200 cycles at 20 mA/cm², creating localized current density enhancements that promote non-uniform lead plating and contribute approximately 25% of capacity loss. Third, these mechanisms are coupled through Pb²⁺ concentration fluctuations, creating a positive feedback loop that accelerates degradation from 0.06% per cycle initially to 0.16% per cycle after 150 cycles. Fourth, electrochemical efficiencies decline in parallel: coulombic efficiency decreases from 90% to 78%, voltage efficiency from 82% to 71%, and energy efficiency from 74% to 64% over 200 cycles.

The mechanistic framework suggests extending SLAFB cycle life requires optimized electrolyte composition (1.0–1.5 mol/L Pb²⁺, 1.5–1.8 mol/L H₂SO₄), moderate current density operation (≤20 mA/cm²), electrode surface engineering for minimal initial roughness, and potentially advanced charging protocols to periodically regenerate the positive electrode surface. Future work should focus on real-time monitoring of PbO₂ layer thickness and surface roughness during operation, and development of electrolyte additives that suppress passivation without compromising coulombic efficiency.

# Performance and Degradation Mechanisms in Aqueous Organic Redox Flow Batteries: A Comparative Study of Quinone, TEMPO, and Viologen Chemistries

## Abstract

Aqueous organic redox flow batteries (ORFBs) have attracted considerable attention as scalable electrochemical energy storage systems owing to the structural tunability and earth-abundant composition of organic active species. This study presents a systematic investigation of three representative chemistries—anthraquinone, (2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidin-1-yl)oxyl (TEMPO), and methyl viologen—operated under varied pH conditions and current densities in a single-cell flow battery configuration. Anthraquinone served as the negolyte active species in aqueous KOH electrolyte at pH 12 and 14, while TEMPO and methyl viologen were employed as posolyte species at pH 7, 8, and 9. The anion-exchange membrane (AEM) and Nafion 117 were compared as separator materials. At a current density of 100 mA/cm², the anthraquinone-TEMPO full cell achieved a coulombic efficiency (CE) of 97%, a voltage efficiency (VE) of 85%, and an energy efficiency (EE) of 82% over 200 cycles. Capacity fade analysis revealed that the quinone system exhibited a fade rate of 0.08% per cycle, predominantly attributed to dimerization of reduced anthrahydroquinone species at pH 14. The methyl viologen system demonstrated superior CE of 99% at 60 mA/cm² but suffered from capacity imbalance due to differential crossover rates between the negolyte and posolyte compartments. Membrane selectivity measurements indicated that the AEM achieved an ion selectivity of 94% at pH 9, compared to 89% for Nafion 117 under identical conditions. Extended cycling at 40 mA/cm² for 500 cycles showed that TEMPO underwent negligible structural degradation, whereas anthraquinone experienced progressive capacity loss correlated with increased dimer formation detected by in situ UV-Vis spectroscopy. These findings elucidate the trade-offs among redox potential, stability, and transport properties across the three active species classes, and provide quantitative benchmarks for rational electrolyte and membrane selection in next-generation ORFB systems.

## 1. Introduction

The transition toward intermittent renewable energy sources has intensified the demand for large-scale electrochemical energy storage technologies that offer long cycle life, independent power-energy scaling, and minimal reliance on critical raw materials. Among the various battery architectures under investigation, redox flow batteries (RFBs) distinguish themselves through the physical separation of energy-containing electrolytes from the power-converting electrochemical stack, thereby enabling flexible system design and straightforward capacity augmentation. While all-vanadium RFBs have achieved commercial deployment, the high cost and geographic concentration of vanadium resources have motivated the exploration of organic-based alternatives that utilize molecularly engineered redox-active compounds dissolved in aqueous or non-aqueous solvents.

Organic redox flow batteries employ active species derived from sustainable feedstocks such as quinones, nitroxyl radicals, and bipyridinium salts, each offering distinct electrochemical signatures and degradation pathways. Anthraquinone and its derivatives have emerged as particularly promising negolyte materials due to their two-electron reduction chemistry at moderately negative potentials, with the redox potential shifting systematically from approximately -0.52 V versus SHE at pH 7 to -0.71 V versus SHE at pH 14 as the solution alkalinity increases. On the posolyte side, TEMPO and related nitroxyl radicals operate via a single-electron oxidation with exceptional reaction reversibility, exhibiting redox potentials near +0.65 V versus SHE in neutral aqueous media. Methyl viologen, a bipyridinium-based compound, offers an intermediate potential window with a two-electron redox cascade that has proven effective in asymmetric cell configurations. Despite these promising attributes, the long-term stability of ORFBs is compromised by several interrelated degradation mechanisms including organic crossover through the separator membrane, dimerization and subsequent precipitation of reduced species, and capacity imbalance arising from asymmetric decay rates between the negolyte and posolyte.

Previous investigations have examined individual active species or specific membrane-electrolyte pairings; however, a comprehensive comparison under standardized operating conditions remains limited. Furthermore, the sensitivity of quinone redox kinetics to pH, the influence of membrane chemistry on organic species permeation, and the extent to which dimerization contributes to capacity fade across different molecular classes require systematic quantification. This study addresses these gaps by evaluating anthraquinone, TEMPO, and methyl viologen in a unified experimental framework, with particular emphasis on pH-dependent performance, membrane selectivity, and degradation mode identification over extended galvanostatic cycling.

## 2. Experimental System and Operating Conditions

All electrochemical measurements were conducted in a single-cell flow battery with an active electrode area of 5 cm². Carbon felt electrodes (SGL Carbon, GFD 4.6 EA) were employed as the standard electrode material, with graphite felt used in select comparative experiments. The electrolyte reservoirs contained 100 mL of aqueous solution on each side, with active species concentrations of 0.1 M unless otherwise specified. The negolyte comprised anthraquinone dissolved in aqueous KOH at either pH 12 or pH 14, while the posolyte contained either TEMPO or methyl viologen in aqueous NaCl buffered to pH 7, 8, or 9. A commercial anion-exchange membrane (AEM, Fumasep FAA-3-PK-75) was employed as the primary separator, with Nafion 117 tested in parallel experiments for direct comparison. The electrolyte flow rate was maintained at 60 mL/min throughout all experiments to ensure adequate mass transport. Galvanostatic cycling was performed at current densities of 20, 40, 60, and 100 mA/cm², with voltage cutoff limits of 0.3 V (lower) and 1.5 V (upper). In situ UV-Vis spectroscopy was implemented to monitor the formation of dimerized species and track active material concentration changes in real time during extended cycling protocols.

## 3. Electrochemical Performance and pH Dependence

### 3.1 Anthraquinone Negolyte Behavior

The electrochemical behavior of anthraquinone in alkaline media exhibited strong pH dependence, reflecting the proton-coupled electron transfer mechanism governing the reduction of the quinone moiety to the hydroquinone state. At pH 12 and a current density of 100 mA/cm², the anthraquinone half-cell demonstrated a CE of 95% and stable charge-discharge profiles over the initial 100 cycles. Increasing the alkalinity to pH 14 shifted the redox potential by 60 mV in the negative direction, consistent with the Nernst equation for a two-proton, two-electron process, but simultaneously increased the capacity fade rate from 0.05% per cycle to 0.08% per cycle. This accelerated degradation at elevated pH was correlated with enhanced dimerization of the anthrahydroquinone intermediate, as evidenced by the appearance of a characteristic absorption band at 380 nm in the UV-Vis spectrum. At pH 14 and 40 mA/cm², the dimerization extent reached approximately 12% of the total active species after 300 cycles, compared to 6% at pH 12 under otherwise identical conditions.

The charge-discharge voltage profiles at 100 mA/cm² revealed a discharge plateau at approximately -0.69 V versus SHE for the pH 14 electrolyte, with an average polarization of 120 mV attributable to the combined ohmic and kinetic overpotentials. Lowering the current density to 20 mA/cm² reduced this polarization to 45 mV and improved the VE from 78% to 88%, though the CE remained unchanged within experimental uncertainty. The EE consequently increased from 74% at 100 mA/cm² to 84% at 20 mA/cm² for the pH 12 anthraquinone system, establishing a clear trade-off between power density and round-trip efficiency.

### 3.2 TEMPO Posolyte Performance

TEMPO exhibited remarkable electrochemical reversibility across all tested pH conditions, with the highest CE observed at pH 8 where the radical cation formed upon oxidation demonstrated optimal stability in the aqueous medium. At a current density of 100 mA/cm² and pH 8, the TEMPO half-cell achieved a CE of 98%, a VE of 89%, and an EE of 87%. The redox potential of the TEMPO/TEMPO+ couple remained relatively insensitive to pH variation, shifting by only 15 mV between pH 7 and pH 9, consistent with the single-electron nature of the redox reaction that does not involve proton transfer. This pH independence represents a significant practical advantage when pairing TEMPO with pH-sensitive negolyte species, as it minimizes potential mismatch across the operating pH window.

Galvanostatic cycling at 60 mA/cm² over 200 cycles revealed negligible capacity fade for TEMPO, with the discharge capacity retention exceeding 99.5% throughout the test duration. UV-Vis monitoring confirmed the absence of significant dimerization or decomposition products, with the characteristic absorption peak at 460 nm maintaining constant intensity. The primary loss mechanism for TEMPO was identified as slow crossover through the AEM, with a measured permeation rate of 2.1 x 10⁻⁷ cm²/s, which contributed to a gradual state-of-charge (SOC) imbalance between the negolyte and posolyte reservoirs. At pH 9, this crossover rate increased modestly to 2.4 x 10⁻⁷ cm²/s, likely due to changes in the ionization state of the membrane functional groups affecting pore structure and transport pathways.

### 3.3 Methyl Viologen Characteristics

Methyl viologen displayed distinct electrochemical behavior characterized by two sequential one-electron redox transitions. The first reduction at approximately -0.45 V versus SHE generated the stable radical cation (MV+), while the second reduction at -0.76 V versus SHE produced the neutral species (MV⁰) with significantly lower aqueous solubility. Operating at pH 7 and 60 mA/cm², the methyl viologen half-cell achieved a CE of 99% for the first reduction step, which decreased to 95% when cycling through both reduction states due to the onset of precipitation of the fully reduced MV⁰ species. The VE at 60 mA/cm² was 82% for the single-electron process, declining to 71% for the two-electron process as the increased overpotential associated with the second reduction step contributed additional kinetic losses.

Extended cycling of methyl viologen at 40 mA/cm² and pH 8 demonstrated a capacity fade rate of 0.04% per cycle, which was lower than that observed for anthraquinone at comparable pH but higher than the essentially negligible fade of TEMPO. The degradation mechanism differed qualitatively from that of anthraquinone, with methyl viologen exhibiting minimal dimerization but significant capacity imbalance due to its relatively high membrane permeation rate of 4.8 x 10⁻⁷ cm²/s through the AEM—more than double the TEMPO crossover rate under identical conditions. This elevated crossover rate was attributed to the doubly charged nature of the oxidized methyl viologen dication, which interacts strongly with the fixed cationic groups in the AEM and facilitates transport through Donnan exclusion failure.

## 4. Full Cell Configuration and Comparative Assessment

### 4.1 Anthraquinone-TEMPO Cell

The full cell pairing anthraquinone (pH 12) with TEMPO (pH 8) across the AEM yielded an open-circuit voltage of 1.34 V and achieved a CE of 97%, a VE of 85%, and an EE of 82% at 100 mA/cm² over 200 cycles. The capacity fade rate of 0.06% per cycle was intermediate between the individual half-cell fade rates, suggesting that the full cell degradation was dominated by anthraquinone dimerization rather than TEMPO crossover losses. Post-cycling analysis of the negolyte by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) confirmed the presence of dimerized anthraquinone species at a concentration corresponding to approximately 9% of the initial active material, while the posolyte TEMPO concentration remained within 2% of its starting value.

Reducing the current density to 40 mA/cm² improved the EE to 88% due to decreased ohmic and activation overpotentials, but extended cycling at this lower rate for 500 cycles revealed an accelerating capacity fade mechanism. Beyond cycle 300, the fade rate increased from 0.06% to 0.11% per cycle, concomitant with a measurable increase in the UV-Vis absorption at 380 nm indicating progressive accumulation of dimerized quinone species. This nonlinear degradation profile suggests that dimerization may proceed through an autocatalytic pathway in which the presence of existing dimers promotes further dimer formation, or alternatively, that the increasing surface area of precipitated material provides nucleation sites for additional dimer accumulation.

The SOC imbalance between the negolyte and posolyte sides increased gradually during cycling, reaching 8% after 200 cycles at 100 mA/cm² and 14% after 500 cycles at 40 mA/cm². This imbalance was primarily driven by the differential crossover rates—TEMPO crossing at 2.1 x 10⁻⁷ cm²/s compared to anthraquinone at 0.7 x 10⁻⁷ cm²/s—creating an asymmetric loss that reduced the utilizable capacity of the full cell. Periodic remixing of the electrolytes restored approximately 85% of the lost capacity, confirming that permanent active material degradation accounted for only a fraction of the observed capacity loss.

### 4.2 Anthraquinone-Methyl Viologen Cell

The alternative full cell configuration pairing anthraquinone at pH 14 with methyl viologen at pH 7 delivered an open-circuit voltage of 1.05 V, lower than the anthraquinone-TEMPO pairing due to the less positive redox potential of methyl viologen relative to TEMPO. At 60 mA/cm², this cell achieved a CE of 95%, a VE of 80%, and an EE of 76% over 150 cycles. The capacity fade rate of 0.09% per cycle exceeded that of the anthraquinone-TEMPO system, driven primarily by the high crossover rate of methyl viologen and the second-step precipitation of MV⁰ species. After 150 cycles, HPLC analysis detected only 3% dimerized anthraquinone but a 15% reduction in methyl viologen concentration, with the lost material recovered in the opposing electrolyte reservoir, unequivocally establishing crossover as the dominant degradation pathway in this cell configuration.

State-of-charge imbalance developed more rapidly in the anthraquinone-methyl viologen cell compared to the anthraquinone-TEMPO cell, reaching 12% after just 100 cycles at 60 mA/cm². The membrane selectivity proved insufficient to prevent significant viologen transport, with the measured effective permeability of 4.8 x 10⁻⁷ cm²/s representing the highest crossover rate among the three active species investigated. This elevated permeation rate was consistently observed across pH 7, 8, and 9 conditions, indicating that the structural characteristics of the methyl viologen dication—specifically its planar geometry and delocalized positive charge—predominated over membrane ionization effects in determining transport behavior.

### 4.3 Membrane Comparison: AEM versus Nafion 117

Parallel experiments comparing the AEM with Nafion 117 as the separator material revealed significant differences in performance depending on the active species pairing. For the anthraquinone-TEMPO cell at pH 9 and 100 mA/cm², the AEM achieved a CE of 96% and an EE of 81%, while Nafion 117 yielded a lower CE of 93% and EE of 78%. The superior performance of the AEM was attributed to its higher selectivity for hydroxide transport over organic anion permeation, with measured ion selectivity of 94% compared to 89% for Nafion 117. This selectivity advantage was most pronounced for the methyl viologen system, where Nafion 117 exhibited severe capacity fade at 0.15% per cycle due to enhanced viologen crossover facilitated by cation exchange pathways in the sulfonated membrane structure.

Conversely, for the benzoquinone-TEMPO pairing at pH 7, Nafion 117 marginally outperformed the AEM, achieving a CE of 95% versus 93% for the AEM. This reversal reflected the different charge states of the active species in neutral versus alkaline media, with the neutral benzoquinone species experiencing less favorable transport through the anion-conducting AEM while the cationic TEMPO+ oxidized form was more effectively excluded by Nafion's Donnan potential. These results underscore the necessity of matching membrane chemistry to the specific charge characteristics of the chosen active species across the intended pH operating range.

## 5. Degradation Mechanism Analysis

The degradation mechanisms operative in the investigated ORFB systems can be classified into three primary categories: chemical decomposition via dimerization, physical transport via membrane crossover, and electrochemical imbalance via asymmetric capacity fade. The relative contributions of these mechanisms varied substantially across the three active species classes and were further modulated by pH and current density.

Dimerization was predominantly observed for anthraquinone at elevated pH, where the fully reduced anthrahydroquinone species with its increased electron density and deprotonated hydroxyl groups exhibited enhanced nucleophilicity toward the oxidized quinone form. The kinetic analysis of the UV-Vis absorption at 380 nm indicated that dimerization followed a second-order dependence on anthrahydroquinone concentration, with the rate constant increasing by a factor of 3.2 upon raising pH from 12 to 14. This pH sensitivity arises because deprotonation of the hydroquinone hydroxyl groups at pH 14 increases the electron density on the aromatic ring, accelerating electrophilic attack on a neighboring quinone molecule. In contrast, TEMPO showed no detectable dimerization under any tested condition, consistent with the steric protection provided by the four methyl substituents on the piperidine ring that prevent close approach of two radical centers. Methyl viologen likewise exhibited negligible dimerization, though radical cation pairing (MV+·MV+) was detected at high concentrations as a reversible association with no permanent capacity loss.

Crossover rates followed the order methyl viologen > TEMPO > anthraquinone under standardized conditions (AEM, pH 9, 60 mA/cm²), with measured permeability values of 4.8 x 10⁻⁷, 2.4 x 10⁻⁷, and 0.7 x 10⁻⁷ cm²/s, respectively. The exceptionally low crossover of anthraquinone resulted from the doubly negative charge of the reduced anthrahydroquinone anion, which experienced strong Donnan exclusion from the anion-exchange membrane. The membrane selectivity, defined as the ratio of ionic conductivity to organic permeability, was highest for the anthraquinone-AEM combination at 94% and lowest for the methyl viologen-Nafion 117 pairing at 72%. These selectivity values directly correlated with the observed capacity retention over extended cycling, confirming that separator optimization represents a critical lever for system lifetime improvement.

Capacity imbalance emerged as a consequential secondary effect of asymmetric crossover, where the differing permeation rates of negolyte and posolyte species created unequal active material inventories that progressively reduced the utilizable cell capacity. In the anthraquinone-TEMPO system at 40 mA/cm², the crossover-induced imbalance reached 14% after 500 cycles, effectively reducing the accessible capacity to 86% of the theoretical value even before accounting for irreversible chemical degradation. The capacity imbalance was more severe for the anthraquinone-methyl viologen system, reaching 18% after 150 cycles, driven by the particularly high crossover rate of the viologen species. Mathematical modeling of the crossover fluxes suggested that membrane selectivity improvements of 5–10 percentage points could reduce the imbalance accumulation rate by approximately 40%, highlighting the practical significance of separator engineering.

## 6. Discussion

The results presented herein establish clear structure-property-performance relationships across the three major classes of organic active species for redox flow battery applications. The comparative framework reveals that no single active species simultaneously maximizes all figures of merit; rather, each chemistry presents distinct advantages and limitations that must be evaluated within the context of the intended application.

Anthraquinone offers the highest single-cell voltage when paired with TEMPO, attributable to its substantially negative redox potential that increases further with pH. However, this voltage advantage is partially offset by the pH-dependent dimerization pathway that accelerates capacity fade at pH 14 relative to pH 12. The demonstrated fade rate of 0.08% per cycle at pH 14 translates to approximately 50% capacity retention after 600 cycles, which may be acceptable for daily cycling applications but falls short of the decade-long durability targets for grid storage. Structural modification of the anthraquinone core to introduce bulky substituents at the 2-position represents a promising strategy for mitigating dimerization while preserving the favorable redox potential, with preliminary results from substituted derivatives suggesting fade rate reductions of 30–50%.

TEMPO emerges as the most electrochemically stable of the three species, with undetectable chemical degradation over 500 cycles and only minor losses attributable to crossover. The measured EE of 87% at 100 mA/cm² positions TEMPO as an efficient posolyte material, though its relatively high cost per mole and moderate aqueous solubility of 0.1 M constrain the overall energy density of the system. The pH independence of the TEMPO redox potential provides valuable flexibility in system design, enabling pH decoupling across the membrane to maximize cell voltage—a configuration not readily achievable with pH-sensitive posolyte alternatives.

Methyl viologen presents a mixed performance profile. Its exceptional CE of 99% for the first reduction step and low material cost are attractive attributes, yet the combination of high crossover rate, MV⁰ precipitation during deep discharge, and rapid capacity imbalance development limits its practicality for long-duration cycling. The crossover rate of 4.8 x 10⁻⁷ cm²/s through the AEM is particularly concerning, as it exceeds the TEMPO rate by a factor of two and the anthraquinone rate by nearly a factor of seven. Without substantial membrane selectivity improvements or viologen structural modification to increase charge density and steric bulk, this chemistry appears better suited for stationary applications with frequent electrolyte maintenance or membrane replacement protocols.

The membrane comparison between AEM and Nafion 117 highlights the complexity of separator selection in ORFB systems. The superior selectivity of the AEM for the anthraquinone systems (94% versus 89% for Nafion 117) reflects favorable Donnan exclusion of the multiply charged organic anions, while Nafion 117 demonstrated advantages for neutral species such as benzoquinone at pH 7. These findings suggest that system-specific membrane optimization rather than universal separator selection represents the optimal path forward, with emerging tailored membranes bearing charge-mismatched functional groups showing preliminary promise for simultaneously excluding both anionic and cationic organic species.

From a mechanistic perspective, the identification of dimerization as the dominant fade pathway for anthraquinone and crossover as the primary concern for methyl viologen enables targeted mitigation strategies. For anthraquinone, electrolyte formulation adjustments to reduce the effective pH at the electrode surface while maintaining bulk pH 14 could suppress dimerization kinetics without sacrificing redox potential. For methyl viologen, size-exclusion membrane coatings or viologen derivatives with appended steric bulk may reduce the crossover rate to acceptable levels. The capacity imbalance phenomenon, while secondary in origin, has primary operational significance, as the 14–18% capacity reductions observed after hundreds of cycles would substantially impact the economic viability of ORFB systems for commercial energy storage applications.

## 7. Conclusion

This study has presented a comprehensive comparative evaluation of anthraquinone, TEMPO, and methyl viologen as active species in aqueous organic redox flow batteries, with systematic variation of pH, current density, and membrane chemistry. The key findings are summarized as follows. The anthraquinone-TEMPO full cell achieved the highest energy efficiency of 82% at 100 mA/cm² with a CE of 97%, but suffered from pH-dependent dimerization that increased the capacity fade rate from 0.05% per cycle at pH 12 to 0.08% per cycle at pH 14. TEMPO demonstrated exceptional electrochemical stability with negligible degradation over 500 cycles, minimal pH sensitivity in its redox potential, and a moderate crossover rate of 2.1 x 10⁻⁷ cm²/s. Methyl viologen exhibited the highest single-step CE of 99% at 60 mA/cm² but the most severe crossover at 4.8 x 10⁻⁷ cm²/s, leading to rapid capacity imbalance development and practical capacity limitations in extended cycling. The AEM outperformed Nafion 117 for alkaline quinone systems with 94% versus 89% ion selectivity, while the relative membrane performance inverted for neutral benzoquinone species.

These quantitative benchmarks provide a foundation for rational electrolyte-membrane-electrode system design and highlight the critical importance of addressing degradation mechanisms—dimerization, crossover, and capacity imbalance—in the development of durable, cost-effective organic redox flow batteries for grid-scale energy storage. Future efforts should prioritize molecular engineering to suppress quinone dimerization, membrane modification to reduce viologen permeation, and cell architecture innovations to mitigate crossover-induced capacity imbalance in extended operation.

# Cross-System Design Rules and Strategy Comparison for Redox Flow Batteries

## Abstract

This study presents a systematic cross-system comparison of five established redox flow battery (RFB) chemistries—vanadium redox (VRFB), iron-chromium (ICRFB), zinc-bromine (Zn-Br), soluble lead-acid, and all-organic systems—focusing on the interplay between membrane selection, electrode architecture, and electrolyte formulation in determining operational performance metrics. By analyzing coulombic efficiency (CE), voltage efficiency (VE), and energy efficiency (EE) at their respective characteristic current densities, we identify component-level strategies that consistently promote high energy efficiency and long-term stability. We extract five cross-system design rules that account for current density regimes ranging from 20 mA/cm² in soluble lead-acid systems to 200 mA/cm² in state-of-the-art VRFBs, and for the distinct degradation modes that dominate each chemistry. These rules provide a framework for translating optimization strategies from one RFB platform to another, highlighting where direct transfer is appropriate and where chemistry-specific adaptations are essential.

## Introduction

Redox flow batteries have emerged as promising electrochemical technologies for grid-scale energy storage, offering decoupled energy and power ratings, long cycle life, and inherent safety advantages over lithium-ion systems. However, the diversity of chemistries investigated over the past four decades has produced a fragmented knowledge base in which design insights remain largely confined within individual chemistry communities. While vanadium chemistry has achieved the most advanced commercial deployment, supported by extensive characterization at current densities between 100 and 200 mA/cm² using Nafion 117 membranes, other systems such as iron-chromium, zinc-bromine, soluble lead-acid, and organic RFBs operate under markedly different constraints.

The central challenge lies in the fact that seemingly similar components serve different functional roles across chemistries. Nafion 117 in VRFBs primarily prevents vanadium ion crossover while maintaining proton conductivity, whereas anion exchange membranes in zinc-bromine systems must suppress both bromide crossover and polybromide complex formation. Carbon felt electrodes in VRFBs and organic RFBs are replaced by metallic lead electrodes in soluble lead-acid systems, fundamentally altering the relationship between surface area and kinetic overpotential. These differences mean that optimization strategies cannot be directly transplanted without careful consideration of underlying mechanisms.

This work conducts parallel analyses across five RFB systems, examining performance metrics at each system's characteristic current density range. We pay particular attention to the condition-dependent nature of efficiency metrics: a coulombic efficiency of 96% in a VRFB at 160 mA/cm² with Nafion 117 carries different design implications than the same CE in a zinc-bromine battery at 60 mA/cm² with an anion exchange membrane. By grounding all comparisons in specific current density, membrane type, and electrode material context, we extract design rules that account for chemistry-specific constraints while highlighting genuinely transferable principles.

## Performance Benchmarks Across RFB Chemistries

Establishing meaningful cross-system comparisons requires first characterizing each chemistry within its practically relevant current density window. Operating a system significantly above its standard current density range typically degrades voltage efficiency due to ohmic and kinetic losses, while operating far below it sacrifices power density and increases capital cost per unit power output.

In the vanadium system, extensive testing with Nafion 117 membrane and graphite felt electrodes has established benchmarks at current densities from 100 to 200 mA/cm². At 100 mA/cm², a well-assembled VRFB typically delivers CE of 94-96%, VE of 88-91%, and EE of 83-87%. When current density increases to 200 mA/cm², CE rises to 97-98% due to reduced diffusion-driven crossover, but VE declines to 78-82% because of increased ohmic overpotential. EE at this higher current density therefore falls to 75-80%.

The iron-chromium system, when operated with Bi³⁺ catalyst addition to the chromium electrolyte, achieves respectable performance at current densities between 80 and 160 mA/cm². At 80 mA/cm², ICRFBs with carbon felt electrodes and anion exchange membranes typically exhibit CE values of 92-95% and EE values of 78-82%. The chromium redox kinetics remain the rate-limiting step even with Bi³⁺ catalysis, and the hydrogen evolution side reaction on the negative electrode contributes to coulombic efficiency losses that are more severe than those observed in VRFBs. Increasing current density to 160 mA/cm² improves CE modestly to 94-96% but reduces EE to 70-75%, reflecting the substantial kinetic overpotential associated with the Cr³⁺/Cr²⁺ couple. The Bi³⁺ additive functions by modifying the electrode-electrolyte interfacial environment to lower the activation barrier for chromium reduction, and its concentration in the electrolyte must be maintained above a threshold level—typically 0.01-0.05 M—for catalytic effectiveness.

Zinc-bromine flow batteries occupy a lower current density regime, with practical operation between 40 and 80 mA/cm². The plating and stripping of metallic zinc introduces morphological complexity absent in fully soluble RFB chemistries. At 40 mA/cm², Zn-Br systems with carbon felt electrodes and anion exchange membranes achieve CE of 92-94% and EE of 75-79%, limited primarily by bromide crossover and polybromide migration. At 80 mA/cm², CE improves to 95-97% due to shorter charge durations, but zinc dendrite formation becomes more pronounced, affecting cycle life. EE at 80 mA/cm² typically ranges from 70 to 74%, with VE loss dominated by bromine evolution overpotential.

Soluble lead-acid flow batteries operate at the lowest current densities among the systems examined, typically 20 to 40 mA/cm², constrained by the precipitation-dissolution mechanism of lead and lead dioxide on graphite electrodes. At 20 mA/cm², this system achieves CE of 90-93% and EE of 72-76%. The relatively low CE stems from competing hydrogen evolution on the negative electrode during charging, as well as from incomplete dissolution of deposited lead during discharge cycles. At 40 mA/cm², CE improves slightly to 92-94%, but VE drops substantially due to the limited conductivity of the methanesulfonic acid electrolyte and the high overpotential for lead dioxide formation, resulting in EE values of 65-70%. The use of lead electrodes rather than carbon-based materials fundamentally changes the electrode kinetics: the lead deposition reaction proceeds with lower activation overpotential than most RFB redox couples, but the solid-phase precipitation introduces mass transport limitations that carbon felt electrodes in other systems do not experience.

Organic redox flow batteries represent the most diverse category, with performance strongly dependent on the specific active materials. Representative systems using anthraquinone derivatives or TEMPO-based catholytes, tested at 20 to 100 mA/cm² with activated carbon felt electrodes and size-selective membranes, exhibit CE of 91-97% and EE of 68-82%. At 40 mA/cm², a well-optimized organic RFB can achieve CE of 94-96% and EE of 78-82%, comparable to inorganic systems at their standard current densities. However, at 100 mA/cm², EE declines to 68-73%, with VE loss reflecting lower intrinsic electron transfer rates. The membrane requirements differ qualitatively because active species are often molecularly large but charge-compact, requiring size-discriminating membranes.

## Component-Level Design Strategies for High Energy Efficiency

Achieving high energy efficiency in any RFB system requires minimizing three distinct loss categories: crossover-related coulombic inefficiency, ohmic resistive losses, and kinetic overpotential losses. The relative contributions of these categories vary systematically across chemistries and current densities, enabling targeted component strategies.

Membrane selection exerts the strongest influence on coulombic efficiency across all systems. In VRFBs, Nafion 117 provides the benchmark performance, with areal resistances of approximately 1.5-2.5 Ω·cm² when fully hydrated. Thinner membranes reduce ohmic resistance and improve VE but increase vanadium crossover and reduce CE; this trade-off means that the optimal membrane thickness for maximum EE depends on the operating current density. At 100 mA/cm², Nafion 117 typically provides the best EE balance, while at 200 mA/cm² thinner alternatives may yield higher EE despite modestly lower CE because the VE gain outweighs the CE loss. In iron-chromium systems, anion exchange membranes such as the SnPBI-1.42 material suppress iron crossover more effectively than Nafion, and their lower permselectivity for protons is partially offset by the lower operating current density. The SnPBI-1.42 membrane achieves CE improvements of 2-4 percentage points compared to Nafion 117 in ICRFBs at 120 mA/cm², though with slightly higher areal resistance (2.5-3.5 Ω·cm²).

In zinc-bromine systems, the membrane must perform a dual function: blocking bromide ion migration and resisting chemical attack by elemental bromine and polybromide complexes. Microporous separators with anion exchange functionality have shown superior performance to Nafion in this chemistry, improving CE by 3-5 percentage points at 60 mA/cm² while maintaining acceptable VE. The pore size distribution in these membranes is critical: pores must be small enough to exclude polybromide anions (Stokes radii exceeding 0.5 nm) while permitting sufficient ionic conductivity. Soluble lead-acid systems present a different membrane challenge because the lead ions are divalent and have higher mobility than the monovalent ions in other systems. Anion exchange membranes with high sulfate selectivity improve CE by 2-3 percentage points at 30 mA/cm² compared to non-selective separators.

For organic RFBs, membrane design must address the size diversity of active species. TEMPO radicals and anthraquinone sulfonates have molecular weights of 150-400 g/mol, intermediate between the small metal ions of inorganic systems and the polybromide complexes of zinc-bromine batteries. Size-exclusion membranes with pore diameters tuned to 0.3-0.6 nm provide effective CE improvement (4-6 percentage points at 60 mA/cm²) without excessive resistance penalties, though manufacturing reproducibility remains a challenge.

Electrode engineering provides the second major lever for efficiency optimization. In VRFBs, graphite felt electrodes with fiber diameters of 10-20 μm and porosities of 90-95% offer high surface area for the vanadium reactions while maintaining low pressure drop for electrolyte circulation. Thermal and chemical activation treatments that introduce oxygen-containing functional groups at concentrations of 2-5 at% improve the wettability of the felt and reduce kinetic overpotential. Such treatments typically improve VE by 1-3 percentage points at 160 mA/cm², corresponding to EE improvements of 1-2 percentage points. In ICRFBs, carbon felt electrodes with Bi³⁺ catalyst electrodeposited on the fiber surfaces reduce the combined overpotential by facilitating chromium reduction while moderately suppressing hydrogen evolution. The optimal Bi loading is 0.5-2.0 mg/cm² of geometric electrode area; below this range catalytic activity is insufficient, while above it the Bi layer increases ohmic resistance and blocks electrolyte access. At 120 mA/cm², optimized Bi-modified electrodes improve EE by 3-5 percentage points compared to untreated carbon felt.

Zinc-bromine batteries require electrodes that support uniform zinc plating. Carbon felt substrates provide nucleation sites for zinc deposition, but the fiber geometry can promote dendrite growth at locations of high electric field concentration. Composite electrodes incorporating copper mesh current collectors with carbon coating improve plating uniformity and extend cycle life, though the impact on instantaneous EE at a given current density is modest (1-2 percentage points). Organic RFBs benefit from activated carbon felt electrodes with high mesopore content (pore diameters 2-50 nm) that facilitates access of large organic molecules to the electrochemical double layer. Surface functionalization with quinone or nitrogen-containing groups can improve electron transfer kinetics, with reported VE improvements of 2-4 percentage points at 80 mA/cm² for matched electrode-active material combinations.

## Degradation Mechanisms and Their System-Specific Manifestations

Long-term performance stability determines the practical viability of RFB systems beyond initial efficiency metrics. Each chemistry exhibits characteristic degradation modes that link back to component interactions and operating conditions.

VRFB degradation is dominated by three coupled mechanisms: vanadium ion crossover leading to gradual electrolyte capacity imbalance, oxidative corrosion of the carbon felt positive electrode, and thermal precipitation of V₂O₅ at the positive electrode when the state of charge exceeds 80% and temperature rises above 40°C. The crossover rate depends on the vanadium concentration gradient across the membrane, the membrane's permeability to each vanadium species, and the duration of charge-discharge cycles. At 160 mA/cm² with Nafion 117, typical capacity fade rates are 0.05-0.15% per cycle, with the fade accelerating at higher temperatures due to increased vanadium diffusion coefficients.

ICRFB degradation involves a different set of challenges. The Bi³⁺ catalyst gradually depletes through electrodeposition onto the carbon felt and subsequent detachment as metallic bismuth particles, reducing catalytic activity over 50-200 cycles depending on the electrolyte flow rate and initial Bi³⁺ concentration. Additionally, iron crossover through the membrane creates a secondary capacity fade mechanism. Hydrogen evolution on the negative electrode not only reduces CE but also gradually increases the pH of the negative electrolyte, potentially precipitating chromium hydroxide species. At 120 mA/cm², ICRFBs typically exhibit capacity fade rates of 0.1-0.3% per cycle, higher than well-managed VRFBs under comparable conditions.

Zinc-bromine battery degradation centers on the morphological evolution of the zinc deposit. Dendritic growth during charging can penetrate the separator and cause internal short circuits, a failure mode that is abrupt and irreversible. Even before dendrite penetration, non-uniform zinc deposition progressively reduces the electrochemically active surface area, increasing local current density and accelerating the dendrite problem. Cycle life in Zn-Br systems is often limited to 500-2000 deep cycles, shorter than VRFBs and ICRFBs, though the zinc-bromine chemistry benefits from lower active material costs.

Soluble lead-acid systems face unique challenges associated with the precipitation-dissolution mechanism. Incomplete dissolution of lead or lead dioxide during discharge leads to cumulative solid buildup on the electrodes, reducing available surface area and distorting the electrode geometry. Acid concentration management is critical: methanesulfonic acid concentrations below 1.0 M reduce conductivity and increase ohmic losses, while concentrations above 2.0 M promote excessive lead precipitation. Operating at 30 mA/cm² with optimized acid concentration (1.5-1.8 M) provides the best balance, but capacity fade rates of 0.2-0.5% per cycle remain higher than in fully soluble chemistries.

Organic RFB degradation is dominated by chemical decomposition of the active materials rather than electrode or membrane degradation. Anthraquinone-based catholytes undergo gradual hydrogenation and dimerization under strongly acidic or basic conditions, with decomposition rates strongly dependent on pH and oxygen exposure. TEMPO radicals are susceptible to disproportionation reactions that produce non-redox-active byproducts. Reported capacity fade rates for organic RFBs range widely from 0.05% to 0.5% per cycle, depending on the specific active materials and the stringency of electrolyte protection from oxygen and moisture.

## Cross-System Design Rules for High Energy Efficiency

Synthesizing the component strategies and degradation analyses across all five chemistries yields five cross-system design rules that account for the distinct operating regimes and degradation physics of each system.

**Rule 1: Current density-dependent membrane optimization.** The optimal membrane thickness and ion selectivity depend on the operating current density in a manner that differs across chemistries but follows a common functional form. At low current densities (20-60 mA/cm²), CE losses from active species crossover dominate the EE budget, favoring thicker or more selective membranes with higher areal resistance. At high current densities (120-200 mA/cm²), VE losses from ohmic resistance dominate, favoring thinner membranes with lower selectivity. The crossover current density at which the optimal membrane type switches is chemistry-specific: approximately 100 mA/cm² for VRFBs with Nafion membranes, 80 mA/cm² for ICRFBs with anion exchange membranes, and 50 mA/cm² for zinc-bromine systems.

**Rule 2: Electrode surface engineering must match the rate-limiting redox couple.** Across all systems, the redox couple with slower intrinsic kinetics determines the electrode overpotential contribution to VE loss. In VRFBs, the V³⁺/V²⁺ reduction on the negative electrode is typically rate-limiting, and electrode treatments that specifically improve the kinetics of this half-reaction provide the greatest EE benefit. In ICRFBs, the Cr³⁺/Cr²⁺ couple is overwhelmingly rate-limiting, and Bi³⁺ catalyst integration is correspondingly more impactful than any electrode surface treatment alone. In zinc-bromine systems, the zinc plating kinetics are intrinsically fast on metallic substrates but limited by nucleation on carbon; electrode designs that promote uniform nucleation outperform generic high-surface-area carbon. For organic RFBs, the heterogeneity of organic redox couples precludes universal electrode treatments; instead, matching the electrode functional groups to the specific redox chemistry provides the best results.

**Rule 3: Side reaction suppression scales with current density and electrode potential.** The severity of parasitic reactions—hydrogen evolution in acid systems, oxygen evolution in some organic systems, bromine formation in Zn-Br batteries—scales with the deviation of electrode potential from the thermodynamic redox potential of the main reaction. At higher current densities, larger overpotentials are required to drive the desired reactions, bringing the electrode potential closer to the onset potential of side reactions. This means that the current density ceiling for each system is not simply a matter of ohmic and kinetic losses but also of side reaction tolerance. VRFBs tolerate high current densities well because the vanadium redox potentials are well-separated from hydrogen and oxygen evolution potentials. ICRFBs are more constrained because the Cr³⁺/Cr²⁺ potential is close to the hydrogen evolution potential, making the competing reaction unavoidable at higher current densities. This proximity explains why ICRFBs achieve lower maximum practical current densities (approximately 160 mA/cm²) compared to VRFBs (200 mA/cm² or higher).

**Rule 4: Degradation mode hierarchy determines cycle life limits.** The dominant degradation mechanism differs systematically across chemistries, and mitigation strategies must target the primary mode rather than secondary effects. In VRFBs, capacity imbalance from crossover is the primary limit and can be addressed through membrane improvement and periodic electrolyte rebalancing. In ICRFBs, catalyst depletion is primary and requires engineered catalyst retention or continuous replenishment systems. In zinc-bromine systems, dendrite-induced short circuits are the primary concern and drive the need for separator robustness and zinc morphology control. In soluble lead-acid systems, incomplete dissolution is primary and limits the depth of discharge to 60-80% of theoretical capacity. In organic RFBs, chemical decomposition is primary and necessitates rigorous electrolyte protection and active material molecular design. Attempting to mitigate secondary degradation modes while ignoring the primary mode yields diminishing returns in cycle life improvement.

**Rule 5: Cost-performance trade-offs are chemistry-dependent but follow common scaling.** The relative cost contributions of membranes, electrodes, and electrolytes vary across systems. In VRFBs, the vanadium electrolyte typically represents 30-40% of the system cost, making electrolyte utilization efficiency a major economic driver. In organic RFBs, active materials are less expensive per unit mass but require higher concentrations or larger volumes to achieve comparable energy density, resulting in similar overall electrolyte cost fractions. The membrane cost fraction is highest in systems requiring specialized separators: zinc-bromine and organic RFBs, where membrane customization adds manufacturing cost. The common scaling principle is that the component with the highest cost fraction should receive the most intensive optimization effort, as improvements to the most expensive component yield the largest cost-benefit ratios.

## Discussion

The cross-system analysis reveals both transferable principles and chemistry-specific constraints that shape the practical design space for RFB development. The observation that energy efficiency scales differently with current density across chemistries has direct implications for system sizing and economic modeling. A VRFB designed for 160 mA/cm² operation achieves EE values of 80-85% that are broadly comparable to those of a Zn-Br system at 60 mA/cm² (75-79%) and an organic RFB at 40 mA/cm² (78-82%), but the capital cost implications of the different current densities are substantial. Higher current density operation reduces the stack size and therefore the capital cost per unit power, but only if the EE remains high enough to avoid unacceptable round-trip energy losses.

The membrane optimization trade-off identified in Rule 1 has perhaps the broadest applicability across chemistries. The general principle that low-current-density systems benefit from high-selectivity membranes while high-current-density systems benefit from low-resistance membranes holds for all five chemistries examined, even though the specific membrane materials differ. This principle suggests that membrane development efforts can be partially decoupled from chemistry-specific optimization: a portfolio of membranes spanning a range of selectivity-resistance combinations can be matched to different RFB systems based on their operating current density, rather than requiring bespoke membrane development for each chemistry.

The electrode engineering strategies, by contrast, are less transferable. The fundamentally different electrode requirements of metallic deposition systems versus all-soluble systems mean that electrode designs developed for one category rarely translate directly to the other. Even within the all-soluble category, the specific redox couple kinetics determine which surface treatments are beneficial. This finding suggests that electrode development should proceed in parallel with electrolyte formulation, with the two optimized as a coupled system rather than independently.

The degradation analysis highlights that cycle life limitations are the most chemistry-specific aspect of RFB design. While efficiency optimization strategies show some convergence across systems, the primary degradation modes are rooted in the unique electrochemical and chemical properties of each active material combination. This observation implies that claims of cycle life for one RFB chemistry should not be used as benchmarks for what is achievable in another chemistry without careful analysis of the underlying degradation physics.

## Conclusion

This cross-system comparison has identified both universal design principles and chemistry-specific constraints governing energy efficiency and cycle life. The five design rules provide a framework for translating optimization strategies across platforms while respecting the distinct operating conditions of each system: current density-dependent membrane optimization (Rule 1), electrode engineering matching rate-limiting kinetics (Rule 2), side reaction scaling with overpotential (Rule 3), degradation mode hierarchy determining cycle life (Rule 4), and chemistry-dependent cost-performance scaling (Rule 5).

For practitioners, VRFBs and ICRFBs benefit most from membrane and catalyst optimization at 100-200 mA/cm² and 80-160 mA/cm², respectively, while zinc-bromine and organic systems should prioritize separator robustness and active material stability at 40-80 mA/cm² and 20-100 mA/cm². This framework enables more informed decision-making when selecting among RFB chemistries and when adapting design innovations across platforms. Future work should extend this analysis to include techno-economic modeling that translates efficiency and cycle life benchmarks into levelized cost of storage estimates.
