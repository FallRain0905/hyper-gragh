# Bismuth Ion as an Electrolyte Additive for Suppressing Hydrogen Evolution and Enhancing Chromium Redox Kinetics in Iron-Chromium Redox Flow Batteries

## Abstract

The iron-chromium redox flow battery (ICRFB) represents a promising candidate for large-scale electrochemical energy storage, yet its commercial viability is severely constrained by the competitive hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) on the negative electrode and the sluggish kinetics of the Cr(III)/Cr(II) redox couple. In this work, we investigate the use of bismuth ions (Bi3+) as a homogeneous electrolyte additive to simultaneously address both limitations. Cyclic voltammetry and rotating disk electrode measurements demonstrate that the addition of Bi3+ at concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 3.0 ppm substantially accelerates the Cr(III)/Cr(II) charge transfer reaction, with the standard rate constant increasing from 2.1 × 10^-3 cm/s in the pristine electrolyte to 4.8 × 10^-3 cm/s upon the introduction of 2.0 ppm Bi3+. Simultaneously, Bi3+ undergoes underpotential deposition on the graphite felt electrode surface, forming a bismuth adlayer that increases the HER overpotential by approximately 85 mV at 80 mA/cm^2. Single-cell tests conducted at current densities of 40, 80, 120, and 160 mA/cm^2 reveal that the optimal Bi3+ concentration of 2.0 ppm yields a coulombic efficiency of 94% and an energy efficiency of 87% at 80 mA/cm^2, compared to 87% and 72% respectively for the baseline cell without additive. Long-term cycling over 200 charge-discharge cycles at 80 mA/cm^2 confirms that the Bi3+-containing cell maintains stable performance with a capacity fade rate of 0.03% per cycle, whereas the baseline cell exhibits accelerated capacity degradation at 0.12% per cycle, predominantly attributable to hydrogen accumulation and subsequent electrolyte imbalance. These findings establish Bi3+ as an effective, low-cost additive strategy for improving the performance and durability of ICRFB systems.

## 1. Introduction

Redox flow batteries (RFBs) have garnered substantial research attention as grid-scale energy storage technologies capable of decoupling energy and power ratings, thereby enabling flexible system design for integrating intermittent renewable energy sources. Among the various RFB chemistries explored to date, the all-vanadium redox flow battery (VRFB) has achieved the highest degree of commercial maturity; however, the high cost of vanadium salts and the limited availability of vanadium resources have motivated continued exploration of alternative redox couples based on earth-abundant elements. The iron-chromium redox flow battery (ICRFB), which utilizes Fe(III)/Fe(II) and Cr(III)/Cr(II) redox couples in acidic aqueous electrolytes, offers a potentially attractive cost advantage owing to the natural abundance and low cost of both iron and chromium precursors.

Despite its economic appeal, the ICRFB faces two fundamental technical barriers that have impeded its widespread deployment. The first and most critical challenge arises from the thermodynamic favorability of the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) on the negative electrode, where the standard reduction potential of the Cr(III)/Cr(II) couple (-0.41 V vs. SHE) lies negative to the reversible hydrogen electrode potential under typical operating conditions. This potential overlap results in substantial parasitic hydrogen evolution during the charging process, leading to low coulombic efficiency (CE), rapid electrolyte imbalance, and accelerated capacity fade over extended cycling. The second challenge concerns the intrinsically slow kinetics of the Cr(III)/Cr(II) redox reaction, which is characterized by a large inner-sphere reorganization energy associated with the d-electron transfer process in octahedral chromium complexes. This kinetic limitation manifests as high activation overpotential, restricting the operating current density and thus reducing the power density and energy efficiency (EE) of the ICRFB system.

A diverse range of strategies has been pursued to mitigate HER and enhance chromium redox kinetics in ICRFBs. These include the development of catalytic electrode materials such as bismuth-modified carbon felts, gold nanoparticle-decorated electrodes, and nitrogen-doped carbon surfaces; the optimization of electrolyte compositions through the addition of catalytic metal ions, complexing agents, and supporting electrolytes; and the engineering of cell configurations including flow field design and membrane selection. Among these approaches, the use of bismuth-based additives has attracted particular interest due to the high hydrogen overpotential of metallic bismuth and its ability to form underpotential deposition layers on carbon substrates. Previous studies have primarily focused on electrodeposited bismuth coatings on solid electrodes, which require additional fabrication steps and may suffer from delamination during extended operation.

In contrast to solid-phase bismuth modification, the present work explores the direct addition of Bi3+ ions to the electrolyte as a homogeneous additive that can continuously replenish the bismuth adlayer on the electrode surface through dynamic adsorption-desorption equilibrium. This approach offers several potential advantages, including simplified cell assembly, uniform distribution of the bismuth species throughout the porous electrode structure, and self-healing of the catalytic surface during long-term cycling. Herein, we report a systematic investigation of Bi3+ additive effects on the electrochemical kinetics of the Cr(III)/Cr(II) couple, the HER suppression mechanism, and the overall performance of a laboratory-scale ICRFB single cell. The influence of Bi3+ concentration is examined across the range of 0.5 to 3.0 ppm, and the relationship between additive concentration, operating current density, and cell efficiency is rigorously quantified. Furthermore, the long-term cycling stability of Bi3+-containing ICRFB cells is evaluated and compared against baseline configurations to assess the practical viability of this additive strategy.

## 2. Electrochemical Characterization of Cr(III)/Cr(II) Redox Kinetics with Bi3+ Additive

### 2.1 Cyclic Voltammetry Analysis

The electrochemical behavior of the Cr(III)/Cr(II) redox couple in the presence of varying Bi3+ concentrations was first characterized by cyclic voltammetry (CV) on a glassy carbon electrode in 1.0 M HCl containing 0.5 M CrCl3. Figure 1a presents the CV curves recorded at a scan rate of 50 mV/s for electrolytes containing 0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, and 3.0 ppm Bi3+, added in the form of BiCl3. In the absence of Bi3+, the Cr(III) reduction peak appears at -0.58 V vs. Ag/AgCl with a peak current density of 3.2 mA/cm^2, while the Cr(II) oxidation peak is observed at -0.31 V vs. Ag/AgCl at 2.8 mA/cm^2. The large peak separation of 270 mV indicates quasi-reversible electron transfer kinetics consistent with the known inner-sphere character of the Cr(III)/Cr(II) couple.

Upon the addition of Bi3+, significant changes in the voltammetric response are observed. At 0.5 ppm Bi3+, the reduction peak shifts positively to -0.54 V vs. Ag/AgCl and the peak current density increases to 3.8 mA/cm^2, suggesting an onset of catalytic enhancement. As the Bi3+ concentration is progressively increased to 1.0 ppm and 1.5 ppm, the reduction peaks continue to shift toward less negative potentials, reaching -0.50 V and -0.47 V respectively, while the corresponding peak current densities rise to 4.3 mA/cm^2 and 4.7 mA/cm^2. The most pronounced catalytic effect is observed at 2.0 ppm Bi3+, where the reduction peak potential shifts to -0.44 V vs. Ag/AgCl and the peak current density reaches 5.1 mA/cm^2, representing a 59% increase in peak current relative to the pristine electrolyte. The peak separation narrows to 180 mV, reflecting significantly improved electron transfer kinetics. Interestingly, further increasing the Bi3+ concentration to 3.0 ppm results in a slight decrease in peak current density to 4.9 mA/cm^2 and a small negative shift in peak potential to -0.45 V, suggesting that the catalytic enhancement reaches an optimum at 2.0 ppm and diminishes marginally at higher concentrations, possibly due to competitive adsorption effects or partial blocking of active surface sites by excess bismuth species.

### 2.2 Rotating Disk Electrode Measurements

To quantitatively assess the electron transfer kinetics, rotating disk electrode (RDE) measurements were performed on the Cr(III)/Cr(II) system across the same range of Bi3+ concentrations. The Levich-Koutecky analysis of the RDE data at rotation rates from 400 to 2500 rpm yielded the standard heterogeneous electron transfer rate constants (k0) summarized in Table 1. The pristine electrolyte exhibits a k0 value of 2.1 × 10^-3 cm/s, which is consistent with literature values for the Cr(III)/Cr(II) couple in hydrochloric acid supporting electrolyte. With the addition of 0.5 ppm Bi3+, k0 increases to 2.7 × 10^-3 cm/s, corresponding to a 29% enhancement. At 1.0 ppm Bi3+, the rate constant reaches 3.4 × 10^-3 cm/s, and at 1.5 ppm it further increases to 4.2 × 10^-3 cm/s. The maximum rate constant of 4.8 × 10^-3 cm/s is obtained at 2.0 ppm Bi3+, representing a 2.3-fold acceleration of the electron transfer reaction relative to the baseline. This substantial kinetic enhancement translates to reduced activation overpotential during battery operation, particularly at elevated current densities. At 3.0 ppm Bi3+, the rate constant decreases slightly to 4.6 × 10^-3 cm/s, confirming the existence of an optimal concentration window.

The origin of the catalytic enhancement can be attributed to the formation of Cr(III)-Bi3+ surface complexes or adsorbed bismuth adatoms that facilitate the inner-sphere electron transfer pathway. Bismuth species adsorbed on the electrode surface may act as redox mediators, providing an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy compared to the direct electron transfer between Cr(III) and the carbon electrode. The optimal concentration at 2.0 ppm likely reflects a balance between maximizing the surface coverage of catalytically active bismuth species and avoiding excessive site blocking at higher concentrations.

## 3. Hydrogen Evolution Reaction Suppression Mechanism

### 3.1 HER Polarization Behavior

The suppression of parasitic hydrogen evolution represents the primary motivation for introducing Bi3+ into the ICRFB electrolyte. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was conducted on graphite felt electrodes in 1.0 M HCl with and without Bi3+ additive to evaluate the HER polarization characteristics. In the pristine electrolyte, the HER onset potential on the untreated graphite felt is approximately -0.38 V vs. Ag/AgCl, and the current density reaches 10 mA/cm^2 at an overpotential of -0.21 V, corresponding to the HER overpotential required to sustain this geometric current density.

Following the introduction of 2.0 ppm Bi3+ into the electrolyte, the HER polarization curve exhibits a marked shift toward more negative potentials. The HER onset potential shifts by approximately 65 mV in the negative direction, and the overpotential required to achieve 10 mA/cm^2 increases to -0.30 V, representing an 85 mV increase in HER overpotential compared to the baseline system. At 80 mA/cm^2, the HER overpotential in the Bi3+-containing electrolyte is 142 mV more negative than in the pristine electrolyte. This substantial increase in the kinetic barrier for hydrogen evolution is the direct consequence of bismuth underpotential deposition on the graphite felt surface, which creates a high-overpotential metallic adlayer that kinetically inhibits proton reduction.

The dependence of HER suppression on Bi3+ concentration was systematically investigated. At 0.5 ppm Bi3+, the HER overpotential at 10 mA/cm^2 increases by 25 mV relative to the baseline. As the Bi3+ concentration increases to 1.0 ppm and 1.5 ppm, the HER overpotential increases by 48 mV and 68 mV respectively. The maximum HER suppression is achieved at 2.0 ppm Bi3+, with the 85 mV overpotential increase noted above. At 3.0 ppm Bi3+, the HER overpotential increase is 82 mV, marginally lower than the optimum at 2.0 ppm, which mirrors the trend observed in the Cr(III)/Cr(II) kinetic measurements. This concentration dependence strongly suggests that the HER suppression and the chromium redox catalysis share a common mechanistic origin in the surface-adsorbed bismuth species.

### 3.2 In-Situ Hydrogen Evolution Monitoring

To quantify the parasitic hydrogen evolution under conditions representative of actual battery operation, in-situ hydrogen gas detection was performed during galvanostatic polarization of graphite felt electrodes at applied current densities of 40, 80, 120, and 160 mA/cm^2 in electrolytes containing 0.5 M CrCl3 and varying Bi3+ concentrations. The H2 evolution rate was determined by gas chromatographic analysis of the headspace gas collected during controlled-potential electrolysis.

In the absence of Bi3+, the H2 evolution rate at 80 mA/cm^2 corresponds to a current efficiency loss of approximately 13% to parasitic hydrogen evolution, meaning that 13% of the applied current is diverted to HER rather than the desired Cr(III) reduction. At 40 mA/cm^2, this parasitic loss is lower at 8%, while at 120 mA/cm^2 and 160 mA/cm^2 it increases to 17% and 21% respectively, reflecting the increasing overpotential-driven thermodynamic driving force for HER at higher current densities.

Upon addition of 2.0 ppm Bi3+, the H2 evolution rate at 80 mA/cm^2 decreases dramatically, reducing the parasitic current loss to 3.5%. This represents a 73% reduction in hydrogen evolution compared to the baseline system. At 40 mA/cm^2, the parasitic loss is further suppressed to 1.8%, while at 120 mA/cm^2 and 160 mA/cm^2 it remains at 5.2% and 7.8% respectively. The significantly lower parasitic losses at all current densities confirm that the Bi3+-derived surface adlayer provides effective kinetic inhibition of HER across the practical operating range of the ICRFB. The remaining residual hydrogen evolution at high current densities likely reflects incomplete coverage of the graphite felt surface by the bismuth adlayer, particularly in regions of the porous electrode where mass transport limitations may lead to locally enhanced proton concentrations.

### 3.3 Mechanistic Insights from Surface Analysis

Post-mortem analysis of the graphite felt electrodes by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) provides direct evidence for the formation of a bismuth-containing surface layer on electrodes cycled in Bi3+-enriched electrolyte. SEM images reveal sub-micron bismuth crystallites distributed across the carbon fiber surfaces of electrodes retrieved from cells operated with 2.0 ppm Bi3+, with an estimated surface coverage of 35-45% based on image analysis. No bismuth signals are detected on electrodes from the baseline cell, confirming that the bismuth deposition is additive-mediated rather than originating from impurities. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements further confirm the presence of metallic bismuth (Bi 4f7/2 binding energy of 157.0 eV) on the electrode surface, indicating that Bi3+ is reduced to Bi(0) during the underpotential deposition process. The combination of catalytically active metallic bismuth sites for Cr(III)/Cr(II) electron transfer and the high HER overpotential of the bismuth surface accounts for the dual functionality observed experimentally.

## 4. Single Cell Performance Evaluation

### 4.1 Coulombic and Energy Efficiency as Functions of Bi3+ Concentration

Single ICRFB cells were assembled using graphite felt electrodes, a Nafion 115 membrane, and electrolytes composed of 1.0 M FeCl2 in 2.0 M HCl for the positive half-cell and 1.0 M CrCl3 in 2.0 M HCl for the negative half-cell. Bi3+ was added exclusively to the negative electrolyte at concentrations of 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, and 3.0 ppm. All cell tests were conducted at ambient temperature (25 °C) with an electrolyte flow rate of 100 mL/min.

The coulombic efficiency (CE) and energy efficiency (EE) of the cells were measured at a fixed current density of 80 mA/cm^2 over 20 conditioning cycles, and the average steady-state values are reported. The baseline cell without Bi3+ additive achieves a CE of 87% and an EE of 72% at 80 mA/cm^2. The relatively low CE is a direct consequence of the parasitic HER competing with the Cr(III)/Cr(II) charge transfer reaction, while the EE is further penalized by the high activation overpotential associated with the sluggish chromium redox kinetics.

With the progressive addition of Bi3+, both CE and EE exhibit substantial improvements, as shown in Figure 3. At 0.5 ppm Bi3+, the CE increases to 90% and the EE rises to 76%. At 1.0 ppm Bi3+, the CE reaches 92% and the EE improves to 81%. Further increasing the Bi3+ concentration to 1.5 ppm yields a CE of 93.5% and an EE of 85%. The optimal performance is achieved at 2.0 ppm Bi3+, where the cell delivers a CE of 94% and an EE of 87%, representing improvements of 7 percentage points in CE and 15 percentage points in EE relative to the baseline. At 3.0 ppm Bi3+, a marginal decline is observed, with the CE decreasing slightly to 93.8% and the EE to 86.2%, consistent with the subtle catalytic inhibition observed in the RDE measurements at this elevated concentration.

The EE improvement of 15 percentage points at the optimal 2.0 ppm Bi3+ concentration arises from the combined effects of HER suppression, which increases the CE by reducing parasitic current losses, and the accelerated Cr(III)/Cr(II) kinetics, which reduces the voltage efficiency penalty associated with activation overpotential. The voltage efficiency at 80 mA/cm^2 increases from 83% for the baseline cell to 93% for the cell with 2.0 ppm Bi3+, accounting for the majority of the EE improvement beyond the CE gain.

### 4.2 Current Density Dependence of Cell Performance

To evaluate the performance characteristics across the practical operating window, the cell with 2.0 ppm Bi3+ was tested at current densities of 40, 80, 120, and 160 mA/cm^2, and the results were compared against the baseline cell at the same current densities. At 40 mA/cm^2, the baseline cell achieves a CE of 92% and an EE of 80%, while the Bi3+-containing cell delivers a CE of 96% and an EE of 90%. At 80 mA/cm^2, the respective values are 87% CE and 72% EE for the baseline versus 94% CE and 87% EE for the Bi3+-enhanced cell. As the current density is increased to 120 mA/cm^2, the baseline cell performance degrades to 80% CE and 62% EE, whereas the Bi3+-containing cell maintains 91% CE and 80% EE. At the highest current density of 160 mA/cm^2, the baseline cell exhibits only 72% CE and 51% EE, while the Bi3+-enhanced cell achieves 87% CE and 72% EE.

These results demonstrate that the Bi3+ additive provides performance benefits across the entire current density range, with particularly pronounced advantages at elevated current densities where both HER parasitic losses and kinetic overpotentials are most severe. The ability to maintain an EE of 72% at 160 mA/cm^2 in the Bi3+-containing cell, equivalent to the baseline EE at only 80 mA/cm^2, indicates that the additive effectively doubles the accessible power density range for comparable energy efficiency. This characteristic is of significant practical importance for reducing the stack cost of ICRFB systems, as higher operating current densities enable lower electrode area requirements for a given power rating.

### 4.3 Comparison with Alternative Additive Strategies

The performance of the Bi3+ additive approach was benchmarked against two alternative strategies: a gold nanoparticle (Au NP) modified graphite felt electrode and a lead ion (Pb2+) electrolyte additive at 5.0 ppm. The Au NP-modified electrode achieved a CE of 93% and an EE of 85% at 80 mA/cm^2, comparable to but slightly lower than the Bi3+ additive system. The Pb2+ additive system achieved 91% CE and 82% EE at the same current density. While both alternative approaches provide meaningful improvements over the baseline, the Bi3+ additive at 2.0 ppm offers a superior combination of CE and EE performance. Furthermore, the substantially lower concentration requirement of Bi3+ (2.0 ppm) compared to Pb2+ (5.0 ppm), combined with the avoidance of precious metal usage in the Au NP system, positions the Bi3+ strategy as a more cost-effective approach for large-scale deployment.

## 5. Long-Term Cycling Stability and Degradation Analysis

### 5.1 Extended Cycling Performance

The long-term durability of the Bi3+-enhanced ICRFB was assessed through 200 continuous charge-discharge cycles at 80 mA/cm^2 with a voltage window of 0.8-1.4 V. The baseline cell and the cell with 2.0 ppm Bi3+ were cycled under identical conditions for direct comparison. For the baseline cell, the initial discharge capacity of 280 mAh exhibits a steady decline over the cycling period, reaching 212 mAh after 200 cycles. This corresponds to a capacity retention of 75.7% and an average capacity fade rate of 0.12% per cycle. The capacity fade is accompanied by a progressive decrease in CE from the initial 87% to 81% by cycle 200, indicating accelerating parasitic HER losses as the cell degrades.

In contrast, the cell with 2.0 ppm Bi3+ demonstrates markedly improved cycling stability. The initial discharge capacity of 295 mAh gradually decreases to 277 mAh after 200 cycles, corresponding to a capacity retention of 93.9% and an average fade rate of only 0.03% per cycle. The CE remains stable at 94 ± 1% throughout the entire cycling period, and the EE decreases only modestly from 87% to 84% over 200 cycles. The higher initial capacity of the Bi3+-containing cell compared to the baseline (295 mAh versus 280 mAh) reflects the improved CE during the initial conditioning cycles, which results in more complete utilization of the active species.

### 5.2 Degradation Mechanism Analysis

Post-cycling electrolyte analysis reveals distinctly different degradation signatures for the baseline and Bi3+-enhanced cells. In the baseline cell, the negative electrolyte shows a significant decrease in Cr(III) concentration from the initial 1.0 M to 0.82 M after 200 cycles, accompanied by a pH increase from 0.3 to 0.6 due to proton consumption in the HER. Gas chromatographic analysis of the headspace confirms cumulative hydrogen evolution of 18.6 mmol over 200 cycles, corresponding to a total charge loss of 3578 mAh to parasitic HER. This hydrogen accumulation leads to progressive state-of-charge imbalance between the positive and negative half-cells, which is the primary driver of capacity fade in the baseline ICRFB.

For the Bi3+-enhanced cell, the Cr(III) concentration decreases only slightly from 1.0 M to 0.96 M over 200 cycles, and the pH remains essentially stable at 0.3 ± 0.05. The cumulative hydrogen evolution is reduced to 4.2 mmol, representing a 77% reduction in total parasitic hydrogen generation compared to the baseline. The residual capacity fade of 0.03% per cycle in the Bi3+-containing cell is attributed to a combination of minor membrane crossover of iron and chromium species and slow oxidative degradation of the graphite felt electrode, rather than HER-driven electrolyte imbalance. These results confirm that the Bi3+ additive effectively mitigates the dominant degradation mechanism in ICRFBs by suppressing parasitic hydrogen evolution and maintaining electrolyte compositional stability over extended cycling.

### 5.3 Bi3+ Consumption and Replenishment

The concentration of Bi3+ in the negative electrolyte was monitored periodically during the 200-cycle test by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES). The initial Bi3+ concentration of 2.0 ppm gradually decreases to 1.6 ppm after 200 cycles, corresponding to an average consumption rate of 0.002 ppm per cycle. This gradual depletion is attributed to the incorporation of bismuth into the electrode material and minor crossover to the positive half-cell through the membrane. The relatively slow consumption rate suggests that periodic replenishment of Bi3+ at intervals of several hundred cycles would be sufficient to maintain the additive concentration near the optimal level, adding minimal operational cost to the ICRFB system.

## 6. Discussion

The results presented in this study demonstrate that Bi3+ ions function as an effective dual-action electrolyte additive in ICRFBs, simultaneously addressing the two most critical limitations of this battery chemistry: parasitic hydrogen evolution and sluggish Cr(III)/Cr(II) redox kinetics. The mechanistic basis for both effects originates from the underpotential deposition of Bi3+ on the graphite felt electrode surface, where metallic bismuth adatoms create catalytically active sites that facilitate chromium redox electron transfer while presenting a high kinetic barrier for proton reduction.

The optimal Bi3+ concentration of 2.0 ppm identified in this work represents a compromise between maximizing surface coverage of catalytically active bismuth species and avoiding excessive site blocking at higher concentrations. The observation of slightly diminished performance at 3.0 ppm suggests that an excess of bismuth species may partially occlude the carbon surface, reducing the effective electrochemically active surface area available for the Cr(III)/Cr(II) reaction. This concentration-dependent behavior highlights the importance of precise additive dosage in achieving optimal performance.

The comparison between the ICRFB with Bi3+ additive and the all-vanadium RFB reveals competitive efficiency metrics. At 80 mA/cm^2, the Bi3+-enhanced ICRFB achieves a CE of 94% and an EE of 87%, which approach the typical performance of VRFB systems operated under comparable conditions. Given the substantially lower electrolyte cost of the ICRFB chemistry, these efficiency levels suggest that the cost-performance gap between ICRFBs and VRFBs can be significantly narrowed through effective additive strategies. However, it should be noted that the long-term cycling stability of the Bi3+-enhanced ICRFB, while substantially improved over the baseline, still falls short of the multi-thousand-cycle stability demonstrated by commercial VRFB systems, and further research is needed to address remaining degradation mechanisms including membrane crossover and electrode oxidation.

The HER suppression mechanism identified in this work differs fundamentally from approaches based on electrolyte pH modification or the use of organic complexing agents. Unlike pH adjustment, which inherently limits the thermodynamic driving force for chromium reduction, the Bi3+ additive operates through a purely kinetic mechanism that selectively inhibits HER without compromising the Cr(III)/Cr(II) redox potential. This selectivity is a critical advantage, as it enables the full utilization of the thermodynamic cell voltage while minimizing parasitic losses. The dynamic nature of the bismuth adlayer, which is continuously replenished from the electrolyte bulk, also provides superior durability compared to static electrode coatings that may degrade through delamination or dissolution over extended operation.

## 7. Conclusion

This study has established Bi3+ as a highly effective electrolyte additive for iron-chromium redox flow batteries, achieving simultaneous suppression of hydrogen evolution and acceleration of Cr(III)/Cr(II) redox kinetics through an underpotential deposition mechanism on the graphite felt electrode surface. Key findings include: (1) the addition of 2.0 ppm Bi3+ increases the Cr(III)/Cr(II) standard electron transfer rate constant by a factor of 2.3, from 2.1 × 10^-3 cm/s to 4.8 × 10^-3 cm/s; (2) the HER overpotential at 80 mA/cm^2 increases by 85 mV in the presence of 2.0 ppm Bi3+, reducing parasitic current losses from 13% to 3.5%; (3) single-cell testing at 80 mA/cm^2 demonstrates a coulombic efficiency of 94% and an energy efficiency of 87% with the optimal Bi3+ concentration, compared to 87% CE and 72% EE for the baseline cell; (4) long-term cycling over 200 cycles at 80 mA/cm^2 reveals a capacity fade rate of only 0.03% per cycle with Bi3+ additive, versus 0.12% per cycle for the baseline, corresponding to a fourfold improvement in cycling stability. These results position Bi3+ electrolyte additive as a promising, cost-effective strategy for advancing the commercial viability of ICRFB technology for grid-scale energy storage applications.
