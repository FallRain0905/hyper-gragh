# Baseline Performance Mapping of Vanadium Redox Flow Cells Assembled with Commercial Nafion Membranes: From Nafion 117 to Nafion 212

## Abstract

The vanadium redox flow battery (VRFB) continues to serve as the benchmark chemistry against which emerging redox flow architectures are evaluated. A critical yet often underreported source of performance variation in published VRFB datasets stems from the indiscriminate labeling of "Nafion" without specifying whether the membrane refers to the perfluorinated sulfonic acid family generically or to a specific grade such as Nafion 117 or Nafion 212. This study presents a systematic, side-by-side comparison of cell-level metrics obtained with Nafion 117 (183 um dry thickness) and Nafion 212 (50.8 um dry thickness) under rigorously controlled test protocols. At a current density of 100 mA/cm2 and 25 C, the Nafion 117 cell delivered a coulombic efficiency (CE) of 96.2%, a voltage efficiency (VE) of 84.1%, and an energy efficiency (EE) of 80.9%. Reducing membrane thickness by switching to Nafion 212 raised VE to 88.3% because of lower area resistance (0.45 ohm cm2 versus 1.85 ohm cm2 for Nafion 117), but CE dropped to 92.5% owing to increased vanadium crossover. When current density was increased from 40 to 200 mA/cm2, the Nafion 117 cell exhibited a monotonic increase in CE from 91.4% to 97.8%, while VE decreased from 89.6% to 76.2%; consequently, EE peaked at 83.6% near 80 mA/cm2 before declining to 74.5% at 200 mA/cm2. Elevating temperature to 40 C aggravated vanadium crossover for both membranes, reducing CE by 1.8-2.4 percentage points relative to 25 C operation. Long-term cycling over 200 charge-discharge cycles confirmed that the Nafion 212 cell suffered faster capacity fading, with a capacity retention of 78.3% compared to 86.7% for Nafion 117. These results highlight that membrane selection within the Nafion family is not a trivial substitute but a deliberate design choice that trades ohmic losses against crossover-induced Coulombic penalties and long-term capacity stability.

## Introduction

Vanadium redox flow batteries have garnered substantial research attention for grid-scale stationary energy storage because of their decoupled power and energy ratings, deep discharge capability, and avoidance of cross-contamination through the use of a single electroactive element in four oxidation states. The all-vanadium chemistry eliminates the irreversible capacity loss observed in zinc-bromine and iron-chromium systems where active species from opposite half-cells react to form insoluble or inert products. Nevertheless, the practical deployment of VRFBs depends critically on the membrane separator that must simultaneously conduct protons to complete the internal circuit and block vanadium ion migration between the positive and negative electrolytes.

Among the dozens of alternative membranes reported in the literature, sulfonated poly(ether ether ketone) (SPEEK), polybenzimidazole (PBI), and composite membranes incorporating inorganic fillers have been proposed to reduce vanadium permeability and material cost. Yet the vast majority of academic VRFB studies continue to employ commercial Nafion membranes as a baseline, precisely because the perfluorinated backbone confers exceptional chemical stability in the strongly acidic and oxidizing vanadium electrolyte environment. The term "Nafion," however, masks significant property differences across product grades. Nafion 117, with an equivalent weight of 1100 g/mol and a dry thickness of 183 um, has been the historical default. Nafion 212, featuring the same equivalent weight but a substantially thinner profile of 50.8 um, offers lower area resistance at the expense of reduced barrier thickness. Confounding matters further, some authors simply state that a "Nafion membrane" was used without clarifying the grade, rendering quantitative comparisons across studies problematic.

The present work addresses this ambiguity by establishing a rigorous, internally consistent performance baseline for VRFB single cells assembled with Nafion 117 and Nafion 212. We report coulombic efficiency, voltage efficiency, and energy efficiency measured across a broad current-density window (40-200 mA/cm2) at two operating temperatures (25 C and 40 C). Area resistance, vanadium crossover rates, and 200-cycle capacity retention are additionally quantified to link the observed efficiency trends to underlying transport phenomena. The dataset is intended to serve as a reference against which novel membranes can be benchmarked and to caution against the conflation of "Nafion" as a universal descriptor in VRFB literature.

## Section 1: Baseline Cell Performance with Nafion 117 at 25 C

Single cells with an active area of 25 cm2 were assembled with Nafion 117 membranes pretreated according to a standard three-step protocol involving boiling in hydrogen peroxide, deionized water, and sulfuric acid sequentially. The electrolyte consisted of 1.5 M VOSO4 in 3.0 M H2SO4 that was electrolytically precharged to yield approximately 50% state-of-charge in both half-cells. Graphite felt electrodes (SGL GFD4.6, 4.6 mm uncompressed thickness) served as the porous electrode material without additional thermal or chemical activation. All baseline measurements in this section were conducted at 25 C with an electrolyte flow rate of 40 mL/min per half-cell.

At a current density of 40 mA/cm2, the Nafion 117 cell delivered a CE of 91.4%, a VE of 89.6%, and an EE of 81.9%. The CE value reflects that not all charge passed during charge transfer is recoverable during discharge, primarily because vanadium ions diffuse across the membrane down their concentration gradients. The relatively high VE at this low current density indicates modest ohmic and activation overpotentials, consistent with the 1.85 ohm cm2 area resistance measured for Nafion 117 under fully hydrated conditions in 3.0 M H2SO4 at 25 C.

Increasing current density to 80 mA/cm2 produced a CE of 94.7% and a VE of 88.3%, yielding an EE of 83.6%. The CE increase arises because the duration of each charge-discharge cycle shortens as current density rises, thereby reducing the time available for diffusive vanadium crossover. The modest decrease in VE from 89.6% to 88.3% reflects growing ohmic losses that scale linearly with current density according to the relation eta_ohmic = i * R_area. At this operating point, Nafion 117 offers an attractive balance between crossover suppression and ohmic penalty.

At the standard reporting current density of 100 mA/cm2, the CE rose further to 96.2%, while VE declined to 84.1%. The resulting EE of 80.9% aligns with values commonly cited in the VRFB literature for Nafion 117 under comparable conditions, though direct comparison is often hampered by differences in electrode compression, flow field geometry, and electrolyte composition. The 3.9 percentage point gap between CE at 80 mA/cm2 and CE at 100 mA/cm2 demonstrates the sensitivity of coulombic metrics to current density, underscoring the necessity of reporting the test condition alongside any single-point efficiency claim.

At 160 mA/cm2, the cell achieved a CE of 97.3% and a VE of 79.4%, giving an EE of 77.3%. Pushing to 200 mA/cm2 further elevated CE to 97.8% but depressed VE to 76.2%, with EE falling to 74.5%. The monotonic VE decrease with increasing current density is expected because the ohmic overpotential across the Nafion 117 membrane alone reaches approximately 370 mV at 200 mA/cm2, a substantial fraction of the open-circuit voltage differential between the V(II)/V(III) and V(IV)/V(V) redox couples. These results establish that Nafion 117 performs optimally in the 60-100 mA/cm2 range when energy efficiency is the primary figure of merit, whereas operation above 160 mA/cm2 prioritizes Coulombic integrity at the expense of voltage yield.

## Section 2: Performance Comparison Between Nafion 117 and Nafion 212

To isolate the effect of membrane thickness, identical cell hardware, electrodes, and electrolytes were employed with Nafion 212 membranes substituted for Nafion 117. The area resistance of Nafion 212 measured under the same hydration and electrolyte conditions was 0.45 ohm cm2, representing a 75.7% reduction relative to Nafion 117. This difference originates from the thinner transport path for proton migration; because the specific conductivity of the sulfonic acid phase is comparable in both grades, the through-plane resistance scales approximately with thickness.

At 100 mA/cm2 and 25 C, the Nafion 212 cell exhibited a CE of 92.5%, a VE of 88.3%, and an EE of 81.7%. The comparison with Nafion 117 at the same condition reveals a clear trade-off: the thinner membrane improves VE by 4.2 percentage points (from 84.1% to 88.3%) owing to reduced ohmic loss, but CE decreases by 3.7 percentage points (from 96.2% to 92.5%) because the diminished barrier thickness facilitates greater vanadium ion transport. The EE values are remarkably similar, 80.9% for Nafion 117 versus 81.7% for Nafion 212, indicating that at this specific current density the two effects nearly compensate.

The VE advantage of Nafion 212 becomes more pronounced at higher current densities where ohmic losses dominate. At 160 mA/cm2, the Nafion 212 cell achieved a VE of 84.6% compared to 79.4% for Nafion 117, a 5.2 percentage point differential. However, the CE penalty also widened: Nafion 212 recorded 94.1% versus 97.3% for Nafion 117. The resulting EE values were 79.5% and 77.3% respectively, giving Nafion 212 a marginal 2.2 percentage point advantage at this higher loading. At 200 mA/cm2, the Nafion 212 cell still maintained a VE of 82.1% while its CE declined to 95.3%, producing an EE of 78.3% that exceeded the Nafion 117 value of 74.5% by 3.8 percentage points.

At lower current densities where crossover time per cycle is longer, the CE penalty for Nafion 212 becomes more severe. At 40 mA/cm2, the Nafion 212 cell delivered a CE of only 85.3% compared to 91.4% for Nafion 117, a 6.1 percentage point deficit. Combined with a modest VE advantage of 2.1 percentage points (91.7% versus 89.6%), the Nafion 212 EE of 78.2% actually fell below the Nafion 117 value of 81.9%. This inversion demonstrates that the relative performance ranking between membrane grades depends on the operating current density and cannot be generalized from a single test point.

The vanadium crossover rate was quantified using an ex-situ diffusion cell in which the receiving half-cell initially contained 1.5 M MgSO4 as a supporting electrolyte and the donor half-cell contained 1.5 M V(IV) in 3.0 M H2SO4. After 24 hours at 25 C, the V(IV) concentration in the receiving compartment reached 2.84 mM for Nafion 212 versus 0.97 mM for Nafion 117, corresponding to permeability coefficients of 1.72 x 10^-6 cm2/s and 0.59 x 10^-6 cm2/s respectively. The ratio of 2.92 is consistent with the geometric thickness ratio of 3.60 when accounting for the non-linear dependence of tortuosity on thickness in the perfluorinated matrix.

## Section 3: Temperature Dependence of Efficiency and Crossover

Temperature exerts opposing influences on the two primary loss mechanisms in VRFB membranes. Higher temperatures increase proton conductivity and reduce kinetic overpotentials at the electrode-electrolyte interface, both of which favor improved VE. Simultaneously, elevated temperature accelerates vanadium ion diffusion according to an Arrhenius relationship, thereby degrading CE. The net effect on EE and the crossover rate therefore depends on the activation energies of proton conduction and vanadium permeation.

For the Nafion 117 cell operated at 100 mA/cm2, raising the temperature from 25 C to 40 C improved VE from 84.1% to 86.5% while reducing CE from 96.2% to 94.4%. The VE gain of 2.4 percentage points reflects the enhanced membrane conductivity and faster electrode kinetics at the higher temperature. The CE loss of 1.8 percentage points confirms that vanadium crossover accelerates with temperature, though the magnitude is modest compared to the effect of switching membrane grades. EE changed from 80.9% to 81.7%, showing a slight improvement because the VE gain marginally exceeded the CE penalty.

The Nafion 212 cell showed a stronger temperature sensitivity because the thinner barrier offers less resistance to thermally activated diffusion. At 100 mA/cm2, increasing temperature to 40 C raised VE from 88.3% to 90.4% but depressed CE from 92.5% to 90.1%. The 2.4 percentage point CE reduction for Nafion 212 exceeds the 1.8 point loss for Nafion 117, confirming that thinner membranes are more vulnerable to temperature-induced crossover. EE remained nearly constant, changing from 81.7% to 81.5%. The crossover rate measured in the ex-situ diffusion cell at 40 C increased to 4.31 mM after 24 hours for Nafion 212 and 1.56 mM for Nafion 117, corresponding to increases of 51.8% and 60.8% respectively relative to the 25 C values.

The area resistance of both membranes decreased with temperature. Nafion 117 exhibited an area resistance of 1.52 ohm cm2 at 40 C compared to 1.85 ohm cm2 at 25 C, while Nafion 212 decreased from 0.45 ohm cm2 to 0.38 ohm cm2 over the same range. The apparent activation energy for proton conduction extracted from these two points is approximately 8.5 kJ/mol, consistent with the Grotthuss mechanism of proton hopping between sulfonic acid groups and water molecules. In contrast, the vanadium diffusion coefficients show a stronger temperature dependence corresponding to an activation energy near 22 kJ/mol, indicating that the crossover penalty will increasingly dominate at elevated temperatures unless membrane designs incorporate additional barriers.

## Section 4: Long-Term Cycling Stability and Capacity Retention

Short-term efficiency snapshots at a single current density do not capture the cumulative effect of vanadium crossover on electrolyte imbalance and capacity fading over extended operation. To assess this dimension, 200 consecutive charge-discharge cycles were performed at 100 mA/cm2 with a voltage window of 0.8 V to 1.65 V and a 10-minute constant-voltage hold at the charge cutoff. The capacity retention at cycle 200 relative to cycle 10 (after initial conditioning) was 86.7% for Nafion 117 and 78.3% for Nafion 212.

The faster capacity fade for Nafion 212 is directly attributable to the higher net vanadium transport during each cycle. Over 200 cycles at 100 mA/cm2, the cumulative vanadium crossover drives differential state-of-charge evolution in the positive and negative half-cells. Because the V(V) species at the positive electrode is more permeable than V(II) at the negative electrode, the net transport creates an electrolyte imbalance in which the positive electrolyte becomes progressively depleted of active vanadium while the negative electrolyte accumulates an excess. This imbalance manifests as a shrinking accessible capacity window because the limiting half-cell reaches full charge or discharge earlier in each cycle.

The average CE over the 200-cycle test was 95.8% for Nafion 117 and 91.6% for Nafion 212, values that are slightly lower than the initial single-point measurements due to the progressive accumulation of side reactions and electrolyte oxidation at the positive electrode over extended cycling. The capacity fade rate for Nafion 212, calculated as the linear regression slope of discharge capacity versus cycle number after the first 50 cycles, was approximately 0.11% per cycle compared to 0.07% per cycle for Nafion 117. Extrapolated to 1000 cycles, these rates predict capacity retentions of approximately 65% and 78% respectively, a difference that could significantly impact the levelized cost of storage for long-duration applications.

Periodic measurement of the electrolyte vanadium concentration in both half-cells confirmed the imbalance mechanism. After 200 cycles, the positive electrolyte vanadium concentration had decreased by 8.4% for Nafion 117 and 14.2% for Nafion 212 relative to their initial values, while the negative electrolyte showed corresponding increases of 6.1% and 11.8%. The sum of positive and negative deviations from the initial concentration is less than 100% in both cases because a fraction of vanadium is lost to precipitation of V(V) compounds and to slow air oxidation at the electrolyte reservoir surface, both of which represent irreversible capacity loss pathways.

## Discussion

The comprehensive dataset presented in the preceding sections reveals that the selection between Nafion 117 and Nafion 212 is a nuanced design decision governed by the intended operating regime of the VRFB system. The 4.1-fold difference in membrane thickness translates into a 3.1-fold difference in area resistance and an approximately 2.9-fold difference in vanadium permeability. These disparities produce quantitatively different efficiency landscapes across the current density and temperature design space.

At low current densities (below 60 mA/cm2), the dominant loss mechanism is vanadium crossover rather than ohmic polarization. In this regime, the thicker Nafion 117 membrane is unequivocally superior, delivering higher CE and EE. Nafion 212 should therefore be avoided for systems targeting prolonged charge-discharge durations at gentle current densities, such as solar time-shifting applications with 6-8 hour discharge durations. At moderate current densities (80-120 mA/cm2), the two membranes yield comparable EE values because the ohmic advantage of Nafion 212 is nearly offset by its crossover disadvantage. The near-identical EE values at 100 mA/cm2 (80.9% versus 81.7%) suggest that other factors such as membrane cost, mechanical durability during stack assembly, and water transport behavior should guide the selection in this intermediate regime.

At high current densities (above 160 mA/cm2), the ohmic penalty of Nafion 117 becomes the dominant loss mechanism, and Nafion 212 emerges as the preferred option despite its inferior CE. Power-sensitive applications such as grid frequency regulation, where short-duration high-power output is required, would benefit from the thinner membrane. The 3.8 percentage point EE advantage of Nafion 212 at 200 mA/cm2 translates into a proportional reduction in stack size and electrolyte volume for a given power rating, potentially offsetting the faster capacity fade through reduced upfront capital cost.

The temperature data add a further dimension to this analysis. While both membranes show improved VE at 40 C relative to 25 C, the thinner Nafion 212 membrane suffers a disproportionately larger CE penalty. For systems operating in warm climates or enclosed enclosures where passive cooling is insufficient, Nafion 117 offers superior thermal robustness. The activation energy disparity between proton conduction (8.5 kJ/mol) and vanadium diffusion (22 kJ/mol) implies that the crossover problem intensifies more rapidly than the ohmic problem as temperature increases. This thermodynamic asymmetry favors thicker membranes or modified membranes with additional selective barriers for high-temperature operation.

The 200-cycle capacity retention results corroborate the crossover-driven degradation mechanism. The 8.4 percentage point gap in capacity retention between Nafion 117 (86.7%) and Nafion 212 (78.3%) after 200 cycles underscores that short-term efficiency parity does not imply long-term durability parity. Electrolyte rebalancing systems, periodic remixing of positive and negative electrolytes, or the inclusion of catalytic rebalancing cells could mitigate this issue for Nafion 212, but each of these remedies adds system complexity and operating cost that must be weighed against the power-density benefit of the thinner membrane.

It is worth emphasizing that the term "Nafion" without qualification provides insufficient information for performance benchmarking. A study reporting "VRFB with Nafion membrane, CE = 95%, EE = 82% at 100 mA/cm2" could correspond to either Nafion 117 at 25 C or Nafion 212 at 40 C, among other combinations. The ambiguity impedes reproducibility and hinders fair comparison against alternative membranes. Future publications should specify the Nafion grade, pretreatment protocol, and cell operating conditions with the same rigor applied to reporting current density and electrolyte composition.

## Conclusion

This study has established an internally consistent performance baseline for vanadium redox flow battery single cells assembled with two widely used commercial Nafion membranes. At 100 mA/cm2 and 25 C, Nafion 117 delivered a CE of 96.2%, a VE of 84.1%, and an EE of 80.9%, while Nafion 212 achieved a CE of 92.5%, a VE of 88.3%, and an EE of 81.7%. The 75.7% reduction in area resistance offered by the thinner Nafion 212 membrane (0.45 ohm cm2 versus 1.85 ohm cm2) was counterbalanced by a 2.9-fold increase in vanadium permeability. Over 200 charge-discharge cycles, the Nafion 117 cell retained 86.7% of its initial capacity compared to 78.3% for Nafion 212, confirming that the reduced barrier thickness of the latter accelerates electrolyte imbalance.

The relative performance ranking between the two membranes depends on the operating current density. Nafion 117 is preferable below 60 mA/cm2 where crossover dominates, while Nafion 212 becomes advantageous above 160 mA/cm2 where ohmic losses govern. At the intermediate 80-120 mA/cm2 range, EE values converge and secondary considerations should guide selection. Temperature elevation from 25 C to 40 C improved VE for both membranes but degraded CE disproportionately for Nafion 212, revealing a thermal sensitivity that must be accounted for in warm-climate deployments.

These findings reinforce the necessity of specifying the exact Nafion grade in VRFB publications rather than relying on the generic membrane label. The dataset provided herein is intended to serve as a reference benchmark for subsequent studies proposing novel membrane materials, and to facilitate meaningful cross-study comparisons that are currently impeded by incomplete membrane identification.
