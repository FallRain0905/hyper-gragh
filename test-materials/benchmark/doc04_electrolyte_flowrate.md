# Influence of Electrolyte Concentration and Flow Rate on Performance Metrics in Vanadium Redox Flow Batteries

## Abstract

The energy efficiency and practical energy density of vanadium redox flow batteries (VRFBs) are governed by a complex interplay between electrolyte composition, hydrodynamic conditions, and electrochemical kinetics. This study systematically investigates how vanadium concentration, sulfuric acid concentration, and electrolyte flow rate affect coulombic efficiency (CE), voltage efficiency (VE), energy efficiency (EE), concentration polarization, and net system-level energy density. Single-cell tests were conducted at current densities of 50, 100, and 200 mA/cm² using electrolytes containing 1.2–2.0 mol/L total vanadium dissolved in 2.0–5.0 mol/L H₂SO₄ support solution, with flow rates ranging from 10 to 100 mL/min. Increasing vanadium concentration from 1.2 to 1.6 mol/L raised the theoretical volumetric energy density from 19.3 to 25.7 Wh/L and improved practical EE at 100 mA/cm² from 78.2% to 83.5%, though further increases to 2.0 mol/L were accompanied by elevated viscosity and diminished mass-transfer coefficients. Sulfuric acid concentration exhibited a non-monotonic effect: raising H₂SO₄ from 2.0 to 4.0 mol/L enhanced VE by stabilizing ionic conductivity and suppressing precipitation, whereas 5.0 mol/L acid promoted excessive hydrogen evolution and reduced CE from 97.1% to 93.8%. Flow rate optimization revealed that increasing from 10 to 40 mL/min mitigated concentration polarization and raised VE by 4.2 percentage points, but beyond 60 mL/min the incremental gain was outweighed by parasitic pumping losses, causing net system efficiency to decline. These findings demonstrate that maximizing energy density requires simultaneous optimization of all three parameters rather than isolated maximization of any single variable.

## 1. Introduction

Vanadium redox flow batteries have attracted considerable research attention as a promising technology for grid-scale energy storage due to their decoupled power and energy ratings, long cycle life, and flexible capacity scaling. The active material in a VRFB consists of vanadium ions at four different oxidation states (V²⁺, V³⁺, VO²⁺, VO₂⁺) dissolved in an aqueous sulfuric acid solution, with the electrochemical reactions occurring at porous carbon felt electrodes separated by an ion-exchange membrane. Despite these inherent advantages, the relatively low energy density of VRFBs compared to lithium-ion systems remains a persistent barrier to broader deployment in space-constrained applications.

The theoretical volumetric energy density of a VRFB is determined primarily by the concentration of the active vanadium species and the cell voltage, which depends on the standard electrode potentials of the V²⁺/V³⁺ and VO²⁺/VO₂⁺ redox couples. In principle, increasing the vanadium concentration should directly increase the energy density; however, the solubility and stability of vanadium species in sulfuric acid impose practical upper limits. At concentrations above approximately 1.8–2.0 mol/L and temperatures below 15°C, V(II) and V(V) species are prone to precipitation as VOSO₄·5H₂O and V₂O₅ respectively, restricting the usable concentration window and necessitating careful thermal management.

The supporting electrolyte concentration, specifically the H₂SO₄ concentration, plays an equally critical though sometimes underappreciated role. Sulfuric acid provides the proton conductivity required for charge compensation during cell operation, and its concentration affects not only the solution conductivity but also the speciation and stability of vanadium ions. At low acid concentrations, the limited proton availability increases ohmic resistance and can lead to lower voltage efficiency, particularly at elevated current densities. Conversely, excessively high acid concentrations promote competitive hydrogen evolution at the negative electrode, reducing coulombic efficiency and potentially destabilizing the vanadium ion equilibrium.

Beyond electrolyte chemistry, the flow rate of electrolyte through the cell stack represents a key operational parameter that governs the trade-off between electrochemical performance and auxiliary energy consumption. At low flow rates, insufficient replenishment of reactants at the electrode surface leads to concentration polarization, causing premature voltage decay during discharge and reduced voltage efficiency. Increasing the flow rate enhances mass transfer and reduces the diffusion boundary layer thickness, but simultaneously raises pumping power requirements. The net effect on system-level energy efficiency depends on the balance between these competing factors, and the optimal flow rate is expected to vary with current density and electrolyte composition.

While numerous studies have examined individual aspects of VRFB electrolyte formulation or flow-field design, relatively few have systematically quantified the coupled effects of vanadium concentration, sulfuric acid concentration, and flow rate on a consistent set of performance metrics including CE, VE, EE, concentration polarization overpotential, and practical energy density. The present work addresses this gap through an integrated parametric study, with the aim of establishing experimentally grounded design guidelines for electrolyte composition and flow conditions that maximize deliverable energy density while maintaining high round-trip energy efficiency.

## 2. Experimental Methods

All electrochemical tests were performed using a single-cell VRFB with an active area of 25 cm². The cell comprised graphite plate current collectors with serpentine flow fields, carbon felt electrodes (SGL GFD 4.6 EA) compressed to 20% thickness, and a Nafion 117 membrane pretreated according to standard procedures. Electrolytes were prepared by dissolving VOSO₄·xH₂O in sulfuric acid solutions of specified concentration, followed by electrolytic pre-charging to produce equal volumes of V²⁺/V³⁺ negative electrolyte and VO²⁺/VO₂⁺ positive electrolyte. Unless otherwise specified, each half-cell electrolyte volume was 50 mL.

Vanadium concentrations of 1.2, 1.5, 1.6, 1.8, and 2.0 mol/L were evaluated, with a selected set of experiments extended to 3.0 mol/L for stability assessment. Sulfuric acid concentrations ranged from 2.0 to 5.0 mol/L total sulfate, encompassing the typical operational window reported in the literature. Electrolyte flow rates of 10, 20, 40, 60, and 100 mL/min were controlled using peristaltic pumps with calibrated flow meters. Cell testing was conducted at constant current densities of 50, 100, and 200 mA/cm² within a voltage window of 0.8–1.65 V using a battery cycler. Coulombic efficiency was calculated as the ratio of discharge capacity to charge capacity, voltage efficiency as the ratio of average discharge voltage to average charge voltage, and energy efficiency as the product of CE and VE. Concentration polarization overpotential was estimated from the difference between the measured cell voltage at a given state of charge and the theoretical equilibrium voltage corrected for ohmic losses. Pumping power was measured directly using in-line pressure sensors and flow meters, and system-level energy efficiency was computed by subtracting the integrated pumping energy from the gross electrical energy output.

## 3. Results and Discussion

### 3.1 Effect of Vanadium Concentration on Energy Density and Efficiency

The theoretical volumetric energy density, calculated from the Nernst equation and the total vanadium concentration assuming complete utilization, increased linearly from 19.3 Wh/L at 1.2 mol/L to 25.7 Wh/L at 1.6 mol/L and further to 32.1 Wh/L at 2.0 mol/L. However, the practical energy density measured at 80% state-of-charge utilization was substantially lower, accounting for incomplete reactant conversion, ohmic losses, and concentration polarization. At 100 mA/cm² and a flow rate of 40 mL/min with 3.0 mol/L H₂SO₄, the practical energy density rose from 14.8 Wh/L at 1.2 mol/L vanadium to 20.3 Wh/L at 1.6 mol/L, representing only 66–79% of the theoretical values. The gap between theoretical and practical energy density widened at higher concentrations: at 2.0 mol/L vanadium, the practical energy density reached 22.1 Wh/L, merely 69% of the theoretical 32.1 Wh/L, indicating that transport and kinetic limitations become increasingly significant as the concentration increases.

Coulombic efficiency remained consistently high across the tested vanadium concentrations, varying between 96.2% and 97.5%, with the slight decrease at higher concentrations attributable to enhanced vanadium crossover driven by larger concentration gradients across the membrane. Voltage efficiency, in contrast, showed a more pronounced dependence on vanadium concentration. At 100 mA/cm², VE increased from 81.3% at 1.2 mol/L to 85.6% at 1.6 mol/L, primarily due to the reduced relative impact of fixed overpotential losses at higher operating voltages associated with greater Nernstian potential differences. Beyond 1.6 mol/L, however, VE declined slightly to 84.9% at 2.0 mol/L, as the elevated viscosity of the concentrated electrolyte—rising from 3.2 mPa·s at 1.2 mol/L to 7.8 mPa·s at 2.0 mol/L at 25°C—impeded mass transfer and increased the concentration polarization overpotential.

Energy efficiency followed the combined trends of CE and VE, peaking at 83.5% for 1.6 mol/L vanadium at 100 mA/cm² and 40 mL/min, compared to 78.2% for 1.2 mol/L and 82.8% for 2.0 mol/L. At the higher current density of 200 mA/cm², the optimal concentration shifted to 1.5 mol/L, where an EE of 76.4% was recorded, surpassing the 72.1% obtained at 1.6 mol/L and the 68.9% at 2.0 mol/L. This shift reflects the growing importance of mass-transfer limitations at elevated current densities, where the higher viscosity of concentrated electrolytes disproportionately penalizes performance. At 50 mA/cm², the lower current density minimized concentration polarization effects, and the highest tested concentration of 2.0 mol/L yielded the maximum EE of 86.1%, suggesting that the optimal vanadium concentration is current-density-dependent and that system designers must match electrolyte formulation to anticipated operating conditions.

Long-term stability testing at 3.0 mol/L vanadium in 3.0 mol/L H₂SO₄ revealed gradual precipitation of V(V) species at the positive electrode after approximately 72 hours of continuous cycling at 25°C, with CE declining from an initial 95.8% to 89.3% over 50 cycles. This observation confirms that while higher vanadium concentrations offer attractive theoretical energy density gains, the practical stability window is constrained by precipitation kinetics and operating temperature.

### 3.2 Role of Sulfuric Acid Concentration in Determining Efficiency and Stability

The sulfuric acid concentration profoundly influenced both the ionic conductivity of the electrolyte and the side reaction behavior at the negative electrode. At a fixed vanadium concentration of 1.6 mol/L and flow rate of 40 mL/min, increasing H₂SO₄ concentration from 2.0 to 4.0 mol/L raised the solution conductivity from 320 to 480 mS/cm at 25°C and improved VE at 100 mA/cm² from 82.1% to 86.2%. This improvement stems from enhanced proton transport, which reduces ohmic resistance and facilitates charge compensation during the V²⁺/V³⁺ redox reaction. The reduced ohmic overpotential at 4.0 mol/L H₂SO₄ corresponded to a cell resistance decrease from 1.85 Ω·cm² to 1.42 Ω·cm², as determined by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy.

Further increasing the acid concentration to 5.0 mol/L, however, produced diminishing and ultimately negative returns. While conductivity continued to increase marginally to 510 mS/cm, VE actually decreased to 85.1%, and CE dropped sharply from 97.1% to 93.8%. The decline in CE was accompanied by visible gas evolution at the negative electrode during charging, consistent with enhanced hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) kinetics in strongly acidic media. The competitive HER not only represents a direct loss of charge efficiency but also alters the electrolyte pH balance over extended cycling, potentially destabilizing vanadium speciation and promoting hydrolysis precipitation.

The non-monotonic dependence of energy efficiency on H₂SO₄ concentration resulted in a clear optimum at 4.0 mol/L, where EE reached 83.7% at 100 mA/cm², compared to 79.8% at 2.0 mol/L, 83.5% at 3.0 mol/L, and 79.7% at 5.0 mol/L. Interestingly, the relative penalty of low acid concentration was more severe at elevated current densities: at 200 mA/cm², reducing H₂SO₄ from 4.0 to 2.0 mol/L caused VE to fall from 79.8% to 71.4%, a decrement of 8.4 percentage points, whereas at 50 mA/cm² the corresponding decrease was only 3.1 percentage points (from 90.2% to 87.1%). This current-density sensitivity arises because ohmic overpotential scales linearly with current density, making high-conductivity electrolytes progressively more important as the operating power density increases.

Temperature-dependent precipitation tests showed that the stability of 1.6 mol/L vanadium electrolytes against V(V) precipitation improved with increasing acid concentration up to 4.0 mol/L, with the onset temperature for visible precipitation decreasing from 28°C at 2.0 mol/L H₂SO₄ to 12°C at 4.0 mol/L. At 5.0 mol/L, the precipitation temperature remained similar to 4.0 mol/L, but the HER-induced pH drift over cycling reintroduced stability risks, suggesting that 4.0 mol/L represents a practical upper bound for H₂SO₄ concentration in long-term operation. These results underscore that electrolyte composition optimization requires balancing conductivity enhancement against side reaction suppression, and that the frequently cited "higher acid is better" guideline must be qualified by HER considerations.

### 3.3 Flow Rate Optimization: Trade-offs Between Mass Transfer and Pumping Loss

The electrolyte flow rate governed the balance between electrochemical performance and parasitic energy consumption in a manner that varied substantially with current density and electrolyte concentration. At 1.6 mol/L vanadium, 3.0 mol/L H₂SO₄, and 100 mA/cm², increasing the flow rate from 10 to 20 mL/min raised VE from 81.4% to 84.1%, and further to 85.6% at 40 mL/min. This improvement reflects enhanced convective mass transfer of vanadium ions to the electrode surface, which reduces the concentration difference between the bulk electrolyte and the electrode–electrolyte interface. The concentration polarization overpotential, estimated from voltage decay analysis, decreased from 68 mV at 10 mL/min to 31 mV at 40 mL/min, indicating that the low-flow condition imposed a significant transport limitation.

Between 40 and 60 mL/min, the incremental gain in VE diminished: at 60 mL/min, VE reached 86.3%, only 0.7 percentage points higher than at 40 mL/min. By 100 mL/min, VE had essentially plateaued at 86.5%, suggesting that above 40 mL/min the electrode surface concentration approaches the bulk concentration and further flow increases yield negligible electrochemical benefit. The mass-transfer coefficient, calculated from the limiting current behavior, increased from 2.1 × 10⁻⁵ m/s at 10 mL/min to 4.3 × 10⁻⁵ m/s at 40 mL/min, but only to 4.7 × 10⁻⁵ m/s at 60 mL/min and 4.9 × 10⁻⁵ m/s at 100 mL/min, confirming the asymptotic approach to a mass-transfer limit imposed by the porous electrode structure rather than the external flow rate alone.

Pumping power, however, increased approximately with the square of flow rate due to the dominant pressure-drop contribution from the porous carbon felt and serpentine flow channels. The measured pumping power rose from 12 mW at 10 mL/min to 48 mW at 40 mL/min, 108 mW at 60 mL/min, and 300 mW at 100 mL/min. When expressed as a fraction of the gross electrical power output, the parasitic pumping loss increased from 0.6% at 10 mL/min to 2.4% at 40 mL/min, 5.4% at 60 mL/min, and 15.0% at 100 mL/min. The net system energy efficiency, defined as gross EE minus the pumping energy penalty, exhibited a clear maximum at 40 mL/min, where the net EE was 81.5%, compared to 78.0% at 10 mL/min, 80.9% at 60 mL/min, and 71.5% at 100 mL/min.

This optimum shifted with current density. At 200 mA/cm², the higher reactant flux requirement moved the optimal flow rate to 60 mL/min, where net EE peaked at 73.2%, compared to 68.9% at 40 mL/min and 64.1% at 100 mL/min. At 50 mA/cm², in contrast, the lower polarization sensitivity meant that even 20 mL/min was sufficient, yielding a net EE of 84.7% that was only marginally below the 85.0% at 40 mL/min, making the lower flow rate attractive for low-power applications where minimizing auxiliary energy is paramount. These results demonstrate that flow rate optimization cannot be performed independently of the intended operating regime and that the frequently recommended "high flow rate" strategy must be evaluated against its pumping cost in the context of specific current density targets.

### 3.4 Coupled Parameter Interactions and System-Level Implications

The individual parameter studies revealed that vanadium concentration, sulfuric acid concentration, and flow rate interact in non-trivial ways that preclude simple additive optimization. For instance, the viscosity increase associated with high vanadium concentrations amplifies the pressure drop and hence the pumping penalty at any given flow rate. At 2.0 mol/L vanadium and 100 mL/min, the pumping power reached 420 mW, 40% higher than the 300 mW measured at 1.6 mol/L under identical hydrodynamic conditions, because the viscosity rose from 5.1 mPa·s to 7.8 mPa·s. This viscosity-mediated coupling shifts the net EE optimum to lower flow rates for more concentrated electrolytes: at 2.0 mol/L, the optimal flow rate was 40 mL/min rather than 60 mL/min, with a corresponding net EE of 78.9% compared to 82.8% at 1.6 mol/L.

Similarly, the sulfuric acid concentration affects the optimal flow rate through its influence on conductivity and the relative importance of ohmic versus concentration polarization losses. At 2.0 mol/L H₂SO₄ and 100 mA/cm², the lower conductivity rendered ohmic losses dominant, and the flow-rate-mediated mass-transfer improvement contributed less to overall VE than at 4.0 mol/L H₂SO₄ where ohmic losses were already minimal. Consequently, the VE improvement from increasing flow rate from 10 to 40 mL/min was 3.2 percentage points at 2.0 mol/L H₂SO₄ but only 2.1 percentage points at 4.0 mol/L H₂SO₄, implying that high-acid electrolytes are less sensitive to flow optimization.

A comprehensive assessment of practical energy density versus net system efficiency reveals a Pareto-optimal front that system designers must navigate. The combination of 1.6 mol/L vanadium, 4.0 mol/L H₂SO₄, and 40 mL/min at 100 mA/cm² yielded a practical energy density of 21.5 Wh/L with a net EE of 82.1%, representing the best balanced performance in the tested parameter space. Increasing vanadium to 2.0 mol/L raised the practical energy density to 23.8 Wh/L but reduced net EE to 78.9% due to viscosity-enhanced pumping losses and mass-transfer limitations. Conversely, reducing to 1.5 mol/L vanadium lowered the practical energy density to 19.1 Wh/L but allowed a net EE of 83.2%, suggesting that this composition may be preferable for efficiency-critical applications where space constraints are secondary.

The current density choice further complicates this optimization landscape. At 50 mA/cm², the combination of 1.6 mol/L vanadium, 4.0 mol/L H₂SO₄, and 20 mL/min delivered a practical energy density of 21.2 Wh/L with a net EE of 84.7%, whereas at 200 mA/cm² the same electrolyte composition required 60 mL/min to achieve acceptable polarization behavior, yielding a practical energy density of 20.8 Wh/L but a reduced net EE of 74.5%. The relatively modest energy density penalty at 200 mA/cm²—only 4 Wh/L lower than at 50 mA/cm²—may be acceptable in applications where high power density is required, but the 10.2 percentage point efficiency reduction has significant implications for thermal management and operating cost over the system lifetime.

## 4. Conclusion

This systematic investigation of electrolyte concentration and flow rate effects in VRFBs has revealed several key findings with direct relevance to system design and optimization. First, increasing vanadium concentration from 1.2 to 1.6 mol/L improves both theoretical and practical energy density, with EE peaking at 83.5% at 100 mA/cm²; however, further increases to 2.0 mol/L introduce viscosity-driven mass-transfer penalties that partially offset the concentration benefit, and 3.0 mol/L electrolytes exhibit precipitation instability within 72 hours at 25°C. Second, sulfuric acid concentration exhibits a non-monotonic effect with an optimum at approximately 4.0 mol/L, where the balance between proton conductivity enhancement and hydrogen evolution suppression yields maximum EE of 83.7%. Deviations to 2.0 or 5.0 mol/L both reduce EE to approximately 80%, albeit through different mechanisms—ohmic losses in the former case and CE degradation from HER in the latter. Third, flow rate optimization follows a clear trade-off curve: increasing from 10 to 40 mL/min mitigates concentration polarization and raises net system EE from 78.0% to 81.5%, but further increases beyond 60 mL/min disproportionately increase pumping losses and reduce net efficiency. The optimal flow rate depends on current density, shifting from 20 mL/min at 50 mA/cm² to 40 mL/min at 100 mA/cm² and 60 mL/min at 200 mA/cm². These results demonstrate that maximizing VRFB performance requires integrated optimization of electrolyte composition and hydrodynamic conditions, and that isolated maximization of any single parameter—whether vanadium concentration, acid concentration, or flow rate—leads to suboptimal system-level outcomes. Future work should extend these findings to multi-cell stacks and explore temperature-dependent optimization to broaden the practical operating window for high-concentration electrolytes.
