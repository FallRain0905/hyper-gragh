# ABSTRACT

Despite advancements,dendrite growth at the anode continues to be a persistent roadblock in accelerating the widespread deployment of hybrid flow bateries as the next-generation energy storage solution,due to the significant impactof dendrites oncycling performanceandthe potentialfor batery failure.Theabilityto analyze energy storage systems at micro-to-macro levels offers unprecedented insights into their behavior and performance.Herein,we develop a multiscale model utilizing phase-field method to investigate dendrite formation, growth,and stripping under operational conditions.The Zn-I system is employed to unravel the intricacies of dendrite evolution and its mitigation through strategic utilization of critical battery parameters. Our findings not onlyuncover precise zinc morphologies but also provide valuable insights into battery performance toward developing a strategy for mitigating dendrite growth and enhancing battery efficiencyat high currentdensity.To our knowledge,this is the first work to comprehensively untangle electrodeposition dynamics at multiscale n the field offlow battery and related research.The findings revolutionize our understanding of deposition behavior, driving transformative advancements in hybrid flow battery design and development,with potential applicability to other battery systems.

# 1．Introduction

Energy storage systems have become an essential part of renewables as a climate change mitigation strategy for storing intermittent electricity produced from solar and wind for power supply during periods of high demand.Flow batteries with metal ion-based anolytes,such as zinc, iron,lead, tin,and bismuth,have been identified as the next-generation energy storage solution for coping effectively with the rising energy demand [1-3].However,inhomogeneous electrodeposition incurs uncontrolled dendrite growth on anode and remains a critical issue during charging process,inevitably leading to confined cycling life,low efficiency, low capacity,and safety concerns [4-6].Dendrite formation is an intrinsically complex process triggered by inhomogeneous ion concentration,insufficient ion transport to anode surface,unregulated electric field,and inhomogeneity of solid/electrode electrolyte interface (SEI/EEI) [7-11].To effectively mitigate the dendrite formation and growth,it is critical to understand the intricate deposition morphologies vary depending on interface and operating conditions.For instance, applied current density,temperature,electrolyte flow rate,and the roughness of the anode surface induce concentration,electric field,and flow field variations across the anode surface,which result in distinguished deposit morphologies,such as faceted,spherical,disc-like, needle-like,fiber-like,bush-like,and mossy [l2-15]．Since in-situ monitoring of metal deposit morphology during charging is challenging,over the past few years,theoretical modelling usinga diffuse interface,such as the phase-field method,has been extensively used to visualize and understand the microstructure evolution as a function of space and time in metal-based batteries.Initially,a1D phase-field model was utilized to investigate the kinetic behavior of the electrodeposition process and charge separation, uncovering underlying mechanisms [16, 17].Researchers then developed specialized phase-field models to study growth kinetics,dendrite-separator interaction,and complex morphologies of Li deposits,providing deeper insights into their behavior[18, 19].To capture non-linear behavior,thermodynamically consistent phase-field models incorporating Butler-Volmer kinetics were formulated,facilitating a realistic study of Li deposit patterns and growth [20, 21].The studies also focused on the role of the SEI microstructure and properties in Li dendrite evolution,highlighting the impact of SEI on dendrite formation [22-24].Additionally,phase-field methods explored the influence of exchange current density and diffusion-limited current density on Li deposits,enhancing the understanding of growth and behavior concerning electrochemical kinetics [25,26].

Despite these advancements, a critical gap remains as most existing studies focus on small-scale,isolated models or specific aspects of metal deposition,often neglecting the complexity of electrodeposition at the cell level under realistic operating conditions.Additionally,the incorporation of critical parameters,such as local current density variations across the electrode surface and concentration- and temperaturedependent physicochemical properties,as well as their integration into the phase-field method,has yet to be fully explored.Furthermore, while extensive efforts have been made to study the deposition process, less attention has been given to the stripping process,which involves the dissolution of metal deposits from the electrode surface.The formation of inactive metal during stripping significantly affects the reversibility of dendrite growth, leading to decreased electrode performance and potential safety risks.This issue is particularly pronounced in hybrid flow batteries (HFBs), where understanding both the deposition and stripping dynamics is essential for achieving high efficiency and long-term stability [27-29].HFBs present unique challenges due to their complex design and operation,which involve the flow of electrolytes and active materials.The dynamic nature of HFBs-governed by the interplay of flow dynamics,electrode kinetics,and mass transport—makes it difficult to accurately predict and control the electrodeposition process. Bridging the gap between theoretical models and real-world cell-level behavior is crucial for optimizing electrodeposition in these system. Therefore,there is an urgent need fora comprehensive approach that can accurately mimic the experimental setups and conditions. By doing so,researchers can gain insights into the mechanisms of electrodeposition,where the clues drawn by microscale models are questioned to devise dendrite-suppression environments for an actual HFB system.

In this study,we address this gap by developing a multiscale model based on the phase-field approach to gain a fundamental understanding of the deposition dynamics and their impact on macroscale electrochemical performance under realistic working conditions. Specifically,a representative zinc-iodine (Zn-I) HFB model is established to systematically unravel the complex mechanisms of dendrite growth and subsequent stripping behavior under critical battery conditions.The results reveal the model not only explores the deposition dynamics but also provides insights into mitigating dendrite growth in HFBs,ultimately contributing to enhanced battery efciency and safety.By integrating multiscale analysis,this work offers a unique perspective on the interplay of microscale mechanisms and macroscale behavior,paving the way for developing strategies to suppress dendrite formation in practical flow battery systems.

# 2.1.Early-Stage zinc deposition

The deposition dynamics at the initial growth stage were analyzed, revealing distinct planner (valley region) and nucleation (tip) regimes at $1 . 6 7 \ \mathrm { m A h } { \cdot } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ as shown in Figs. 2a and S1 for $\mathbf { r } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathbf { r } _ { 2 } , \mathbf { r } _ { 3 } ,$ respectively.During this stage, zinc ions are attracted to the anode surface and start to form small, localized clusters of $\mathtt { Z n }$ deposits that act as initial nucleation sites with strong electric fields.The morphologies of protrusions vary in shape,size (length towards IEM),thickness,and nucleation density depending on battery condition.For instance, C-R exhibits a dendrite near the inlet with $2 4 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ size and few tip heads, while C-1 shows a dendrite with $3 2 \mu \mathrm { m }$ size,reduced thickness in nuclei, numerous side branches,higher nucleation density,and accelerated growth due to enhanced electrochemical reactions and mass transport (Fig.2a). This accelerated rate of zinc ion deposition and consumption affects the $\mathrm { Z n }$ ion concentration on the electrode surface,creating a steeper concentration gradient at the tip,Fig.2b,driving zinc ions more rapidly towards the tip.Eventually,it creates a strong local electric field (Fig.2c), leading to rapid ion migration from surroundings towards the tips to replenish the depleted concentration and facilitate further deposition.In contrast, lowering the current density in C-1A results in thicker nuclei, reduced side branches,and lower nucleation density, indicating slower dendritic growth and weaker ion migration towards the tips (Fig. Sla). This can be attributed to reduced electrochemical reactions and mass transport,resulting in a less steep concentration gradient (Fig.S1b) and weaker local electric field (Fig. S1c),limiting the migration of ions and deposition solely on the tip.While lower flow rate in C-2 reduces fluid velocity,prolonging Zn ion residence time at the electrode surface.This extended exposure facilitates the formation of secondary branches from primary dendrite nuclei under the influence of steeper concentration gradient and higher electric field (Figs. 2b and 2c) compared with C-R as zinc ions accumulate and diffuse laterally. Increasing the flow rate in C-2A has a minimal effect on zinc ion accumulation and diffusion,resulting in insignificant changes in the morphology，complexity，and compactness of the Zn deposits.At elevated temperatures (C-3), the nucleation rate (Eq. (lO)) increases significantly,leading to a shorter relaxation time for the initial nucleation process.The heightened thermal energy enhances the mobility of zinc ions,facilitating their diffusion and aggregation.As a result,largersized dendrite nuclei form,depleting the availability of free zinc ions for new nucleation events and causing a substantial decrease in nucleation density. Decreasing temperature in C-3A slows down Zn ion migration (Figs.Sla-Slc),requiring higher overpotential to create a strong electric field that can facilitate and accelerate ion migration, thereby creating more localized hotspots,higher nucleation density,and thinner protrusions.During the C-4 charge protocol, zinc ions migrate towards the electrode surface, promoting nucleation and initial dendrite growth and kinetics like C-R (Figs.2a-2c).However,the subsequent resting period allows zinc ions to diffuse away,redistributing them and reducing the necessary concentration gradient for nucleation.This results in a lower nucleation density compared to C-R. C-4A and C-4B show similar impact (Figs.Sla-S1c),indicating insensitivity of initial protrusions to charge protocols.In C-5 and C-5A,despite increased viscosity,elevated concentration of zinc ions near the electrode surface creates an intensified concentration gradient and effectively screens the electric field, leading to areduction in its strength (Figs. 2d, 2e,S1d,and Sle). This promotes zinc ion diffusion,aggregation,and the formation of thick dendrite nuclei. Decreasing flow channel thickness in C-6 shortens the distance between cathode and anode,intensifying the electric field and concentration gradient. This promotes higher nucleation density on the anode surface due to the shorter ion transport path.In contrast, smooth and seeds surfaces (C-7 and C-7A) and HER reaction (C-8) display similar impact as in C-R,although C-8 exhibits a higher electric field (Figs.2, Sla, Sld,and Sle).

The complexity of initial Zn deposits and deposition effectiveness is quantified by the dimensionless perimeter and deposition ratio (Figs. 2f, ${ \textbf { 2 } } g ,$ ，S1f, Slg,2 h,2i, S1h,Sli).The dimensionless perimeter shows exponential growth with elevated current density $( \mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } { > } 0 . 9 5 )$ ,where it reaches the highest value of 1.6 $( m = 0 . 3 5 / \mathrm { m A h } { \cdot } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 } )$ for C-1,followed by 1.45 $( m = 0 . 2 7 / \mathrm { m A h } { \cdot } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 } )$ for C-2 (Fig.2f).This trend reflects ahigh nucleation density and increased surface area due to increasing intricacy of the nuclei, particularly in C-1.While gradual increase is observed for all other conditions.It is indeed intriguing to observe that despite the smaller protrusion size and nucleation density in the C-3, C-4,and C-5 compared to C-R,the dimensionless perimeter of the Zn deposits remains relatively higher (Figs. 2f, $2 g )$ ．This phenomenon could potentially be explained bya few factors.Firstly,higher temperature (C-3) contributes to the formation of thicker dendrites with multiple tips, resulting in a higher surface area and dimensionless perimeter.Secondly,in C-4,the charge-resting period enables the coalescence of small protrusions,leading to higher surface area and dimensionless perimeter through the merging of nearby dendritic structures.Thirdly,in C-5, abundant Zn ions result in wider dendrite apices with multiple heads, increasing the effective surface area and dimensionless perimeter. Nevertheless,deposition ratio results in Figs.2h and 2i show that C-3 and C-5 have more compact deposition along the y-axis compared to C-$\mathbb { R } ,$ suggesting that although the dimensionless perimeter may be higher in C-3 and C-4,the overall deposition pattern is more confined and concentrated in a narrower region.In contrast,C-1 shows a broader distribution of Zn deposits. Interestingly,a smooth surface in C-7 demonstrates compact deposition due to the absence of surface irregularities and trapping sites,unlike in C-R. The efects of decreasing current density (C-1A),increasing flow rate (C-2A),and extending resting time (C-4A and C-4B) on dimensionless perimeter and deposition thickness are negligible compared to C-R,Figs. # 2.2.Later-Stage zinc deposition

As charging capacity increases, nucleated clusters grow into dendritic structures with varying deposition patterns due to differences in deposition mechanisms,ion transport,and local electric field distribution.Figs.3a and S5a illustrate morphologies,spatial distribution of $\mathrm { Z n }$ ion,electric field,and ion transport strength at $1 2 . 5 ~ \mathrm { m A h } { \cdot } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ Additionally,Figs.3b-3e and S5b-S5e show concentration and electric field profiles on tip and valley regions.At this stage,all conditions consistently exhibit pronounced and extensive dendritic structures,as seen in Fig.3a. The later-stage deposition forms dendrites with intricate branching patterns resembling tree-like structures.These characteristics are particularly pronounced in C-1 and C-2,and C-3A where the dendrites extend to heights of $1 8 5 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ $4 . 6 3 ~ \%$ of anode height), $1 5 1 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ $( 3 . 7 8 ~ \% )$ ,and $1 7 5 \mu \mathrm { m } \left( 4 . 3 8 \% \right)$ ,respectively (Fig. 3a, S5a).In C-1, the dendrites show frail bases,weak branches,and occasional closed cavities or holes.The branching patterns and closed cavities contribute to the complex morphology of the dendrites,adding to their intricacy.In contrast, high temperature (C-3),low current density (C-1A),and high concentration (C-5,C-5A) conditions result in more compact deposition, particularly noticeable for C-3 and C-1A.In C-6,despite smaller dendrites than C-R,the flow channel halved in size,leading to higher dendrite height percentage (6.15) in comparison to C-R (3.63) and all other conditions.Increased flow rate (C-2A) and extended resting periods (C-4A,C-4B) show minimal impact on the size and morphology of zinc deposits,suggesting that variations within the tested range have an insignificant effect on mass transport and nucleation processes during late growth.While presence of seeds (C-7A) shows trivial influence on growth, indicating other factors play a dominant role in determining final deposit size and morphology. The deposition growth at later stage in regions $\mathbf { r } _ { 2 }$ and $\mathbf { r } _ { 3 }$ remains similar (Figs.S6 and S7),as observed during the initial growth stage,where the dendritic development in $\mathbf { r } _ { 2 }$ is mitigated compared to $\mathbf { r } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathbf { r } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { : } }$ ，resulting in smaller dendrite size.This difference in growth intensity suggests that the later-stage growth dynamics in r2 are influenced by distinct factors,such as variations in Zn ion transport and exchange current density,due to the unique deposition pattern.

Later-stage Zn deposition dynamics depend on ion availability and transport,driven by localized electric field enhancements.Significant insights can be gleaned from the analysis of concentration gradients and electric field distributions near the tip and valley regions. Observations show that in the tip region, C-1 exhibits the highest electric field strength and the most rapid concentration descent from bulk to surface,indicating faster ion depletion on the surface (Figs. 3b and 3c). Conversely, C-3,C-5,and C-1A display the lowest electric field strength and concentration descent in the same region (Figs. 3b-3e and S5b-S5e). In C-3, elevated temperature influences ion transport and diffusion dynamics, leading to a reduced electric field strength and a slower concentration descent,while higher initial concentration in C-5 provides a greater reservoir of Zn ions,resulting in a slower depletion and subsequently a reduced electric field strength.In C-1A,reduced current density limits the rate of ion transfer and subsequent depletion of the Zn ions,resulting ina diminished electric field strength and slower concentration descent compared to higher current density in C-1.As a result, smaller and more compact dendrites form,with C-3 having dendrites nearly half the size of those in C-1.In the valley region,concentration gradients diminish, affecting Zn ion diffusion dynamics without complete depletion of concentration.Furthermore,disorganized vortex pairs form in the vicinity of the dendrites,especially in intricate dendrites (C-1),destabilizing the ion transport pathway.Fig.S8 shows chaotic electrolyte streamlines with circulating Zn ions along the edges.This hinders $\mathtt { Z n }$ deposition in the valley region,a phenomenon further exacerbated by the migration of ions toward areas of high electric field strength near the tip.SEM images obtained during the later stages of deposition for C-R, C-1,and C-3 near the inlet (Fig.S9) align with the simulated results.This agreement indicates that the observed growth processes closely match the simulated outcomes,confirming the accuracy and reliability of the simulation in representing real-world deposition behavior.

The complexity and effectiveness of later-stage Zn deposits are analyzed using the dimensionless perimeter and deposition ratio (Figs.3f-3i and S5f-S5i).The dimensionless perimeter shows a more rapid,linear trend compared to the initial deposition stage,except for C-5,where the growth rate of the dimensionless perimeter remains unchanged. C-1 exhibits the highest dimensionless perimeter, indicating a highly complex and intricate dendrite morphology with numerous branches.Likewise,the disruption in Zn ion distribution caused by lower flow rate (Fig.3f for C-2),and reduced mobility and kinetics at lower temperature (Fig. S5f for C-3A) lead to dendrite growth with multiple branches and a larger dimensionless perimeter.Interestingly,in C-2A, C-3,C-4,C-4A,C-4B, C-5,and C-5A,the dimensionless perimeter values compared to C-R decrease as dendrites progress in growth, contrasting with their values at the initial growth stage.This observation suggests a progressive simplification of dendrite structures during later growth. Multiple factors contribute to this phenomenon.For instance,the increased Zn ion mobility at elevated temperature (C-3) promotes fusion of neighboring branches, reducing surface area. Similarly,the resting periods (C-4,C-4A, C-4B) facilitate the coalescence of small dendritic structures,while the higher concentrations (C-5,C-5A） ensure an abundant supply of Zn ions,promoting the growth of existing depositions instead of nucleation and branching This leads to dendrites with reduced surface area and lower dimensionless perimeter. Notably, lower zinc ion utilization due to competing HER and zinc dissolution (C-8)reduces deposition and leads to a smaller dimensionless perimeter. Additionally,Figs.3h,3i, S5h,and S5i show a similar pattern of deposition ratio to the initial growth stage.C-3, C-5,and C1-A exhibit more compact deposition along the y-axis compared to C-R,while C-1 shows the least compact deposition with a broader distribution of Zn deposits. This suggests that the less complex deposition in C-3,C-5,and C1-A, indicated by the lower dimensionless perimeter,leads to a confined and concentrated deposition within a narrower region.

Fig.4 provides a schematic overview of the deposition mechanism observed in this study.During the early stage of electroplating,a rapid electrochemical reaction at the anode surface induces activation polarization, causing a depletion of Zn ion concentration at the interface.This initiates surface and EEI inhomogeneities,leading to uneven deposition and growth of protrusions,especially at higher current densities.Two distinct morphological regions are observed: the region experiencing protrusion growth is referred to as the tip region,while the region with flat deposition and no subsequent protrusion growth is referred to as the valley region. In the tip region,a significant concentration gradient, particularly at higher current densities and lower temperatures,results in a shortened ion transport path with laminar electrolyte streamlines. This concentrated region also generates local electric field hotspots, attracting more $\mathrm { Z n }$ ions from the bulk electrolyte and the surrounding areas.The enhanced ion transport, including migration and diffusion, leads to increased concentration at the interface,accelerating the electrochemical reaction in the tip region and promoting the development of aggregated dendrites.This,in turn,further shortens the ion transport path and intensifies the electric field,creating a dynamic cycle that ultimately leads to a short circuit. On the other hand,the valley region experiences a weaker electric field and an extended transport path with chaotic electrolyte streamlines,which slows down the transport of ions to the interface.Additionally,ions migrate from the surrounding valley region toward the tip under the influence of strong electric field near the crest areas,exacerbating the ion transport and hindering the electrochemical reaction,resulting in poor deposition around the valley areas. However,at low current densities,high temperatures,and high ion concentrations,the surface and EEI inhomogeneities are significantly reduced.As a result, the ion concentration at the interface decreases due to the electrochemical reaction, facilitating ion transport from the bulk electrolyte to the interface.This positive feedback loop ensures stable deposition under such conditions.Since Zn deposition has the potential to impact zinc dissolution during discharge,the subsequent section delves into the influence of deposition on zinc stripping.

# 2.3. Zinc stripping process

Zinc morphologies at early and late discharge capacities (Figs.5a and S10a)reveal interesting findings when considering anodic local current density distribution.Importantly, inactive Zn formation occurs in all conditions,particularly pronounced in C-1,where weak and thinner branches detach due to concentrated local current density influence. Similarly,in C-2,multiple branch detachments are observed from the anode.Conversely C-3,with elevated temperature,shows mild occurrence of inactive zinc formation as higher temperature enhances stripping kinetics,reducing branch detachment.However,C-4 to C-7 exhibit zinc detachment during late discharge due to their deposition patterns. While,in C-8,where HER reduces zinc deposition,minimal branch detachment leads to mild inactive zinc formation. Regarding stripping behavior,region $\mathbf { r } _ { 3 }$ shows a similar pattern,indicating comparable characteristics in removing deposited zinc (Figs.S11 and S12).In contrast,in region $\mathbf { r } _ { 2 } .$ ，smaller and less complex dendrites result in minimal inactive zinc formation during the stripping process (Figs. S11 and S12).

Quantitative analysis of zinc stripping effectiveness is shown in Figs.5b,5c,S1ob,S10c (dimensionless perimeter) and 5d,5e,S10d, S10e (percentage of inactive zinc formation) throughout the discharging process.The dimensionless perimeter decreases nonlinearly for all conditions,with a rapid decrease until reaching a value around 3 $\mathbf { m A h { \cdot } c m ^ { - 2 } }$ ,except for C-8,which continues to decrease further.At 12.0 $\mathbf { m A h { \cdot } c m ^ { - 2 } }$ ,C-1 exhibits the highest dimensionless perimeter (1.61) due to needle-shaped residuals from intricate dendrite structures hindering the stripping process.In contrast,C-3 has the lowest value (1.07), indicating effective stripping due to a less complicated structure of $\mathtt { Z n }$ deposits and efficient kinetics at high temperature.Despite some zinc detachments in C-5,the stripping behaviour in terms of the dimensionless perimeter remains comparable to that of C-3.

Regarding inactive zinc formation (Figs.5d,5e,S1od,and S10e),in C-1, zinc branch detachment from the anode surface begins early in the discharge process and continues during stripping, resulting in around 6 $\%$ formation at $1 2 . 0 \mathrm { m A h } { \cdot } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ . In C-2 and C-6,detachment occurs later but at a slower rate,leading to approximately $2 \%$ and $1 \ \%$ formation, respectively.For C-R,C-1A, C-5, C-5A,C-7,and C-7A,around or slightly above $1 \%$ of inactive zinc formation is observed,while it remains below $1 \ \%$ for C-2A,C-3,C-3A,C-4,C-4A,C-4B,and C-8.

These findings highlight the varying degrees of inactive zinc formation under different conditions.The timing and rate of zinc branch detachment during stripping significantly influence the extent of inactive zinc formation,which in turn impacts overall cell performance,as discussed in the following section.

# 3.Conclusions

A multiscale model based on phase-field method was developed to investigate the deposition dynamics in hybrid flow batteries.A Zn-I flow battery was established to explore the impact of critical battery conditions on the evolution of deposition morphologies, stripping behavior, and cell performance. Parameters such as current density, flow rate, temperature,and initial concentration of the electrolyte were found to have a strong influence on early and later-stage deposition dynamics. The conditions involving charge-rest protocol,anode surface,and flow channel thickness significantly influenced the early deposition stage but gradually diminished in impact as the deposition progressed to later stages.Higher current density,lower flow rate,and lower temperature resulted in complex dendrite formation,poor stripping,and reduced cell efficiencies.In contrast,lower current density,higher temperature,and initial electrolyte concentration led to compact deposition,efective stripping,and enhanced cell efficiencies.The charge-rest protocol and anode surface had minimal effects on cell efficiencies. However, parasitic reactions like the hydrogen evolution reaction significantly decreased cell performance by impacting the deposition process. Moreover,superior reversibility and efficiency are achieved under controlled conditions even at high current density. The results demonstrate that the developed model enables exploration of electrodeposition dynamics and provides performance evaluation under realistic operating conditions.This approach,while powerful for modeling electrodeposition dynamics,has certain limitations that can influence the interpretation of results.For example,it uses a diffuse interface to represent the solid-liquid boundary，which may oversimplify the sharpness and complexity of real interfaces.Additionally,computational constraints limit the spatial resolution and domain size,potentially affecting the accuracy of nanoscale features. Also,the method relies on simplified kinetic models, which may not fully capture complex aspects, such as side reactions.Yet, the model provides valuable insights into the interplay of microscale and macroscale phenomena in electrodeposition. This study presents opportunities for advancements in flow battery development and beyond. For instance, it opens avenues for optimizing battery design and operating conditions,such as the anode/cathode structure, flow rate,and temperature,to enhance performance.Additionally,the study's findings can potentially extend to other battery systems like solid-state and flexible batteries. Ongoing efforts are dedicated to further research in these areas.
