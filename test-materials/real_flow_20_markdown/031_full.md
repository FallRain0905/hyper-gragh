Iron-chromium redox flowbateries (ICRFBs),asan ideal large-scale energy storage technology,exhibit excellent compatibility with intermittent and renewable energy sources.Despite being the preferred electrode material for ICRFBs,carbon cloth (CC)suffer from limited active sites and insufcient electrochemical activity.In this work, we demonstrate a surface enginering strategy involving thedeposition of a polyaniline (PANI)-derived nitrogenenriched carbon network onto carbon cloth via aniline (ANI) self-assembly polymerization folowed by hightemperature carbonization.The modified electrode (denoted as C-PANI@CC)achieves a nearly tenfold increase in specific surface area $( 5 . 3 5 1 ~ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { g }$ Vs. $0 . 5 7 6 \mathrm { ~ m } ^ { 2 } / 8$ for pristine CC),while the abundant active sites and efficient nitrogen doping colectively enhance reaction kinetics and mass transport processes in ICRFBs.The mass transfer processincharge and discharge of flow batery was simulated by Comsol software.Multiphysics simulations reveal that the improved specific surface area and electrical conductivity significantly optimize overpotential and product distribution during discharge. Remarkably,the C-PANI@CC electrode maintains a high energy efficiency of $8 3 . 8 3 \%$ at $1 4 0 \mathrm { \ m A { \cdot } c m ^ { - 2 } }$ and demonstrates stable performance over 3oo charge-discharge cycles.These findings underscore the potential of electrode surface engineering in advancing ICRFB, providing critical insights for its implementation in large-scale renewable energy storage systems.

# 1．Introduction

With the global energy structure transitioning toward green and lowcarbon solutions,the installed capacity of renewable energy sources, such as wind and solar power,has continued to rise [1-5].However, the inherent intermittency and volatility of renewable energy hinder its direct integration into national power grids,resulting in significant energy wastage and inefficient utilization [6,7].This underscores the urgent need to develop efficient, safe,and cost-effective large-scale energy storage technologies to support sustainable grid-scale deployment. Among emerging solutions,redox flow batteries (RFBs) are regarded as ideal candidates for large-scale energy storage due to their decoupled power and capacity design,long cycle life,and high safety [8-11]. Iron-chromium redox flow batteries (ICRFBs),in particular,have garnered widespread attention owing to their low cost,abundant active material reserves,scalability,and adaptability to fluctuating renewable energy inputs [l2,l3].The use of iron and chromium,both earthabundant and stable materials, further enhances their economic viability [14,15].

Nevertheless,ICRFBs face critical challenges,including sluggish reaction kinetics of the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ and $\mathrm { C r } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { C r } ^ { 3 + }$ redox couple [16], low electrode specific surface area,non-uniform mass transport leading to overpotential spikes,and capacity degradation.Porous carbon-based electrodes,such as graphite felt,carbon felt,carbon cloth (CC),and carbon paper,are widely employed in RFBs due to their superior corrosion resistance and electrical conductivity.Carbon cloth,composed of graphitized polyacrylonitrile fibers,offers balanced mechanical strength,flexibility,and electrochemical stability [17].However,its smooth fiber surfaces limit reactive sites and electrochemical activity. Modifications to CC electrodes aimed at enhancing specific surface area, increasing active sites,improving mass transport,and boosting redox activity are essential for advancing ICRFB performance.

Electrode modification strategies for ICRFBs include thermal treatment,elemental doping,acid etching,carbon nanomaterial decoration, defect engineering,and catalyst loading.Thermal activation in air is a common industrial method to introduce surface defects and oxygencontaining functional groups.Non-metal doping，such as nitrogen [14,17,18],adjusts local charge densityand provides additional active sites.Modifying electrode surfaces with metallic [19,2O] or bimetallic composite materials [21,22] enables the enhancement of both electrical conductivity and electrochemical activity. Transition metals,such as indium [23,24] and bismuth [25-27],are effective electrocatalysts for suppressing hydrogen evolution and reducing redox reaction energy barriers [28-3O]. Acid treatment [31-33] and defect engineering [34] further enhance surface area and electron transfer kinetics.Guo et al. [35] through chemical vapor deposition method.In-situ growth of nitrogen-doped vertical graphene and high-density edge graphene on graphite felt shows high ratio.Surface area and significant catalytic activity. Zhang et al.[36] prepared carbon nanotubes co-doped with nitrogen,phosphorus and sulfur.Melamine foam composite electrode enhances conductivity and hydrophilicity.

High specific surface area represents a desirable direction in electrode design for iron-chromium flow batteries,while nitrogen doping has been demonstrated to be an efective catalyst for their electrochemical reactions.As far as we know, there is no reported study on an electrode modification strategy that simultaneously incorporates highsurface-area carbon materials on carbon cloth and introduces efficient nitrogen doping within such a high-surface-area structure.Therefore,an approach that designs specific high-surface-area architectures and incorporates functionally tailored species can effectively integrate multiple electrode modification strategies,thereby providing guidance for the composite modification of electrodes.

Polyaniline(PANI),a nitrogen-rich carbon precursor,has garnered significant attention in the field of electrocatalysts [37,38] and electrochemical devices [39-41]. Through high-temperature pyrolysis, the graphitic structure of polyaniline facilitates the incorporation of various nitrogen configurations (such as pyridinic nitrogen, pyrrolic nitrogen, and graphitic nitrogen) into the carbon framework,making it an efficient medium for nitrogen-rich doping[42]. Su etal.[43] constructed a polyaniline layer modified with sulfanilic acid,which enhanced charge transfer and ion transport processes at the electrochemical reaction interface. Chen et al.[44] utilized protonic acid as an auxiliary agent to electrodeposit polyaniline onto the surface of graphite felt,resulting in a uniformly conductive N,P co-doped carbon fiber structure.Jiang et al. [45]performed stepwise electrochemical polymerization of aniline (ANI) on carbon woven fabric (CWF) electrodes and demonstrated that the PANI@CWF electrode contributed to higher current density output, thereby improving thermoelectric energy storage and power output performance.However,the electrochemical polymerization of aniline is arapid process that tends to form heterogeneous polyaniline layers. Therefore,in the design of high-specific-surface-area microstructures,it is imperative to adopt polymerization methods with higher fault tolerance and better controllability.

In this study,a polyaniline-derived nitrogen-enriched carbon layer was coated onto carbon cloth through aniline self-assembly polymerization followed by high-temperature carbonization.The modified electrode achieved a specific surface area of 5.351 $\mathbf { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathbf { g }$ ,nearly10 times greater than that of pristine carbon cloth $( 0 . 5 7 6 \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { g } )$ .The hierarchical porous structure and nitrogen doping significantly enhanced reaction kinetics,mass transport,and electronic conductivity.Multiphysics simulations revealed optimized overpotential distribution and reaction product homogeneity during discharge,while long-term cycling tests at $\mathsf { \bar { 1 4 0 } m A { \cdot } c m ^ { . 2 } }$ demonstrated exceptional stability over 30o cycles. These results highlight the potential of this scalable modification strategy to address key limitations in ICRFB performance,advancing their application in large-scale renewable energy storage systems.

# 2.2.Electrode preparation

The carbon cloth was washed with ethanol and dried at $6 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for 12 h.After drying,the CC was activated by heating in a muffle furnace at $5 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 5 \mathrm { h } }$ under an o air atmosphere at a heating rate of $5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } / \mathrm { m i n }$ to introduce oxygen-containing functional groups.The activated CC was then cooled to room temperature.

# 2.2.2.Preparation of TCC electrode

The activated CC was washed with ethanol and dried at $6 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for 12 h.Subsequently, the CC was heat-treated in a tube furnace at $9 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ under a nitrogen atmosphere at a heating rate of $5 ~ { ^ \circ } \mathrm { C } / { \mathrm { m i n } }$ ,followed by cooling to room temperature to obtain the thermally treated carbon cloth.

# 2.2.3.Preparation of C-PANI@CC electrode

The activated CC was washed with ethanol and dried at $6 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for 12 h.A1 mol/L HCl solution $\mathrm { ( 2 0 ~ m L ) }$ was mixed with $1 0 \mathrm { m m o l }$ of aniline and stirred thoroughly.The mixture was pre-cooled ina refrigeratorat ${ 2 } ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ .Separately, $2 . 5 \ \mathrm { m m o l }$ of APS was dissolved in $2 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of 1 mol/L HCl,and the CC was immersed in the solution with stirring for 20 min before being pre-cooled at ${ 2 } ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ .The pre-cooled aniline solution was then added to the APS solution containing the CC,and the mixture was stirred rapidly and kept at ${ } ^ { 2 } \ { } ^ { \circ } { \bf C }$ for $^ { 1 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ .Afterpolymerization, the CC was dried and carbonized ina tube furnace at $9 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for2 h under a nitrogen atmosphere at a heating rate of $5 ~ { ^ \circ } \mathrm { C } / \mathrm { m i n }$ The resulting C-PANI@CC was washed repeatedly with $1 \ \mathrm { m o l / L }$ HCl and deionized water, followed by drying.

# 3．Results and discussion

# 3.1．Morphological and structural characteristic

Here,we summarize the rationale for selecting the comparative samples and the battery system parameters to ensure the completeness of the study.First, the reasons for choosing the comparative samples, CC and TCC,are discussed.In the industrial application of iron-chromium flow batteries,it is a common practice to thermally treat carbon electrodes in an air atmosphere to increase their oxygen-containing functional groups.Based on this,we designed and prepared sample CC as a reference representing industrial carbon cloth.Among the modification methods for carbon cloth electrodes,high-temperature calcination in an inertatmosphere is also widely employed; this process can enhance the defect density and graphitization degree of the carbon cloth to some extent,thereby potentially improving electrode performance.Accordingly,we designed and prepared sample TCC to exclude the performance contribution arising from this method.Comparing both of these samples with the core design presented in this work,C-PANI@CC,will further demonstrate the advantages of our design strategy.Furthermore,the flow rate selected for the battery system is also a critical parameter,as it directly affects cell performance.For flow battery systems employing carbon felt, the electrolyte relies more on permeation through the threedimensional structure and a high flow rate is not required.In contrast, for carbon cloth with a two-dimensional structure,a relatively high flow rate is necessary to ensure adequate electrolyte contact and more efficient mass transfer.The flow rate of $2 0 \mathrm { m L } / \mathrm { m i n }$ used in our test system was determined by taking the above considerations into full account, combined with practical testing experience.

The electrochemical polymerization of aniline in a protonic acid environment is a relatively rapid process.The resulting polyaniline structures often exhibit localized and densely aggregated morphologies, making it difficult to achieve uniform dispersion and homogeneous growth across the electrode surface.To address this limitation,we followed a review of the experimental approaches documented in multiple studies (Table S1） and employed a low-temperature chemical polymerization approach to guide ANI self-assembly on carbon cloth surfaces [46].This strategy successfully achieved the formation of a homogeneous reticulated PANI network that conformally coated the carbon cloth electrode. Subsequent high-temperature carbonization transformed the PANI framework into an interconnected Nitrogen-rich Carbon Layer permanently anchored to the electrode surface.

During the aniline self-polymerization process,heterogeneous nucleation and homogeneous nucleation emerge as two distinct mechanisms that directly govern PANI morphology,dimensional characteristics,and functional properties.Heterogeneous nucleation typically produces bulk granular structures,while homogeneous nucleation favors nanofiber formation [47].In the initial polymerization stage,the absence of heterogeneous nucleation sites restricts growth exclusively to homogeneous nucleation pathways,resulting in nanofiber generation [46].As polymerization progresses, newly synthesized PANI molecules may either deposit onto pre-existing PANI particles through heterogeneous nucleation or form new nuclei via homogeneous nucleation. Heterogeneous nucleation dominates the later stages [48],with molecular deposition preferentially occurring on established particulates, ultimately yielding aggregated granular or bulk PANI structures,Such as that shown in fig. S4.Consequently，during early-to-intermediate growth phases on carbon cloth fibers,homogeneous nucleation predominates [48-5O]，producing aligned nanofibrillar networks where molecular chains follow the topological orientation of carbon fiber substrates.The advantages of homogeneous nucleation lie in its capacity to generate high specific surface areas,ordered structural configurations,and uniform conductive networks,making the suppression of heterogeneous nucleation a critical optimization target in experimental design.

As shown in Fig.1,during high-temperature carbonization, peripherally distributed PANI aggregates formed through heterogeneous nucleation undergo significant mass loss,indicating the preferential thermal degradation of late-stage heterogeneous nucleation products. The preserved architecture primarily consists of linear carbonized PANI nanofibrils maintaining their interconnected network configuration. This carbonized network exhibits hierarchical porosity that provides abundant active sites and efficient mass transport pathways. The increased specific surface area enhances reactant diffusion and interfacial participation across extended electrode surfaces,while improved electrical conductivity facilitates ionic conduction within the battery system.Collectively，these structural enhancements substantially improve the electrochemical reaction kinetics at the electrodeelectrolyte interface.

The surface morphology of the carbon cloth is illustrated in Fig.2.As shown in Fig.2(a),the fiber surfaces of CC and TCC (Fig.S2) share a comparable rough texture characterized by ridge-like features.Fig.2(b) demonstrates that after modification,the carbon cloth surface becomes densely covered with PANI-derived coating structures,displaying distinct heterogeneous nucleation-induced bulk PANI layers at peripheral regions.Following high-temperature carbonization (Fig.2(c) and Fig. S3),these peripheral bulk PANI structures predominantly disappear,leaving a porous reticulated carbon layer composed of filamentous nanofibers.The average diameter of these fibrous structures measures approximately $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { n m } }$ ,significantly enhancing the electrode's specific surface area.Abundant active sites distribute uniformly within the carbon network,while the interlaced PANI-derived carbon framework establishes an optimized conductive network that facilitates accelerated ion diffusion at electrode-electrolyte interfaces,thereby improving mass transport eficiency.Elemental mapping analysis further confirms homogeneous distributions of C,N,and O elements,with substantially increased single-atom markers compared to pristine CC and TCC (Fig.S5 and S6).As evidenced in Fig.S4,prolonged polymerization duration (C-PANI@CC-24h(carbonized)) results in disordered aggregated granular or bulk PANI structures,causing drastic deterioration in carbon layer uniformity.These over-dense irregular configurations impede active material transport,emphasizing the criticality of optimizing polymerization time during chemical synthesis.

# 3.2.Comsol simulation of multiple physical fields

The selection and optimization of carbon cloth electrodes play a crucial role in enhancing the performance of flow batteries,as their properties directly affect the energy efficiency and long-term cycling stability of the system.In this study,multiphysics simulations of the discharge process were performed using COMSOL,revealing that C-PANI@CC exhibits significant improvements in specific surface area and electrical conductivity.The related parameters of multiple physical fields are shown in Table S2,and the flow field grid diagram and grid independence verification of Comsol are shown in Figs.S9-S12 respectively.To verify the model's ability to capture the effect of specific surface area,the simulation results were compared with experimental data obtained from three electrodes with varying specific surface areas (CC,TCC,and C-PANI $@ \mathrm { C C }$ ). By comparing the differences in ion concentration distributions $\scriptstyle ( \mathbf { C r } ^ { 2 + }$ and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ ）and overpotential profiles between CC,TCC and C-PANI@CC during battery discharge,the impact of these structural modifications on battery performance was further investigated.

As shown in Figs. 4(a), 4(d) and $4 ( \mathfrak { g } )$ ,the distribution of $\mathrm { C r } ^ { 2 + }$ ions differs markedly between battery systems assembled with different electrodes.The data from the central positions of the aforementioned simulation plots were compiled and plotted as Fig.4(j).In Fig.4(a), CC's limited specific surface area and low conductivity result in concentrated $\mathrm { C r ^ { 2 + } }$ ion accumulation in the central region of thebattry,forming steep concentration gradients.Benefiting from the enhanced conductivity,the $\mathrm { C r ^ { 2 + } }$ concentration distribution in TCC (Fig.4(d)) is more uniform. In contrast, C-PANI@CC (Fig.4(g)) exhibits a more uniform $\mathrm { C r ^ { + } }$ distribution,which is attributed to enhanced conductivity that facilitates ion transport within the battery.Consequently, C-PANI $@ \mathrm { C C }$ achieves homogeneous ${ \mathrm { C r } } ^ { 2 + }$ distribution，enhancing discharge efficiency and operational stability.

Figs.4(j) and 4(k) display cross-sectional $\mathrm { C r ^ { 2 + } }$ and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ concentration profiles at the mid-plane between Figs. $4 ( \mathbf { a } , \mathbf { d } , \mathbf { g } )$ and $4 ( \mathbf { b } , \mathbf { e } , \mathbf { h } )$ ， respectively.The highest concentrations occur near the central inlet channel, while the modified carbon cloth ensures uniform product distribution near the outlet channels,significantly reducing local concentration polarization and enhancing the redox conversion efficiency of $\mathrm { C r } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { C r } ^ { \mathsf { \bar { 3 } + } }$ and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ .We note that the improvement in concentration uniformity of $\mathrm { C r ^ { 2 + } }$ and $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ in C-PANI@CC, compared to TCC, is not superior in all positions.We speculate that the enhanced uniformity primarily stems from a significant increase in electrical conductivity, which accelerates charge transport.However,the complex carbon layer structure on the surface of C-PANI@CC renders the original pathways more tortuous,thereby impeding the mass transfer process to some extent.This effect is mitigated by the substantial improvement in conductivity.This reasoning can explain why TCC exhibits slightly better concentration uniformity at certain locations,as shown in Figs.4(j) and 4(k).

Fig.4(l) presents the overpotential distribution at the central positions between Figs. 4(c),4(f) and 4(i),showing markedly reduced and more uniform overpotentials for C-PANI@CC.It should be noted that the color scale ranges in the aforementioned figures are not identical.This is because the data range varies significantly,and consequently,imposing a unified color scale would lead to missing color blocks in the visualization.Figs.4(c) 4(f) and 4(i) compare the overpotential distributions of CC,TCC and C-PANI@CC during discharge. CC's low conductivity induces localized high current density regions (Fig.4(c))，generating elevated overpotentials that reduce operational voltage and exacerbate Ohmic losses.Although the overpotential of TCC (Fig.4(f)) was reduced to some extent, the improvement was limited.In contrast, C-PANI@CC (Fig.4(i)) achieves uniform current distribution due to its superior conductivity, significantly lowering local overpotentials and improving energy efficiencystability.

Although our model successfully captures the effects of increased specific surface area and enhanced conductivity,we recognize that polyaniline modification may also alter the intrinsic catalytic activity of the electrode surface,which would influence the kinetic parameters (such as the standard rate constant $k _ { 0 }$ )in the Butler-Volmer expression. Fully decoupling these contributions—quantifying the exact change in intrinsic reactivity relative to the change in geometric surface area--would require more detailed experimental kinetic studies (such as rotating disk electrode measurements on planar analogs).We identify this as an important direction for future work to further refine the model.

# 3.3.Electrochemical performance

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests were conducted on the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ and $\mathrm { C r } ^ { 3 + } / \mathrm { C r } ^ { 2 + }$ redox reactions using an electrochemical workstation to evaluate the electrocatalytic activity of the fabricated electrodes.As shown in Fig.5(a),the CV curves for the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ redox couple demonstrate significant improvements in both anodic peak current $\mathrm { ( I _ { p a } ) }$ and cathodic peak current $\mathrm { ( I _ { p c } ) }$ for C-PANI@CC compared to control samples.Fig.5b shows the negative potential CV tests of three electrode samples at a scan rate of $1 0 \mathrm { \ m V / s }$ .At slow scan rates,the electrochemical activity of CC and TCC was relatively low,making it difficult to obtain stable and well-defined redox peaks.At higher scan rates,the influence of the hydrogen evolution reaction on the measurements would become uncontrollable.Therefore,only the CV data obtained ata scan rate of $1 0 \mathrm { m V } / s$ were selected for discussion regarding the negative electrode.The reduction peak is notably absent in the plot,and the current in the negative potential region increases significantly.This effect results from the combined influence of the hydrogen absorption side reaction [23,53] and non-Faradaic current [54]. The hydrogen evolution side reaction produces a very large and continuously rising back-ground current,which completely obscures the weak $\mathrm { C r } ^ { 3 + }$ reduction peak on the CV curve,making it appear as if no reduction peak exists. The rougher the carbon cloth electrode,the higher its porosity,and the more complex its pore size distribution, the greater the non-ideality of the capacitive behavior,leading to a more pronounced increase in non-Faradaic current toward the negative potential region.When the cathodic current dominates,the ohmic resistance appears to exert a greater influence on the peak current [54],driving the system toward an irreversible state dominated.Fig.5(b) reveals markedly enhanced redox activity for the $\mathrm { C r } ^ { 3 + } / \mathrm { C r } ^ { 2 + }$ couple on C-PANI $@ \mathrm { C C }$ relative to CC and TCC, with substantial increases in peak current densities.This enhancement originates from the abundant active sites provided by the PANI-derived carbon layer，which optimizes overpotential distribution during discharge (Fig.4(i)) and facilitates reaction kinetics for the $\mathrm { C r } ^ { 3 + } / \mathrm { C r } ^ { 2 \mp }$ redox system.

To further validate the improved electrochemical activity of C-$\operatorname { P A N I @ C C }$ ，electrochemical impedancespectroscopy(EIS） was employed to characterize charge transfer resistance $\mathrm { ( R _ { c t } ) }$ duringredox processes (Fig.5(c)).Before conductinga detailed analysis, the consistency between the obtained EIS data and the Kramers-Kronig relations was evaluated,as shown in Fig. S15.The measured and transformed spectra exhibit good overlap,indicating that all three datasets demonstrate satisfactory KK compliance.This confirms that the system remained under steady-state conditions during the measurements and that the impedance data are reliable.C-PANI@CC exhibits comparatively lower charge transfer resistance and reduced diffusion limitations, attributable to its increased active site density and enhanced surface accessibility.For precise comparison of electrochemical characteristics across electrode variants, CV measurements were performed at varying scanrates.

As shown in Fig.5(d-f),all samples exhibit progressive increases in redox current densities with elevated scan rates,though C-PANI@CC consistently demonstrates superior peak current performance.The use of the Randles-Sevcik equation for analyzing peak currents is common in flow battery studies,yet this approach warrants caution and further investigation because the equation assumes a planar electrode,whereas the carbon cloth electrode used in this study is porous and does not fully satisfy this assumption.In the specific electrochemical reaction,the pores within the carbon cloth electrode are filled with electrolyte. Therefore,an electrode with a larger electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) can achieve more sufficient contact and reaction with the electrolyte,leading to a higher peak current [55].At different scan rates, C-PANI@CC exhibits superior activity and higher peak currents,which is attributed to the porous structure and nitrogen-doped active sites introduced by C-PANI@CC.The carbon layer structure increases the effective ECSA,while nitrogen doping provides additional active sites;

# 4.Conclusion

In summary,this work presents a binder-free structural modification strategy,utilizing the self-assembly process of aniline to coat the carbon cloth electrode with a polyaniline layer.Through high-temperature carbonization,a nitrogen-enriched carbon network with an ultra-large specific surface area and abundant active sites was designed,significantly enhancing the reaction kinetics and mass transport processes in iron-chromium redox flow batteries.Compared to the pristine carbon cloth $( 0 . 5 7 6 ~ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { g } )$ ,the specific surface area of C-PANI@CC increased nearly tenfold to $5 . 3 5 1 ~ \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { g }$ ，Efficient nitrogen doping improved local current distribution and accelerated electron conduction.Furthermore, Comsol multiphysics simulations demonstrated that the enlarged specific surface area and superior electrical conductivity significantly reduced the overpotential during discharge,resulting in a more uniform concentration distribution of reaction products and respectively enhanced reaction kinetics of the $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ and $\mathrm { C r } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { C r } ^ { \bar { 3 } + }$ redox couple.Ata high current density of $1 4 0 \mathrm { m A } { \cdot } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ ,C-PANI@CC achieved a high energy efficiency of $8 3 . 8 3 \%$ and maintained stable performance over 3oo cycles.These outstanding electrochemical properties highlight the great potential of C-PANI@CC as an advanced electrode material for ICRFBs.
