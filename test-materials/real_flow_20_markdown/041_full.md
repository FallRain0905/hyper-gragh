# 1．Introduction

In the context of electricity decarbonization,all low-carbon electricity production sources,particularly renewable energies,will contribute to meeting the growing energy demand.The massive integration of these intermittent energy sources can impact the stability of the electrical grid,requiring innovative energy management and storage strategies.Regarding the latter,aqueous organic redox flow batteries (AORFBs) represent an interesting solution as they allow for large-scale and long-term storage $\mathrm { ( > 3 h ) }$ ).Since theavailable energyis related to the volume of external tanks and the power to the number of electrochemical cell stacks,it is possible to adapt the battery to the need, thereby reducing costs.Furthermore,as the redox species are solubilized in water, these batteries are safe (non-flammable,non-explosive) and durable (designed for cycling over 1O-2O years,with recyclable electrolytes).However,these batteries still exhibit modest energy densities and stability issues [1-3].Despite numerous studies focused on the development of reversible organic or organometallic compounds,identifying molecules that simultaneously meet all requirements (high solubility,suitable redox potential,and long-term stability） remains a challenge [4].Regarding the stability,the majority of efforts have been focused on identifying degradation products during battery cycling [5-13],on water transfer [14] and on active species crossover across the membrane [15-17]. However, the role of cycling conditions on battery stability has received comparatively little attention.

Most redox flow batteries are tested at laboratory scale under galvanostatic mode, which involves defining a current density and setting cut-off voltage limits (i.e.cell voltage limits) for charge and discharge. The effect of these limits on battery stability has been studied in the context of all-vanadium batteries.It has been shown that using lower cut-off voltage limits results in lower capacity loss during cycling [18, 19].This is explained by the occurrence of side reactions,such as hydrogen and oxygen evolution and the oxidation of the positive electrode,leading to its degradation.It has also been demonstrated that a higher cell voltage can increase the internal resistance of the battery [20].Thus,reducing the cell voltage can be a strategy to mitigate side reactions and improve stability,although it significantly impacts battery capacity and energy efficiency [21].

In the context of AORFBs,the effect of cell voltage limits has also been observed.Similar to vanadium redox flow batteries,studies have shown that a narrow potential window leads to improved stability [22]. More surprisingly,we demonstrated in the case of 2,3-dihydroxylated anthraquinone,which exhibited a capacity fade rate of $0 . 8 \ \% / \mathrm { d a y } ;$ that lowering the discharge cut-off voltage allowed the recovery of the battery initial capacity [23].Similarly,Y.Jing et al.showed that applying a negative cut-off voltage in discharge allowed the battery capacity to be recovered [24]. The authors explained this phenomenon by the reoxidation of anthrone,formed during the decomposition of hydroanthraquinone generated during charge,back into the original anthraquinone [25,26].They also noticed that the dimerization of anthrone into dianthrone can be limited if a lower oxidation overpotential is used during the charge.In other work,we also studied the effect of the discharge cut-off voltage on the stability of 1,4-dihydroxy-2-carboxymethyl-9,1O-anthraquinone [27].In this case,a higher discharge cut-off voltage resulted in improved stability compared to a lower value.Based on NMR analysis, this improvement was attributed to the fact that at lower potential values,anthraquinone underwent oxidative degradation into a non-electroactive compound.

Design of Experiments (DoE) is an experimental planning strategy based on mathematical and statistical principles that is particularly effective in optimizing the organization of tests within a research project,allowing for the maximum amount of information to be obtained from the minimum number of experiments [28,29].DoE has not yet been widely explored in RFBs.It has been used by Delbeke et al. to identify the most significant parameters influencing the performance of a vanadium redox flow battery and to model their evolution based on their importance [3O].They observed that the most critical parameters were temperature and charge/discharge rates,whereas the effect of the minimum and maximum SOC of the cycle was less significant. To our knowledge,for AORFBs,DoE has not yet been employed to analyze the impact of certain operating parameters on their performance.

In this study,DoE was used as a statistical method to overcome reproducibility issues that can arise in redox flow batteries,to better understand the influence of parameters such as charge and discharge cut-off voltages on AORFB stability.The flow rate was initially included among the studied parameters to verify that it did not influence the results within the investigated range.Indeed,recent studies have highlighted that sufficiently high flow rates promote a uniform distribution of the electrolyte within the porous electrode,which mitigates local concentration overpotentials and consequently impacts battery performance [3l].Alizarin was used as a case study because it has been identified as a promising negolyte for AORFBs,owing to its low cost and commercial availability.However,it has been associated with stability issues in AORFBs,exhibiting a capacity fade rate of $0 . 7 3 \%$ per day, which has been attributed to precipitation,crossover,and decomposition [32].Additionally,we previously observed that the chemical or electrochemical oxidation of alizarin produces a hydroxynaphthoquinone that can be directly utilized in batteries [33].

Ascreening DoE was initially conducted to demonstrate the impact of the studied parameters on battery stability,followed by a quantitative experimental design aimed to identify potential interactions between these parameters.The influence of the discharge cut-off voltage was clearly highlighted.By monitoring the potential of the negative electrode during discharge,we could explain the influence of the cut-off voltages on the battery stability.Although other studies have shown that a low discharge cut-off voltage can promote the dimerization of anthrone,this work highlights the importance of this limit in preventing the direct oxidation of anthraquinone.More generally,it provides insights into the increase or decrease in battery capacity following a deep discharge in systems employing hydroxyanthraquinones as negolytes.

# 2．Materials and methods

# 2.1．Electrochemical analyses

Cyclic voltammetry was performed in a three-electrode cell using a Biologic SP50 potentiostat controlled by the $\operatorname { E C - l a b } \textcircled { 1 }$ software.A gold electrode $( 3 . 1 4 \times 1 0 ^ { - 2 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } )$ ,a platinum counter electrode, and an Ag/ AgCl reference electrode in saturated KCl were used $( 1 0 0 \mathrm { ~ m V ~ } s ^ { - 1 } \ V $ ). Linear sweep voltammetry was performed on a gold rotating disk electrode $( 3 . 1 4 \times 1 0 ^ { - 2 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } )$ from Origalys at a scan rate of $2 0 \ \mathrm { m V } \ s ^ { - 1 }$

# 2.2. Battery tests

Redox flow battery experiments were carried out in an AORFB cell (Fig.S1) developed by KEMIWATT (France),containing two graphite felt electrodes (SGL Carbon) with a thickness of $4 . 6 \mathrm { m m }$ and a geometric surface area of $2 5 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ ,graphite current collectors,and a $5 0 ~ { \mu \mathrm { m } }$ Nafion cationic membrane.The positive and negative electrolytes circulated at room temperature at a flow rate of $2 \mathrm { { 0 } { - } 8 0 } \mathrm { \ m L \ m i \mathrm { { \bar { n } } ^ { - 1 } } }$ through KNF pumps.The negolyte compartment was degassed,and both tanks were protected from light using aluminum foil.Typically,the experiments were performed with $2 5 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ of a $0 . 1 \mathrm { ~ M ~ }$ alizarin solution in 1 M KOH as negolyte,and $2 5 \mathrm { m L }$ ofa $_ { 0 . 2 \mathrm { M } }$ potassium hexacyanoferrate (I) solution in $0 . 8 \mathrm { ~ M ~ }$ KOHas posolyte.For the long-term battery experiment, the battery was cycled for 25 days,corresponding to 1058 cycles. During charging, two current intensities of 5oo and $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m A } }$ were applied successively,while discharging was carried out with three successive current intensities of 500,250,and $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m A } }$ 、Afterimplementing $\mathbf { A g / A g C l }$ reference electrodes in the middle of the graphite felt electrodes with the help of needles (Fig.1), the potential was continuously measured during cycling using a PicoLog 1Ooo series multi-channel voltage data logger. These measurements were recorded simultaneously for both the positive and negative electrodes.

# 2.3.Design of experiments

All the DoE analyses were carried out using the AZURAD software. The parameters, referred to as "factors," that varied during the battery experiments were the cut-off voltage during charging and discharging, and the flow rate.The different values of these factors,referred to as "levels,"define the "experimental matrix,"which represents the set of experiments to be conducted.These will constitute the "responses"of the DoE.Four responses reflecting the stability of the battery were chosen: the capacity,the capacity fade rate,the SOC of the posolyte and the percentage of the new redox couple.Cycling based on 1oo chargedischarge cycles was used for the DoE,providinga good compromise between experimentation time and the number of cycles needed to observe the degradation of the molecule.For the DoE study,the same solutions described above were used.During charging,two current intensities of 500 and $1 0 0 \ \mathrm { \ m A }$ were applied successively,while discharging was carried out with three successive current intensities of 500, 250,and $1 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { m A } }$ to maximize capacity. The tests were conducted at room temperature in the same battery,maintaining the same setup, which was cleaned before each experiment.Two experiments were duplicated.

Alizarin exhibits a reversible system ( $\Delta \mathrm { E } = 3 4 \mathrm { m V } )$ at $- 0 . 8 9 \mathrm { V }$ vs. Ag/ AgCl (Fig.S2).The diffusion coefficient was estimated from the linear correlation of $I p = \mathbf { f } \left( { \sqrt { \nu } } \right)$ (Fig. S3) and was found to be $3 . 2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 6 } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ $s ^ { - 1 }$ . The Koutecky-Levich method was used to determine the apparent electron transfer rate constant $\mathrm { k } ^ { 0 }$ through linear sweep voltammetry on a rotating disk electrode at varying rotation speeds (Fig. S4). The Tafel plot yielded a $\mathrm { k } ^ { 0 }$ value of $\mathring { 8 . 2 } ~ \times ~ 1 0 ^ { - 3 } ~ \mathrm { c m } ~ s ^ { - 1 }$ . Due to its low solubility in water,battery experiments were performed with a O.1 M solution of alizarin in 1M KOH as negolyte and 0.2 M $[ \mathrm { \ K _ { 4 } F e ( C N ) } _ { 6 }$ in $0 . 8 \mathrm { ~ M ~ }$ KOH as posolyte.The battery performance is given in Fig. 2 and in Fig. S7.

We did not attempt to optimize the initial battery capacity (around $8 6 \%$ by modifying the charge cut-off voltage or by adding a potentiostatic step at the end of the galvanostatic charge,as we focused on battery stability,and the charge cut-off voltage was used as a factor in the DoE to assess its impact on battery stability.The energy efficiency was calculated to be $8 4 . 0 4 \%$ due to the cell resistance,which increases the energy required to charge the battery.To investigate the evolution of battery resistance and maximum power asa function of cycling,a polarization curve was performed at $5 0 \%$ SOC every 50 cycles, as shown in Fig. 2c. # 3.2. Design of experiments

# 3.2.1． First design of experiments

To prepare the design of experiments,it is necessary to clearly define the metrics that will be used to evaluate the battery performance in relation to its stability.

i) Battery utilization after 1oo cycles $( \% )$ .This refers to the battery capacity compared to its theoretical capacity,influenced by the charge cut-off voltage.This percentage should be as high as possible.   
ii) Capacity retention (CR) $( \% )$ .This parameter,expressed as a percentage per cycle,reflects the stability of the battery over time.Capacity retention is calculated once the capacity evolution stabilizes.Typically,the firstcharge/discharge cycles are affected by osmotic effects (water transfer),which alter the concentrations of redox species in the tanks.   
iii) State of charge of the posolyte (SOC) after cycling $( \% )$ . This state of charge is estimated using linear sweep voltammetry on a rotating disk electrode and indicates the percentage of posolyte remaining in its charged form (Fe(II)) after the battery discharge. The higher this percentage,the more it reveals an imbalance in the battery caused by secondary reactions occurring in the negolyte. This response is therefore correlated with capacity retention.

With the outputs from the DoE determined,the next step is to identify the parameters that may influence them.The first battery tests allowed us to highlight three factors for the experimental matrix.

- Charge cut-off voltage, Echarge (V).It has been shown that the stability of aqueous organic redox flow batteries is closely related to $\mathrm { E } _ { \mathrm { c h a r g e } }$ ，For instance,the study conducted by Goulet et al.[25] demonstrates that the battery capacity loss was $5 . 6 \%$ per day when the cut-off voltage was set to $1 . 6 \mathrm { V }$ ，whereas it decreased to $0 . 1 4 \%$ per day when a value of $1 . 2 5 \mathrm { V }$ was applied.The authors attributed the high capacity loss at $_ { 1 . 6 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } }$ to the reduction of 2,6-dihydroxyanthraquinone into anthrone. In this work, the charge cut-off voltage was limited to $1 . 3 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ to prevent hydrogen evolution at the negative electrode.

- Discharge cut-off voltage, Edischarge (V).The effect of the discharge cut-off voltage has not been widely studied in the literature.However,our previous work showed that it can also affect the battery stability [23,27].

- Flow rate of the electrolytes $\mathbf { ( m L \ m i n ^ { - 1 } ) }$ ).The flow rate is an important parameter in the operation of redox flow batteries.A study by Cazot et al.[34] showed that the battery capacity increases with the flow rate.This phenomenon can be explained by the substantial delivery of active species and the variation in the mass transport coefficient with the flow rate.The range of flow rates was chosen to correspond to standard cycling conditions.A value of $8 0 \ \mathrm { m L } \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ prevents mass transport limitations,while the value of 20 mL min-1 is close to the flow rates used in industrial systems [1].

To limit the number of experiments to be performed, the chosen factors took a different number of levels,meaning we have limited the variations of each parameter to 2 or 3 values.These levels are presented in Table 1.

The levels of Echarge (factor 1) were chosen to maximize the theoreticalcapacity whileimiting waterelectrolysis.ThelevelsofEdichrge were defined based on preliminary battery experiments,which showed degradation of alizarin during deep discharge $( 0 . 3 \mathrm { ~ V ) }$ ，whereas no additional peak was detected at $_ { 0 . 6 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } }$ 、Finally,the flow rate levels correspond,at the lowest, to the acceptable flow rate in an industrial stack,calculated to minimize the energy cost consumed by the pumps [1].The factors in this screening study did not have the same number of levels,resulting in an asymmetric design.

# 3.2.2.Application of the quantitative method: correlation between the charge and discharge cut-off voltages

The initial screening design enabled us to identify the key factors affecting battery performance:the cut-off voltages during charge and discharge.While the impact of the charge cut-off voltage on battery capacity was anticipated, the influence of the discharge cut-off voltage on its stability was demonstrated. To further investigate potential correlations between these two factors,a more refined DoE was developed.

In this refined approach, the responses were maintained,but the flow rate factor was excluded from the analysis,as it did not influence any of the targeted responses within the studied parameter range.Instead,it was fixed at $5 0 \ \mathrm { m L } \ \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ ,ensuring consistent operating conditions. Additionally, $\mathrm { E } _ { \mathrm { c h a r g e } }$ was restricted to two values, $_ { 1 . 3 \mathrm { V } }$ and $1 . 1 5 \mathrm { V }$ since the variation in battery responses between these values was more significant.This symmetry in the design,with an equal number of levels for each factor,was achieved usinga Hadamard matrix,which is known for its efficiency in ensuring balance between factors [37].To enhance statistical robustness and ensure reproducibility,two experiments were duplicated,resulting in the final experimental design outlined in Table 2.

The interactions between the effects of the factors on battery performance are shown in Fig. 6. These interactions represent the coupling effects that describe the variation in the impact of one factor as a function of the variation in the level of a second factor.

The lack of parallelism between the two lines for capacity retention highlighted an interaction between the effects of the two factors. Notably，when Edischarge is set to $0 . 3 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } _ { i }$ ，capacity retention remains relatively consistent,regardless of the Echarge value.This suggests that at a lower discharge cut-off voltage,the system capacity retention is less sensitive to changes in the charge cut-of voltage.

# 3.3.Electrode potential monitoring

To better understand the role of the cut-off voltages,a reference electrode was introduced to monitor the potential of the graphite felt electrodes during battery operation.Reference electrodes have been previously introduced in RFB to follow the potential during battery charge and discharge,outside at the inlet of each half-cell [38,39] or inside,sandwiched between two pieces of membrane [40,41].In this work,to minimize ohmic drop,contamination of the electrolytes,and to stabilize the reference potential, $\mathbf { A g / A g C l }$ references were positioned in the middle of the graphite felt electrodes with the help of needles (Fig. 1).

This experiment was performed with $0 . 0 1 \ \mathrm { m o l } \ \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ alizarine in 0.8 mol $\mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ KOH as negolyte and $0 . 0 2 \ \mathrm { m o l } \ \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 } \ \mathrm { K } _ { 4 } \mathrm { F e } ( \mathrm { C N } ) _ { 6 }$ in $0 . 8 ~ \mathrm { m o l ~ L } ^ { - 1 }$ KOH as posolyte in galvanostatic mode with an applied current of 50 mA.The evolution of potentials during the charge and the discharge is given in Fig.8.During the charge,the potentials of both electrodes evolved quasi-symmetrically in opposite directions.As expected,the difference between these two potentials increased progressively,as did the cell voltage during the charge.During the discharge,the symmetry of the curves was disrupted,and was clearly distinguished by an abrupt increase in the potential of the negative electrode at the end of the discharge cycle.Under these experimental conditions,the potential of the negative electrode approached a value close to $- 0 . 0 5 \mathrm { V }$ VS $\mathbf { A g / A g C l }$ when the cell voltage reached the discharge cut-off limit of $0 . 3 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$

Given that the apparent electron transfer rate constant of ferrocyanide is 2 times higher than that of alizarin, the electrode potential on the posolyte side remained almost constant during discharge,while that on the negolyte side became increasingly less negative,reaching a value of $- 0 . 0 5 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ Vs $\mathrm { A g / A g C l }$ .We previously reported that the cyclic voltammogram of alizarin exhibited an irreversible oxidation wave with an onset potential of $- 0 . 1 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ vs $\mathbf { A g / A g C l }$ [25].When this potential was reached,a new reversible system appeared at $- 0 . 7 7$ Vvs $\mathbf { A g / A g C l }$ Direct oxidation of alizarin at $- 0 . 0 5 \mathrm { V }$ vs $\mathbf { A g / A g C l }$ allowed us to identify the structure of the reversible compound at $- 0 . 7 7 \mathrm { V }$ Vs Ag/AgCl by NMR and mass spectrometry, namely naphthoquinone 2,3-HButCNQ (Scheme 1）[25]. # 3.4．Discussion

It has been previously shown that anthraquinones undergo reduction during charge,either directly or viaa disproportionation reaction into anthrone and dianthrone [24].These reduced species are electrochemically irreversible,leading to irrecoverable capacity loss over repeated charge-discharge cycles.This could explain the capacity loss during the battery cycling of alizarin when using a discharge cut-off voltage of 0.6 V.Interestingly,when the discharge cut-off voltage is set to $0 . 3 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } _ { : }$ the battery exhibits improved stability,despite the appearance of a new reversible redox system in cyclic voltammetry.While this may initially appear contradictory,two phenomena can explain this enhancement in stability.The first concerns the reoxidation of anthrone and dianthrone into the initial anthraquinone during a deep discharge,as reported by Jing etal.[24].As demonstrated in this work,the second phenomenon is the direct oxidation of alizarin into 2,3-HButCNQ during deep discharge. This 4-electron oxidation [33] enables the battery to regain capacity through the formation of a reversible redox species,which is subsequently reduced during the next charging cycle and actively participates in the battery operation.Since the redox potentials of alizarin and 2,

3-HButCNQ are relatively close,the battery power is minimally affected.The Design of Experiments (DoE) reveals an interaction between $\mathrm { E } _ { \mathrm { c h a r g e } }$ and Edischarge in relation to capacity retention, which can be explained by anthrone formation being promoted at high Echarges while low Edischarge facilitates the reoxidation of anthrone back to the original alizarin,and subsequently to 2,3-HButCNQ.Unlike recent reports in the literature [32],no precipitate was observed under our cycling conditions,which may be related to the capacity loss.Furthermore,the possibility of regenerating anthraquinone through chemical, rather than electrochemical, oxidation of the corresponding anthrone and dianthrone by dioxygen,as previously reported,was also considered [25,42]．However，we observed through cyclic voltammetry the appearance of the reversible system at $- 0 . 7 7 \mathrm { V }$ during prolonged oxygen bubbling in an alizarin solution (Fig. S8).The presence of $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ thus leads to the slow oxidation of the anthraquinone into 2,3-HButCNQ,which compromises its regeneration by $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$

# 4.Conclusions

In this study,we investigated the underlying reasons for the greater stability of alizarin when the discharge cut-off voltage was set at $_ { 0 . 3 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } }$ compared to $0 . 6 \mathrm { V }$ ,despite the appearance of a degradation product in cyclic voltammetry.A first DoE highlighted the significant role of both charge and discharge cut-off voltages on battery stability,while confirming that the flow rate had no discernible impact within the studied range.The subsequent Lenth analysis revealed a notable interaction between charge and discharge cut-off voltages,underscoring their interconnected effects on performance.We monitored the potential of the solutions in the graphite felt by introducing reference electrodes. It was shown that the electrode in the negolyte compartment reached a sufficiently high potential during deep discharge to enable the oxidation of alizarin into naphthoquinone,whereas the potential of the posolyte was stable throughout the cycling.This behavior has been attributed to the lower kinetics of alizarin compared with ferrocyanide.The apparent higher stability of the battery occurring when a deep discharge was used was therefore attributed to two factors: the regeneration of alizarin through the oxidation of its reduction products,anthrone and dianthrone,and the oxidation of anthraquinone into a soluble and reversible compound.

These results show that the regeneration of hydroxyanthraquinones through deep discharge or reaction with dioxygen,as previously reported [25,42],can be compromised by their tendency to oxidize at low potential.However,an improvement in stability can be observed with deep discharge if their oxidation leads to stable,reversible naphthoquinones,such as 2,3-HButCNQ in the case of alizarin.This type of behaviour can also be generalized to other hydroxyanthraquinones, which yield reversible naphthoquinone species upon oxidation,as we have already observed for Alizarin Red Sand purpurin [33].

Beyond the specific behavior of hydroxyanthraquinones,this work highlights the value of using design-of-experiment approaches to better understand how cycling parameters influence battery performance. Such an approach could be extended to other key metrics,such as roundtrip efficiencyand energy density,provided that it is applied to chemically stable organic redox systems operating over the full aqueous potential window.
