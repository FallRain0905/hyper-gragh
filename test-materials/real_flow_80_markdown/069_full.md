# Abstract

The imperative for long-duration energy storage (LDES） is rapidly intensifying as global energy systems transition towards high penetration of variable renewables. Among diverse LDES technologies， polysulfide-based redox flow bateries (PSRFBs） stand out due totheir ultra-low-cost potential from earth-abundant sulfur, high solubility of polysulfides,and inherent safety of aqueous electrolytes.However, their commercialization path remains hindered not merely by material-level limitations-such as sluggish kinetics or shutle effects-but more critically, by system-level failure mechanisms that remain inadequately addressed. This review provides a failure-centric analysis of PSRFBs, shifting the conventional material-focused perspective toward a system integrity and recoverability viewpoint. We examine the evolving market and technical requirements for LDES, then systematically deconstruct the four primary failure modes of PSRFBs: interfacial kinetic limitations, electrolyte instability, crossover-induced cross-contamination, and capacity imbalance without efective rebalancing strategies. For each failure mode, we critically assess recent advances in electrodes, electrocatalysts, electrolytes, and membranes—not as isolated material innovations,but through the lens of their contribution to system-level failure mitigation. A dedicated section addresses the electrolyte rebalancing strategy, outlining future research directions that prioritize engineering scalability and capacity recoverability over short-term performance metrics， aiming to transform PSRFBs from a promising lab-scale technology into a commercially viable cornerstone of future carbon-free grids.

# 1 Introduction

The escalating imperatives of climate change mitigation and sustainable development are driving a profound transformation of the global power sector. 1 A successful transition hinges on phasing out unabated fossl-fuel-based generation and massively scaling up carbon-free energy sources, primarily variable renewables like wind and solar.² This dual challenge—simultaneously replacing existing infrastructure and expanding capacity to meet growing electrification demands —is compounded by the inherent intermittency of renewable resources. Unlike dispatchable fossil fuel plants，renewable generation cannot be reliably scheduled to match peak demand, necessitating novel strategies to ensure grid flexibility and reliability.³ In this context, Long-Duration Energy Storage (LDES), defined as technologies capable of storing and delivering energy over periods of hours to weeks, emerges as a critical enabler for a decarbonized, resilient, and cost-effective power system.4-6

Although a universally accepted definition of long-duration energy storage (LDES) does not yet exist, several definitions have been proposed. According to a report in China Energy News, a large-cale, low-cost storage technology capable of continuous discharge for no less than $^ { 4 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ and with a lifespan of at least 2O years can be considered LDES 7.The Long Duration Energy Storage Council, by contrast, defines LDES as a technology that can store energy and then dispatch it over extended periods ranging from 8 h to days, weeks, or even seasons8. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE)takes a stricter view, requiring reliable electricity delivery for 10 to approximately $1 0 0 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } ^ { \ast }$ 9. Despite the lack of a single agreed-upon definition,it is widely understood that the need for LDES is closely tied to the share of renewable energy generation in a given region. Industry consensus suggests that when renewables account for more than $2 0 \%$ of electricity generation, LDES systems with at least $^ { 4 \mathrm { h } }$ of duration become a necessity. When that share reaches $5 0 { - } 8 0 \%$ ， the required duration rises to $^ { 1 0 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ or more.1° In China, renewable energy already makes up over $3 5 \%$ of total electricity generation—well above the $2 0 \%$ threshold that calls for $^ { 4 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ storage. Yet the average duration of energy storage currently deployed in the country remains far below 4 hours. This gap makes it clear that adhering to a threshold of $^ { 4 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ for long-duration storage is no longer sufficient. A higher standard for LDES ( $\geq 8$ hours for example) is urgently needed.

The LDES encompasses a diverse portfolio of technologies that store and release energy via mechanical, thermal, electrochemical, or chemical pathways.1l (Figure 1) Their defining value proposition is the ability to provide large-scale,long-term flexibility at a competitive cost, filling a crucial service gap that short-duration solutions like lithium-ion batteries cannot addres.12,13

While performance characteristics vary widely across different LDES technologies, several key features are universally advantageous for grid integration.14 A cornerstone is the decoupling of power and energy capacities, which alows for independent scaling and leads to significantly lower marginal costs for increasing storage duration. This architecture enables the design of systems optimized for long discharge times without proportionally escalating power-related expenses.15-1Furthermore,ideal LDES technologies exhibit fewer geographical constraints, reduced reliance on critical materials,and potentially shorter deployment timelines compared to traditioal grid expansion,oferingsubstantialoperationalandplanningbenefits.18-20

Among the spectrum of LDES candidates, aqueous Redox Flow Batteries (RFBs) present a highly compatible profile. RFBs are celebrated for their long cycle life, inherent safety, scalability, and potential for low system costs, making them one of the most promising electrochemical LDES platforms. 21-24

Their operational principle epitomizes the LDES advantage: energy capacity is determined by the volume and concentration of electrolyte stored in external tanks，while power rating is governedbythe sizeand numberof electrochemical cellstacks.2-29This design notonlyallows for cost-effective customization across a wide range of discharge durations and power levels for diverse grid services but also means that as storage duration extends,the cost contribution of the power stack is effectively diluted. 30 The ultimate lower bound for cost reduction in this model, however, is set by the expense of the electrolyte (active materials,solvent, supporting salt). Therefore, developing ultra-low-cost electrolyte systems is paramount for RFBs to realize their full LDES potential.21

Currently, vanadium RFBs represent the most technologically mature flow battery system, yet their high electrolyte cost (approximately $\yen 1000$ remains a significant barrier to widespread LDES deployment.31 Research indicates that achieving cost targets requires breakthroughs either in drastically reducing material costs or in increasing the energy density of the system, all while maintaining essential attributes of safety, durability, and scalability. 32 This pursuit has directed significant attention towards earth-abundant alternatives.

Sulfur, a low-cost byproduct of industrial processes, stands out as an exceptionally promising charge-storage material. 33The high-temperature molten sodium-sulfur bateries34，35and nonaqueouslithium-sulfurbateries36.37havebeenextensivelyexploredaqueous polysulfide-based redox flow batteries offer a compelling route to low-cost, safe,and scalable stationary storage. However, their path from laboratory promise to commercial fabrication is hindered by several intrinsic challenges (Figure 2). These include the shuttle effect of soluble polysulfide species,parasitic side reactions,the poor electrical conductivity of sulfur, and the limited solubility of long-chain polysulfides,all of which impact efficiency, longevity,and energy density.38

Most current research on polysulfide-based redox flow batteries has been predominantly confined to lab-scale， material-oriented investigations, such as conventional electrode modification, catalyst optimization, electrolyte formulation design, and separator development. These efforts are often conducted within a fragmented evaluation framework and lack explicit relevance to engineering-scale applications. More critically, the existing literature tends to treat each material challenge in isolation, overlooking the fact that in a real-world system, these limitations interact synergistically to produce system-level failures that cannot be remedied by any single material innovation alone. Capacity fading caused by imbalance between the positive and negative electrolytes， along with corresponding rebalancing strategies， remains critically understudied—yet this is precisely the failure mode that determines whether a PSRFB system can achieve its theoretically projected 20,000 cycle lifetime.

In response to these challenges, this review provides a comprehensive analysis at the intersection of growing LDES market demand and the pursuit of affordable, large-scale storage solutions. However, rather than offering another incremental catalog of material advances,we adopt a failure-centric perspective: we ask not "how can we improve the materials?" but "what causes PSRFB systems to fail, and how can we design materials and system architectures to prevent, mitigate,or reverse these failures?" The article critically examines the latest research advances through this lens, systematically analyzes the persistent barriers to industrialization, and offers a forward-looking perspective on future directions,with particular emphasis on the underexplored domain of electrolyte rebalancing-a necessary precondition for long-term operation in asymmetric PSRFB systems, yet one for which no established engineering solutions currently exist.

# 2.1 LDES technologies current status

The potential of long-duration energy storage (LDES) technologies-including flow batteries, compressed air, gravity-based, and thermal storage—to enhance the integration of low-cost wind and solar power while reducing the overall cost of decarbonized energy systems has spurred a wave of new commercial initiatives and research and experimental development (R&D) efforts. These systems are capable of storing up to 24 hours or days of energy，with the flexibility to extend their energy storage duration further as needed.9,12

According to a Bloomberg New Energy Finance (BNEF） research report, cumulative investment in major LDES companies surpassed USD 2.5 billion in 2O21, nearly tripling over the preceding four years. 15 More than 260 LDES projects have been announced worldwide across various commercial stages, totaling approximately 5 GW and 65 GWh. Roughly 230 of these projects are already contracted, under construction, or operational. Flow battries account for the highest number of individual projects (over 100). However, thermal LDEs 39,40 and compressed airenergystorage (CAES) 41．42represent themajorityoftotalannounced capacity—approximately $6 0 \%$ and $3 0 \%$ ，respectively—benefiting from moreadvanced commercial demonstration and favorable cost developments. While the global average capital expenditure for thermal LDES is approximately $\$ 232/456$ and for CAES about $\$ 293/40$ both lower than that of redox flow batteries (RFBs） at nearly $\$ 500/\mathrm { k W h }$ -these technologies face deployment constraints compared to RFBs，such as larger physical footprints and limited modularity. 16

Lithium-ion battries (LIBs） currently dominate the energy storage market， typically providing storage durations of 2\~4 hours or less (Figure 3). Outside China, where LIB costs are generally higher, several LDES technologies are already more cost-competitive for durations exceeding 8 hours. Within China, however, most LDES options—including RFBs—still struggle to compete due to the significantly lower cost of locally produced LIBs.LIBs benefit from massive scale across both the transportation and power sectors, which continues to drive down costs. In summary， alongside supportive policies and regulatory mechanisms， achieving competitive project costs is essential to spur early adoption and accelerate commercialization of LDES technologies. For RFBs in particular, reducing investment costs through technological and manufacturing innovations remains a critical priority.16

Typically, the cost breakdown of a flow batery system is a decisive factor governing its techno-economic viability and applicability to specific use cases. Figure 1 succinctly elucidates the striking disparities in cost architecture between two systems: the VRFB and the PSRFB.

For VRFB systems,electrolyte costs constitute approximately $4 0 \%$ of total expenditure, representing a fundamental botleneck to further cost reduction. Concurrently, the stack accounts for $3 5 \%$ , while the remaining $2 5 \%$ is allocated to auxiliary components including pumps, piping, and control systems. In sharp contrast, and corroborated by industry survey data, the electrolyte cost of PSRFB systems is merely 1/5 that of VRFBs. This significant economic advantage arises from the inherently low cost and high natural abundance of sulfur-based active materials. Nevertheless,PSRFBs suffer from substantially lower current and power densities,a limitation imposed by the relatively sluggish redox kinetics of polysulfide species. Consequently, to deliver an equivalent power output, the volume of a PSRFB stack is approximately three times that of a VRFB stack. This means that considerably more membrane material (e.g., Nafion) is required, and catalysts must also be used. This disparity directly inflates the stack's cost contribution, establishing it as the dominant cost driver (accounting for $7 5 \%$ of system cost),while auxiliary 一 systems comprise the remaining $2 0 \%$ . (Figure 4)

Therefore, for energy storage with a relatively short duration (which we estimate to be less than 8 hours), the investment cost of iron-sulfur flow batteries is higher than that of all-vanadium flow batteries.This is because the stack and other components account for a relatively high proportion of the total system cost. However, as the energy storage duration increases—that is, as the electrolyte cost becomes a larger share of the overall system cost—sulfur-based flow battries gain a cost advantage over vanadium-based flow batteries. Overall, compared to VRFBs, PSRFBs exhibit a markedly superior economic profile for long-duration energy storage (LDES) applications.

Therefore, the two pathways diverge in their core strategies for cost optimization from a technological development standpoint. For VRFBs, cost reduction efforts are primarily focused on electrolyte recycling， vanadium recovery, and the synthesis of low-cost electrolytes, alongside stack engineering innovations to optimize power-side costs. For PSRFBs， however, the key technological breakthrough hinges on enhancing stack power density. This entailsthe development of high-activity catalytic electrodes, highly selective membranes,and systems to accelerate electrode reaction kinetics,thereby ameliorating the power density limitation and mitigating the engineering challenges posed by the comparatively large stack volume.

# 3.4 limitation of aqueous polysulfide-based redox flow batteries

Despite these significant advantages, sulfur-based redox flow bateries (RFBs) have yet to achieve widespread commercial adoption, primarily due to three persistent challenges associated with polysulfides: sluggish redox kinetics， shuttle effects and electrolyte instability. These limitations continue to hinder their practical implementation.97

The first major challenge stems from the inherently sluggish redox kinetics of polysulfide electrochemistry，which fundamentally restricts the efficiency of polysulfide-based RFBs. In aqueous solutions, elemental sulfur undergoes reduction through disulfide $( - \mathsf { S } - \mathsf { S } - )$ bond cleavage, sequentiall forming short-chain polysulfides $( { \mathsf { S } _ { \mathrm { n } } } ^ { 2 - } )$ and sulfide ions $( \mathsf { S } ^ { 2 - } )$ 98.99 However, aqueous sulfur redox chemistry suffers from a slow conversion process between $\mathrm { S n } ^ { 2 - }$ and $\mathrm { S } ^ { 2 - }$ , compounded by the complex thermodynamics of polysulfides in solution. Therefore, electrocatalysts are widely used to expedite polysulfide redox and improve the battery efficiency. However, the existing form of sulfur species in aqueous environments during electrochemical reactions is still not fully understood. In conventional organic electrolyte-based sulfur batteries,sulfides typically exist in stable forms (e.g., $\mathrm { L i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { S }$ ， $\mathrm { N a } _ { 2 } S$ or ${ \bf K } _ { 2 } { \bf S }$ ） without undergoing hydrolysis.While,in an aqueous electrolyte,the forms of sulfides ( $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { S }$ (aq), $\mathrm { H S ^ { - } }$ ,and $\mathrm { S } ^ { 2 - }$ ）are highly dependent on factors such as pHandelectrolyteconcentration.-102Thesefactorssignificantlyinfluenceboththe thermodynamics and kinetics of sulfidesreaction.89

The second fundamental limitation arises from the insufficient selectivity of existing membranes against polysulfide crossover. Polysulfide species, characterized by their small ionic size and low charge density, readily migrate across membranes driven by concentration gradients. Even cation-exchange membranes (CEMs) often fail to prevent this crossover. Once migrated, polysulfides participate in parasitic reactions with catholyte components, leading to active material loss, progressive capacity fading, and eventual system failure. The consequences of shuttle effects vary with the catholyte chemistry: with high-potential catholytes such as $\mathrm { B r } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { B r } ^ { - }$ , polysulfides may be oxidized to sulfate,lowering electrolyte $\mathrm { p H }$ and releasing $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathbf { S }$ 103；with lower-potential catholytes such as[Fe(CN)6]3-/4or I3-/I, polysulfides tend to precipitate as sulfur (S8), increasing cell overpotential and clogging flow channels. 32

The third challenge relates to the intrinsic stability of polysulfide electrolytes. The Gibbs free-energy of many polysulfides species are close to each other (Table 3), indicating the possible co-existence and conversion of these anions in the solvents by a series of chemical equilibriums. In most organic or aqueous electrolytes, the conversion reactions between the long-chain and short-chain polysulfides lead to the formation of undesirable insoluble intermediate polysulfide species. Beyond these well-known material-level challenges,a critical yet often overlooked issue in practical deployment is capacity fade. A frequently cited advantage of RFBs over lithium-ion bateries is their theoretical immunity to capacity loss. Although many RFB systems—including aqueous polysulfide RFBs—claim cycle lives exceeding 20,0o0 cycles, our survey of commercial manufacturers reveals that no large-scale installation has actually achieved this lifetime. Such figures are typically extrapolated from small-scale cell tests or the durability of individual components. In reality, RFBs deployed for grid storage frequently experience continuous capacity decay. While crossover is not the sole cause of capacity decline within RFBs, it is often the largest contributor and may halve the accessble capacity within 100-20o cycles. 107

One established strategy to mitigate crossover-induced capacity loss is the use of symmetric chemistries, where both half-cells employ the same parent active species. 108 In such systems, crossover does not cause irreversible contamination,and capacity can be restored through periodic electrolyte rebalancing- -transferring and mixing electrolyte between tanks to re-equalize active species concentrations. Here,crossover leads to cross-contamination and associated capacity losses,making capacity restoration far more challenging. The technical feasibility and economic implications of rebalancing in such systems remain poorly understood and rarely reported.

# 4.1.3 Both impact on commercial stack design

As we discussed above， porous carbon felts serve as the electrode, providing sites for electrochemical reactions and pathways for electrolyte transport. Catalysts are typically loaded onto the carbon fiber surfaces via methods including electrodeposition, thermal treatment, or solution-based coating. The in-situ modification of electrode or catalyst loading profoundly affects both the electrode's pore structure and its permeability, creating inherent trade-os that must be carefully balanced，particularly in commercial stack designs. The design trade-offs primarily center on two key aspects: maximizing electrochemical activity (achieved by high catalyst loading, large surface area, and abundant active sites）l65 and maintaining efficient fluid transport (which requires high porosity and permeability to minimize pumping losses and facilitate mass transport)16. This trade-off is fundamentally rooted in the fact that the same pore space that must be open for electrolyte flow is simultaneously required to host the catalytic sites that drive the

redox reactions.

From a structure perspective, the electrode must be suiciently permeable to allow the electrolyte to reach all reactive zones, especially the regions deep within the felt thickness. Poor permeability causes maldistribution of flow, creating dead zones where the electrolyte stagnates and reacting species are depleted. This issue is especially severe under high current density operation, where reactant consumption is rapid and must be compensated by continuous convective supply. Generally, decreased porosity results in increased tortuosity (the winding path fluids must traverse), directly reduces the eective diffusivity of species, finally can limit the overall current density. In fact, experimental studies on compressed carbon felts have shown that reducing porosity directly and rapidly decreases permeability: a $6 7 \%$ mechanical compression resulted in a $5 8 \%$ reduction in permeability and a $2 5 \%$ reduction in single-phase diffusion.167 Besides, at high loading densities, catalyst particles can create local blockages or dead-end pores that further impede flow. This effect occurs more severely for suspended particle catalysts than for pore throats.

From another perspective, excess catalyst loading may not yield proportional benefits in actual operation. In fact, experimental and modeling studies have demonstrated that beyond a certain threshold, additional surface area cannot be accessed by reacting species due to transport limitations of reactants into the deep pore structure. This limit to "useful surface area" is dictated by rate of reaction and transport within the electrode. 168 As consequence, it suggests there is an optimum catalyst loading: too low loading results in poor kinetics; too high loading creates a diffusion-limited regime where the outer catalyst layers are active but the inner layers become inaccessible, wasting catalyst material and increasing fabrication cost.

In a commercial stack, where multiple cells are assembled in series to achieve practical voltage and power, the above trade-off is highly critical due to several practical constraints:

Pumping Power Costs: In large-scale RFBs,pumping the electrolyte through the stack accounts for a significant fraction of the plant's auxiliary power consumption and operational expenditure.High catalyst loading that reduces permeability forces higher pumping rates to maintain sufficient flow, which reduces overall system eficiency. Therefore， stack-level optimization requires balancing pumping power against electrochemical performance.

Flow Field Configuration: The interaction between the flow field and the porous electrode microstructure is critical. There is a systematical investigation of electrode microstructures (paper, cloth,felt) with prevailing flow fields (flow-through,serpentine, interdigitated), which reveals that interdigitated flow fields combined with paper electrodes and flow-through configurations combined with cloth electrodes provide the most favorable trade-of between liquid flowing and electrochemical performance.169 This analysis streses the importance of co-designingflow fields and electrode microstructures in tandem.

Mechanical Compression Effects: In stack assembly, electrodes are mechanically compressed to reduce ohmic losses. Studies on three commercially available porous electrodes (paper, felt, cloth) across compression ratios of $0 \% { - } 5 0 \%$ identified optimal compression ratios of $3 0 \%$ for felt, $2 0 \%$ for paper,and $2 0 \%$ for cloth to achieve the best trade-off between electrochemical performance and pressre drop.17o This demonstrates that even small changes influenced by catalyst loading in compression substantially affect system-level performance.

Manufacturing and Cost Considerations: From a commercial perspective, catalyst loading directly impacts material cost and manufacturing complexity.171 High catalyst loading using expensive noble metals is economically prohibitive for large-scale RFBs.

Consequently, in commercial PSRFBs, the trade-off between catalytic activity and electrode permeability directly impacts two key economic parameters: system eficiency (via pumping losses, for example) and capital cost (via catalyst material and stack sizing). High catalyst loading can reduce permeability, forcing higher pumping power that lowers system effciency. Conversely, insufficient loading may require larger stacks to meet power targets, raising system cost. To balance these factors, industrial design for multi-kW stacks, should adopt moderate and uniformly dispersed catalyst loaded electrodes that achieve adequate activity without severely compromising liquid flow performance. Meanwhile,flow field engineering and optimized compression of electrodes are used to compensate for moderate permeability reductions.

# 4.3 Shuttle effect mitigation via additives and membrane design

Generally， cation exchange membranes(CEMs） including Nafion61，180，PB1196 and SPEEK- $\cdot \mathrm { K } ^ { 9 6 }$ 197 are always used in polysulfideredox flowbateries. Atypical CEMis composedof a hydrophobic polymer backbone with recurrently tethered side chains bearing hydrophilic anionic end groups. Once hydrated, the polymers are rearranged into a phase-separated structure, where aggregation of the end anions forms percolated nanoscale cation transport channels. The negative charges fixed on the polymer chains allow transport of cations and electrostatically repulse the anion at the electrolyte/membrane interface.198 However,even with CEMs, polysulfide crossover through the membrane to the counter electrode side has widely occurred, just like it happens in Li-S batteries. Polysulfide shuttling can lead to the accumulation of solid sulfur at the counter electrode side, which increases cell resistance,clogs flow channels,and frequently results in capacity fade and cellfailure during long-term cycling.199 200

Based on the current CEM microstructure models about ion transport mechanisms201-203, the common characteristics are that the water channels exist as inverted micelles with the fixed anions located on the walls for cation hopping and an equal amount of counterions must be present together to maintain electroneutrality. Upon hydration with an anion electrolyte,if the water channel size permits,anions will diffuse into the membrane driven by the concentration gradient. The Donnan potential at the interface, which points from the solution to membrane direction, was formed due to the uneven ion partitioning between the external bulk solution and the membrane internal under equilibrium conditions.204 The higher the Donnan potential,the better co-ion blocking ability.193

Therefore, both the anions themself and the water channels of membrane affect the crossover of polysulfides. On the one hand, the size of hydrated anions determines the steric hindrance the co-ions encounter before diffusing into the electroneutral phase in the membrane. On another hand, the charge density of anions affects the repulsion by the Donnan potential. Those with larger size or higherchargedensityarelessprone tocrossover through themembrane.205,206Foreample, polysulfides with small ionic size and low charge density often encounter inadequate resistance against shutling the CEM,while polymeric redox molecules are characteristic of negligible crossover even with microporous separators due to the tremendously expanded molecular size.207

Inaddition， the polysulfide anion concentration in the bulk solution governs the concentration gradient across the solution/membrane interface. An increase in bulk polysulfide concentration reduces the Donnan potential, meaning that high polysulfide concentrations in flow cells will consequentlypromote polysulfidecrossover.193,198Furthermore,the highlyhydrated environment, which results in a high water volume fraction within the membrane, may also contribute to polysulfide leakage. This is because strong solvation of the membrane's fixed anions by water molecules can weaken their repulsive interaction with polysulfides, thereby reducing the membrane's blocking ability.208

# 4.4 Capacity recovery via electrolyte rebalancing

In the ideal redox flow batteries, the amount of oxidized and reduced species remains the same during subsequent charge and discharge cycles,so that the catholytes and anolytes stay balanced. Unfortunately， in a real RFBs different processes cause imbalances between the catholytes and anolytes. electrolyte imbalance, such as cross contamination and concentration imbalance, occur and result in decrease of cycle life, performance degeneration and capacity loss, which is one of te main failures of large-scale redox flow battery systems.26,227 Crossoverof active species is inevitable in large-scale redox flow battery systems， despite numerous breakthroughs in membrane, electrolyte, and electrode materials. Unless the active species of the redox flow battery are based on a single parent compound, crossover would directly lead to cross-contamination. When using single parent compound as active species, i.e. all vanadium redox flow battries,crossover caused associated capacity losses are recoverable via periodic electrolyte rebalancing，which allows the electrolyte to be used indefinitely and significantly reduces maintenancecosts.109 However,based on the polysulfide-based redox flow batteries using diferent active species in the catholytes and anolytes，active species crossover results in cross-contamination. Since asymmetric chemistries in the positive and negative half-cels, traditional rebalancing strategies may not suitable, capacity-loss remediation are expected to remain challenges. rr

Electrolyte imbalances can be distinguished into two types: concentration imbalance and redox imbalance.228 (Figure 14)

(i) The concentration imbalance can be characterized by changes in the concentrations of active species in each half-cell Source of this kind of imbalance can be: active species crossover through the membrane, water transfer through the membrane, or the evaporation of water.29-31

(ii)The redox imbalance is characterized by a shift in the formal oxidation states of the mixed electrolytes to higher oxidation states (oxidative imbalance) or the formal oxidation state is shifted towards lower oxidation states (reductive imbalance); In all vanadium redox flow battry, this imbalance can be caused by hydrogen evolution, V(II) oxidation from contact with air , reduction of $\mathrm { V } ( \mathrm { V } )$ or precipitation of V(V） at higher temperature.232 In polysulfide-based redox flow batteries,hydrogen evolution is still existed and the release of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } \mathbf { S }$ is also a challenge. Besides, polysulfides are easily oxidized due to their strong reducing properties, such as oxygen in the air.192 Thus,redox imbalance would remain in polysulfide-based redox flow batteries. However,reports on established rebalancing strategies for asymmetric polysulfide-based flow batteries remain scarce. Accordingly, this section first discusses representative strategies from symmetric flow batteries (all-vanadium) and from non-aqueous Li-S systems. These examples are not put forward as ready-to-use solutions, but as mechanistic inspirations for developing truly applicable rebalancing protocols for PSRFBs.

# 4.4.1 Physical Regeneration

Some methods are focused on physical treatments such as the remixing of the positive electrolyte with the negative one. (Figure 14a) Experimental results show that mixing the positive and negative electrolytes effectively prolongs the cycle number and improves the discharge capacity. Moreover, the mixing process is simple, economic in terms of additional investment cost, and can be applied straight forwardly to industrial storage systems.107 When the mixing process is terminated,in both tanks contain a mix of ionse $\mathrm { V O } ^ { 2 + }$ and $\mathrm { V } ^ { 3 + }$ ） and the battery needs to be pre-charged to oxidize all V(III) in the positive tank and to reduce all V(IV) in the negative tank. Once this process is completed, the battery can be operated regularly. The capital costs of this method may regard a secondary hydraulic circuit, regulating valves and sensors, and a control software to be implemented in the BMS. The operative costs are due to: (i) the energy consumed by the pumps; (ii) the energy for recharging the battery at the end of the process.233 However, as discussed above,this approach is not applicable to polysulfide-based flow batteries because the positive and negative electrolytes use different species.

It has been reported that Abdulmonem Fetyan et al. used asymmetric flow rates in the negative and positive half-cells to mitigate the effect of electrolyte imbalance (Figure 14b).234 They proposed that the different viscosity of the vanadium (II) ions in the negative half-cell builds inconsistent pressure profiles at both half-cells during charge-discharge cycling and therefore magnifies ions and water transfer tendency. Therefore,over different current densities of 50,100,and $1 5 0 \mathrm { m A c m } ^ { - 2 }$ , the use of one magnitude flow factor lower in the negative half-cell and one magnitude flow factor higher in the positive half-cell is superior to the symmetric flow rates on reducing electrolyte imbalance and subsequently, improving the capacity retention.\

For asymmetric PSRFBs, physical mixing of the two electrolytes is not a viable rebalancing method because it would permanently cross-contaminate the different active species. Instead, selective rebalancing must be achieved through chemical or electrochemical means that target only the degraded half-cell without affecting the other.

The chemical regeneration approaches discussed above underscore a fundamental distinction between PSRFBs and symmetric systems such as al- vanadium RFBs. In symmetric systems, crossover causes temporary imbalance but not permanent contamination—physical mixing of the two electrolytes restores capacity. For asymmetric PSRFBs， however， crossover leads to irreversible cross-contamination, making physical mixing ineffective and even detrimental. This imposes a necessary precondition for commercial viability: the development of chemical rebalancing methods that can selectively restore active material without introducing new impurities or disrupting the delicate chemical equilibrium of the two half-cells.

Crucially, polysulfide chemistry offers a potential pathway to meet this challenge-not as an inherent advantage,but as a design opportunity. The work by Cui et al. on "dead sulfide" reactivation via thermal treatment with sulfur demonstrates that sulfide species,once considered irreversibly lost, can be chemically resurrected through sulfur's rich interconversion chemistry. This suggests that targeted chemical interventions-leveraging the very reactions that cause degradation—could form the basis of in-situ chemical rebalancing protocols. Developing such methods, with minimal additional hardware and automated triggering by the battery management system, represents a high-impact research frontier that could transform PSRFBs from a promising concept into a commercially viable LDES technology.

In short， the rebalancing is imperative for asymmetric PSRFBs. Current research on rebalancing methods for polysulfide-based flow bateries remains limited. Nevertheless, existing approaches has still inspired two potential pathways:

(1) Through engineering design, adding external devices to large-scale PSRFB energy storage systems to extract electrolyte for non-in-situ processing. Although this method undoubtedly makes system design more complex.

(2 ）Utilizing the interconversion between sulfur and polysulfides,which ofers another effective route toward electrolyte regeneration.

Further development in this area may ultimately enable polysulfide-based flow batteries to achieve long-term operation with reduced maintenance costs, thereby supporting their future application in large-scale energy storage systems. We propose that future PSRFB development should adopt a Design for Recoverability paradigm—where materials, interfaces,and system architectures are co-designed not only for initial performance,but for their ability to be chemically reset or regenerated in situ.

# 5 Conclusion and Future Perspectives

Building upon the preceding analys1s, it 1s clear that even within the LDES landscape, redox flow bateries (RFBs） do not represent the lowest-cost option. While researchers have pursued diverse strategies to mitigate this challenge,a definitive solution will not materialize overnight. Consequently, the commercialization strategy for flow batteries must foreground their unique value propositions: the independent scalability of energy and power, inherent suitability for long-duration storage,and theoperational flexibility aforded by electrochemical systems.Among the various RFB chemistries targeting long-duration applications, priority should be accorded to systems with inherently low electrolyte costs,such as those based on polysulfides,as they align more closely with the fundamental economic drivers of LDES. Specifically, such systems allow for capacity expansion at a low marginal cost, without compromising the charge-discharge cycle design or requiring additional expensive power components. Despite the intrinsic cost advantages of polysulfide-based flow batteries, their electrolytes present challenges related to complexity and instability. Current research eforts predominantly concentrate on developing catalysts and membrane materials—components that critically influence power density and Coulombic effciencyHowever,itmustbeemphasizedthatmaterialsofferingonlyshort-termbenefts, requiring prohibitivelyhighcosts,orinvolvingcomplexsynthesis pathways hold limited practical value for commercial polysulfide systems, even if they significantly improve redox kinetics or suppress the polysulfide shutle eect. Electrolyte engineering emerges as a highly promising avenue for future advancement, yet current investigations in this domain remain relatively sparse and require deeper refinement to achieve the long-term stability and practicality demanded for field deployment. Furthermore,it is crucial to recognize that even with substantial material optimization, irreversible capacity fade remains an inevitable challnge when polysulfide-based RFBs are scaled to large systems and operated over extended periods. This issue critically undermines a foundational advantage of RFB technology for LDES: long cycle life witout significant capacity degradation. Unlike all-vanadium RFBs, for which electrolyte rebalancing and capacity regeneration methodologies are well-established, scarcely any reports exist on comparable regeneration techniques for polysulfide-based systems. This gap presents a significant barrier to their commercial viability. Therefore, we propose the folowing recommendations for the development of practical polysulfide-based redox flow batteries:

(1) Align with Market Needs and Leverage Distinctive Advantages: At their current stage of development, polysulfide-based RFBs are not positioned to compete directly with lithium-ion batteries (LIBs） for general energy storage applications. Their commercialization strategy should therefore emphasize their inherent strengths: the independent scalability of energy and power,long projected cycle life,and low raw material costs-atributes that make them uniquely suitable for long-duration energy storage (LDES). Future technology development must consistently highlight and optimize these core value propositions.

(2) Bridge the Gap Between Lab-Scale and Engineering Systems in Catalyst Design: Catalyst development must progress beyond simple activity metrics to address practical engineering constraints:(i) Catalysts must demonstrate high selectivity to suppress parasitic reactions, particularly the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) in alkaline media.(i) The impact of catalyst loading on the porosity and hydraulic permeability of the carbon felt electrode must be critically assessed, especially considering the difering flow-field dynamics between small lab cells and large commercial stacks.(ii) Robust, long-term adhesion between the catalyst and the carbon substrate is essential to prevent detachment and performance decay during extended operation.(iv) Synthesis and loading procedures must prioritize cost-effectiveness and simplicity to ensure scalability for mass production.

(3) Design Membrane Modifications for Scalable Manufacturing: Modification strategies should be compatible with industrial roll-to-roll manufacturing processes to ensure scalability，cost efficiency，and high throughput. Furthermore， the transition from lab-scale membrane fabrication to industrial production requires careful consideration，as changes in formation processes (e.g., casting, curing) can significantly alter critical performance parameters such as thickness, equivalent weight, and mechanical strength.

(4) Prioritize the design of multi-functional additives and electrolyte formulations: Introducing functional additives offers a straightforward approach to electrolyte regulation, often enabling multiple improvements simultaneously. These additives can mitigate polysulfide shuttling by binding to metal-polysulfide species, participating in redox reactions,or facilitatig new reaction pathways. Further research should actively explore the synergistic interactions between additives, catalysts,and membrane materials to develop integrated systems.

(5) Accelerate Development of Practical Capacity-Rebalancing Technologies: The lack of reliable capacity regeneration methods constitutes a critical barrier to the commercialization of polysulfide-based RFBs. To ensure long-term operational stability and prevent system failure due to capacity fade in field deployments,it is imperative to establish cost-effective and practical rebalancing techniques. Any viable solution must be compatible with real-world engineering constraints, requiring minimal additional hardware and avoiding complex operational procedures that would increase levelized cost.

In conclusion,while research on polysulfide-based RFBs continues to progress, the field stands at a critical juncture. The conventional approach—incrementally improving electrodes, catalysts, membranes,and electrolytes in isolation—has yielded valuable insights but has not yet delivered a commercially viable system. This review argues that the path forward requires a fundamental shift in perspective: from material optimization to system failure mitigation, from manufacturability, and from capacity decay as an accepted phenomenon to capacity recoverability as a design imperative. The absence of viable engineering-scale systems-particularly the lack of effective capacity-rebalancing methodsremains a critical barrer， rendering long-term operation without significant capacity decay currently unfeasible. However, this gap also represents an opportunity. Polysulfide chemistry, with its rich interconversion between elemental sulfur and polysulfide species, provides a chemical handle for developing rebalancing strategies-a necessary prerequisite for overcoming the irreversible capacity fade inherent to asymmetric flow battery systems. Harnessing this chemistry through targeted interventions， integrated system design， and manufacturing-aware material development could transform PSRFBs from a promising laboratory concept into a truly differentiated LDES technology—one that delivers not only ultra-low electrolyte cost, but also the long-term operational stability and capacity recoverability that grid-scale energy storage demands.

With focused efforts in these directions, polysulfide-based RFBs hold the potential to mature into a cost-effective and commercially viable LDES technology in the foreseeable future—not by competing with lithium-ion batteries on power density, but by excelling where lithium-ion cannot:

ultra-low-cost, long-duration,recoverable energy storage.
