# 1． Introduction

The increasing integration of variable renewable electricity is driving demand for grid-scale energy storage capable of time-shifting energy over multi-hour to multi-day periods [1].Among electrochemical options,redox flow batteries (RFBs) are attractive for stationary deployment due to their decoupling of power and energy,flexible system sizing,and intrinsic safety benefits associated with their architecture [2, 3].Commercially mature aqueous RFBs (ARFBs),such as vanadium systems,provide robust cyclability and practical kinetics.However, these systems are fundamentally constrained by the narrow electrochemical stability window of water,which typically limits operating voltage and,consequently,practical energy density [4,5].

A brief comparison with representative aqueous high-voltage redox pairs is also instructive.Aqueous redox flow batteries benefit from the intrinsic safety and high ionic conductivity of water,but their voltage remains constrained by the practical water window,so higher-voltage aqueous designs usually rely on careful pH control,solubility engineering,membrane management,and redox pairs that approach,rather than exceed,aqueous stability limits [6]．Representative examples include quinizarin/ferri-ferrocyanide-type systems operated in alkaline media,which illustrate how aqueous organic-inorganic pairing can deliverabout1 V withextendedcycling [7]; pyrrolidinium-functionalized TEMPO paired with extended viologen for high-voltage and long-life aqueous operation [8]； extended bipyr-idinium derivatives engineered for stable two-electron aqueous storage [9]；quaternary-ammonium-functionalized naphthalene diimide/nitroxyl radical systems for durable pH-neutral aqueous flow batteries [l0]；more recent crossover-free aqueous pairs based on N-heterocycle-linked TEMPO and two-electron-capable bipyridinium motifs [1l]；and host- or carrier-mediated aqueous strategies,including $\beta$ -cyclodextrin-assisted systems,that further improve stability within the aqueous operating window [l2].In comparison, the nonaqueous ROMs discussed in this review target substantially larger formal potential gaps, often in the ${ \sim } 2 { \mathrm { - } } 3 \mathrm { V }$ range,but at the cost of lower conductivity,greater transport penalties,greater sensitivity to ion pairing and crossover,and more demanding solvent-safety considerations.Thus,aqueous and nonaqueous systems should be viewed not as direct substitutes,but as distinct design spaces in which voltage,transport,stability,and safety are balanced differently.

Against this background, nonaqueous redox flow batteries (NRFBs) offer a complementary route to higher voltage by utilizing organic solvents that support a broader electrochemical window [13,14]. Solvent platforms such as acetonitrile, propylene carbonate,and ethers provide access to significantly larger potential ranges,enabling the pairing of higher-potential posolytes with lower-potential negolytes beyond what is possible in aqueous media.Concurrently,advances in synthetic chemistry and structure-property analysis have expanded the library of redox-active organic molecules (ROMs),facilitating the tuning of redox potential, engineering of solubility,and stabilization of charged states in organic electrolytes [15].

Recent studies highlight this potential. High-potential posolytes derived from phenothiazine, particularly thosewith electronwithdrawing substituents,have demonstrated reversible oxidation at elevated potentials while maintaining solution-phase stability in acetonitrile-based electrolytes [l6].Additionally,extended quinones [17],perylene diimides [18],and pyridinium-based structures [19] have been developed as nonaqueous negolytes or posolytes,exhibiting reversible redox cyclingand favorable stability windows.Collectively, these findings demonstrate that organic molecular design can increase the voltage and energy-density potential of flow batteries beyond the limitations of aqueous systems.

Despite significant molecular-level advancements,the translation of high-voltage ROMs into durable,high-energy NRFB full cells remains limited.A critical and often overlooked challenge is that voltage enhancement is seldom an isolated benefit [2O].Increasing redox potential often increases the chemical reactivity of charged states,while molecular strategies to stabilize radicals or improve solubility,such as the introduction of bulky substituents or ionic side chains,frequently result in transport penalties,including increased viscosity,reduced diffusivity,and elevated polarization [21-23].Consequently,theoretical gains in energy density at the molecular level are often diminished ornegated under deployment-relevant current densities.

Electrolyte and separator compatibility further restricts the translation from promising half-cell performance to durable full-cell operation [24,25]. Supporting salts and solvents affect not only conductivity but also solvation structure,redox energetics,and degradation chemistry [26].Nonaqueous-stable membranes with low resistance and minimal crossover are scarce,and many systems depend on porous separators that can accelerate capacity fade through species crossover and imbalance [27-29].These challenges intensify at higher concentrations and operating potentials,where parasitic reactionsand crossover-driven inefficiencies are exacerbated.

A second barrier is methodological.The diversity of molecular structures explored for NRFBs has outpaced the emergence of unified, comparable design principles [3O].Redox potentials are often reported against diferent reference conventions; solubility, viscosity,and cycling metrics are measured under disparate conditions;and full-cell demonstrations span a wide range of current densities,electrode configurations， and separator types[31]．These inconsistencies hinder cross-comparison and can obscure which apparent gains reflect intrinsic molecular advantages versus system-specific choices [32].

This review provides a chemistry-centric analysis of high-voltage, organic ROMs for NRFBs,with a focus on species exhibiting redox activity at or above $2 . 0 \ \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ vs.ferrocene/ferrocenium $\mathrm { ( F c / F c ^ { + } ) }$ .We explicitly connect molecular design choices to device-relevant constraints,including transport losses,electrolyte viscosity,and separator compatibility,with the aim of delivering an actionable rather than descriptive review.To this end,we map the redox-potential landscape across key organic scaffold families,such as phenothiazines,quinones and anthraquinones,imides,pyridinium and viologen derivatives,and selected N-heterocycles,and distill molecular design rules linking functional-group tuning,steric protection,and resonance delocalization to the stability of highly charged states.

Beyond molecular energetics， thisreview critically assesses solubility-enhancing strategies,including PEG chains,branched substituents,and ionic side groups,and their associated transport penalties in terms of viscosity,diffusion,conductivity,and crossover.Full-cell demonstrations are evaluated to clarify trade-offs among operating voltage,active-species concentration,current density,efficiency,and lifetime, with particular attention to separator limitations and inventory loss due to crossover or imbalance.This integrated perspective underscores the necessity of co-optimizing redox potential, active-species concentration,and transport properties.The review further emphasizes standardized reporting,molecule-separator co-design,and coupled screening descriptors as key priorities for advancing the discovery and scale-up of high-voltage ROMs.

# 2.Fundamentals of high-voltage operation in NRFBs

Practical energy density scales with the average discharge voltage and the amount of charge stored per unit electrolyte volume.In aqueous redox flow batteries,cell voltage is constrained by water stability. Hydrogen and oxygen evolution bound the thermodynamic window (O and $1 . 2 3 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ vs.SHE) [33].Although kinetic suppression of water electrolysis and pH decoupling strategies can extend the practical operating voltage of aqueous systems beyond the thermodynamic stability window of water,sustained operation at voltages above ${ \sim } 1 . 5 ~ \mathrm { V }$ is typically accompanied by increased parasitic reactions arising from water splitting，ultimately constraining long-term performance [34,35]．This intrinsic limitation is a primary driver for interest in NRFBs,where higher voltages are possible if the redox species and electrolyte remain stable.

NRFBs replace water with organic solvents that exhibit wider nominal electrochemical stability windows,often on the order of $2 { - } 5 . 4 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ ， depending on the solvent,salt,electrode surface,and operating conditions[13].This wider window enables posolyte-negolyte pairings that can exceed $_ { 3 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } }$ in principle,as demonstrated in recent NRFB systems [36].In practice,three coupled constraints set usable voltage: (i) electrochemical reversibility and chemical stability of the redox-active organic molecules, (ii) transport and conductivity at the target concentration,and (ii) separator and electrode compatibility under sustained polarization.

A central principle in nonaqueous electrochemistry is that redox energetics are solvent-and electrolyte-dependent [37,38].While ferrocene and the $\mathrm { F c / F c ^ { + } }$ couple are commonly employed as internal reference standards,measured potentials remain sensitive to electrolyte composition,ion pairing,and specific solvation effects,particularly at high concentrations [38,39].Molecules operating far positive or far negative of $\mathrm { F c / F c ^ { + } }$ must remain reversible while avoiding solvent oxidation at high potentials and solvent or salt reduction at low potentials.Reactive intermediates,catalytic electrode surfaces,impurities, and salt-derived pathways often narrow the effective stability window relative to the nominal solvent window [37].

High-voltage operation introduces additional chemical stress. Electron-withdrawing substitution and extended conjugation can raise voltage,but can also increase susceptibility to radical coupling,polymerization,bond cleavage,and nucleophilic attack [37,4O].Deep reductions can trigger electrolyte decomposition or generate reactive intermediates [37].Because flow systems repeatedly expose large inventories to charged states,even low-probability side reactions can accumulate into significant capacity loss [31].Stabilization must therefore address both electrochemical reversibility and chemical persistence,using resonance delocalization, steric shielding,and substituent choices that preserve solubility and kinetics [41].

Transport and conductivity constraints often determine whether a voltage advantage translates into practical energy efficiency and power density.Nonaqueous electrolytes typically show lower ionic conductivity than aqueous solutions,and viscosity can rise sharply at high active concentration or when bulky solubilizing groups are introduced [42].Aggregation, strong ion pairing,and slow diffusion of large aromatic scaffolds can further increase polarization and reduce energy efficiency under practical current densities [22,37]. Since energy density scales with concentration,limited solubility,aggregation,or precipitation directly reduces usable capacity.Voltage,solubility, viscosity,and diffusion should therefore be optimized together rather than treated as independent targets [22].

Separator and membrane compatibility adds another system-level boundary thatis often decisive.Many ion-exchange membranes developed for aqueous environments swell,lose selectivity,or degrade in organic solvents,which raises resistance and shortens lifetime [43].As a result,many nonaqueous demonstrations employ porous separators or hybrid architectures and accept some crossover while managing imbalance operationally. However, crossover of active species remains a major cause of capacity fade,particularly at high concentration gradients and sustained polarization.Molecular strategies to reduce crossover,such as increasing size or introducing charge,can also raise viscosity or reduce kinetics. Separator selection and molecule design should therefore be treated as a co-design problem rather than independent decisions.Fig.1 contrasts the intrinsic voltage limitation of ARFBs with the expanded nominal voltage window accessible in nonaqueous systems,while highlighting the practical constraints that govern high-voltage operation.Although organic solvents enable higher cell voltages,their realization depends on ROM and electrolyte stability, adequate ionic conductivity and mass transport at practical concentrations,and separators that effectively suppress crossover without incurring excessive resistance.

In summary, NRFBs provide a viable route to exceed the voltage limits of aqueous systems; however, the realizable gains are governed by tightly coupled chemical and engineering constraints.High-voltage operation is sustainable only when redox energetics are matched to electrolyte stability,degradation pathways are effectively suppressed over long-term cycling,transport penalties remain acceptable at practical concentrations,and crossover is mitigated through integrated molecule-separator design.Collectively, these factors indicate that solvent stability,achievable concentration,and separator-limited transport jointly define the true operating window of high-voltage redox-active organic molecules in nonaqueous flow batteries.

# 3. Molecular design strategies for high-voltage and high-nergydensity ROMs

Achieving high cell voltage in NRFBs requires redox-active organic molecules whose potentials fit within the usable stability window of the solvent and supporting salt [26],while remaining soluble and chemically persistent under repeated cycling.The design space is strongly coupled.Pushing potentials to more extreme values often increases the reactivityof charged states,whereas solubility-driven functionalization can introduce viscosity and transport penalties that reduce efficiency at practical current densities [22].This section consolidates the leading high-voltage molecular families and distills design principles for selecting redox cores, tuning potential, enabling multi-electron storage, and maintaining reversibility under high oxidative or reductive stress.

# 3.1．Redox-active cores for high-voltage operation

Recent NRFB research converges on a small set of ROM scaffolds that repeatedly deliver high-voltage pairing while maintaining electrochemical reversibility in nonaqueous electrolytes. These include phenothiazines,quinones and anthraquinones,imides,pyridinium or viologen derivatives,and selected N-heterocycles.Each family offers a distinct balance among redox potential,charged-state stability,and practical solubility.

Phenothiazines are among the most established posolyte cores for high-voltage operation because their one-electron oxidation can be highly reversible,and their scaffold tolerates extensive substitution.In manyacetonitrile-based electrolytes,substituted phenothiazines exhibit oxidation couples in the approximate range of $+ 0 . 6$ to $+ 1 . 1$ V vs.Fc/ $\mathrm { F c } ^ { + } [ 4 4 - 4 7 ]$ .Recent studies show that N-substitution and 3,7-position functionalization can simultaneously improve solubility and suppress parasitic pathways such as radical coupling,while maintaining high potential [44,48].

Quinones and anthraquinones remain the dominant low-potential negolyte family in nonaqueous systems.While simple benzoquinones often suffer from limited stability in aprotic media,extended quinones such as anthraquinones and naphthoquinones generally exhibit improved chemical robustness and enable two-electron charge storage through carbonyl redox chemistry.In nonaqueous electrolytes,their reduction potentials typically fall in the range of approximately $- 0 . 9$ and $- 0 . 3 { \mathrm { ~ V } }$ vs. $\mathrm { F c / F c ^ { + } }$ ,depending on molecular structure and substitution,enabling high cell voltages when paired with high-potential posolytes [49-53].A central design challenge remains stabilizing the reduced intermediates while preserving high solubility and manageable viscosity.

Imide-based scaffolds,including perylene diimides (PDI) and naphthalene diimides (NDI),provide well-defined and often highly reversible reductions.Their electron-deficient cores support low redox potentials with strong chemical robustness,and side-chain engineering can reduce aggregation and increase practical concentration.Imide-based negolytes typically exhibit reduction potentials in the range of approximately $- 0 . 6$ to $- 1 . 8 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ Vs. $\mathrm { F c / F c ^ { + } }$ ,depending on the molecular core structure and substitution pattern [54-57].Recent reports demonstrate imide-based negolytes with excellent cycling stability in nonaqueous media,with improved resistance to radical aggregation and parasitic side reactions [55,58].

Pyridinium and viologen derivatives remain important negolytes because their reduction potentials can be systematically tuned through molecular substitution and counterion selection，and their redox chemistry is well established. These compounds typically undergo reversible reductions with first reduction potentials in the approximate range of $^ { - 0 . 6 }$ to $- 1 . 1 \mathrm { V }$ Vs. $\mathrm { F c / F c ^ { + } }$ ,with second reductions extending to approximately $^ { - 1 . 1 }$ to $- 1 . 6 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ depending on substitution pattern and electrolyte environment [59-62].However,dimerization or disproportionation of radical intermediates can drive capacity loss during prolonged cycling.Recent studies demonstrate that steric protection and electronic tuning strategies can suppress radical cation dimerization and improve long-term electrochemical reversibility [19,63].

OtherN-heterocycles,includingphenazines, triazines,and substituted carbazoles,are increasingly explored as alternative redox cores.Their extended conjugation and resonance stabilization can support relatively high oxidation potentials and stabilize radical intermediates [64]. Reported redox potentials vary depending on molecular design，with phenazine derivatives typically exhibiting reversible reduction potentials in the approximate range of $- 0 . 8$ to $- 1 . 7$ Vvs. $\mathrm { F c } / \mathrm { F c } ^ { + } [ 6 5 , 6 6 ]$ ，whilesubstitutedcarbazoleand triazine-containing systems generally display oxidation couples in the approximate range of $+ 0 . 5$ to $+ 0 . 9 \ : \mathrm { V }$ VS. $\mathrm { F c / F c ^ { + } }$ [67,68]. While full-cell validation remains less common than for phenothiazines or quinones, several studies indicate strong potential for further development.

Molecular design efforts in NRFBs have largely centered on increasing redox potential to maximize theoretical cell voltage [69]. Scaffold selection and functional-group tuning have successfully produced organic ROMs operating at potentials well beyond aqueous limits. However,redox potential alone is a poor predictor of deployable performance.In practice,ROMs exhibiting high redox potentials often operate near the chemical stability boundaries of the electrolyte,electrodes,or supporting salt.At these extremes,small increases in potential can disproportionately increase parasitic reactions,radical reactivity,or solvent oxidation, even when the nominal electrochemical window appears suficient.As a result,molecules that appear highly reversible in cyclic voltammetry may degrade or induce electrolyte decomposition undersustainedpolarizationinflowoperation.Moreover, voltage-centric molecular screening neglects the fact that practical energy density depends on average discharge voltage under load, not on equilibrium redox potential. Transport losses arising from viscosity,ion pairing,and limited diffusivity can substantially reduce voltage efficiency，eroding the apparent advantage of high-potential ROMs at deployment-relevant current densities.Fig.2 summarizes the approximate redox-potential landscape of these scaffold families vs. $\mathrm { F c / F c ^ { + } }$ ， emphasizing how appropriate posolyte-negolyte pairing enables theoretical cell voltages above $_ { 2 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } }$ in NRFB configurations.

# 3.2.Functional group tuning of redox potential

Beyond selecting the core scaffold, substitution is the primary lever for tuning redox potential while managing stability and solubility [70]. For oxidation processes,electron-withdrawing groups (EWGs) typically lower the highest occupied molecular orbital energy,making oxidation more diffcult and shifting oxidation potentials in the positive direction. Electron-donating groups (EDGs) generally have the opposite effect and shift oxidation potentials negatively [71].For reduction processes, EWGs often stabilize the reduced state and shift reduction potentials in the positive direction (less negative),whereas EDGs can shift reduction potentials more negative.In practice,these trends can be amplified or attenuated by solvent polarity,ion pairing,and specific electrolyte interactions,so potential tuning should be evaluated under the intended electrolyte conditions rather than inferred from gas-phase arguments [72].

In phenothiazines,electron-withdrawing and sterically protective substitution at electronically coupled positions can shift oxidation potentials positively by hundreds of millivolts,enabling higher-voltage posolytes [73].This benefit must be balanced against increased oxidative stress and higher reactivity of radical cations at elevated potentials, particularly in acetonitrile-based electrolytes [74].In quinones,substitution alters both redox potential and the stability of reduced intermediates,so electronic tuning must be balanced against chemical persistence of the reduced states under cycling [75].

Side-chain engineering often dominates practical concentration and transport.Ether-rich substituents,including oligoethylene glycol chains, can improve solubility and enhance stabilization of charged states through stronger solvation,but they can impose viscosity penalties that increase polarization at high concentration [76].This strategy has been applied across phenothiazines and related ROM families to raise achievable concentration without immediate loss of reversibility [77]. Ionic functionalization (for example,quaternization) can improve solubility and reduce crossover via charge exclusion,but it can also increase ion pairing or reduce diffusion depending on salt and solvent choice [78].As a result, substitution should be evaluated not only by redox potential and solubility,but also by concentration-dependent viscosity,conductivity,diffusion,and crossover propensity.

Substituent placement is as important as substituent identity.Modifications at positions that participate directly in redox delocalization can disrupt conjugation and increase reorganization energy,which often reduces reversibility and increases polarization.In contrast,successful designs frequently locate solubilizing and steric groups at electronically less active positions to preserve the redox-active pi system while controlling intermolecular interactions [79].

# 3.3.Multi-electron transfer design for energy density

Multi-electron ROMs should be viewed primarily as an energydensity strategy rather than a voltage-tuning strategy,because their main benefit is to increase the amount of charge stored per molecule and,when solubility remains adequate,per unit electrolyte volume. However, the practical value of multi-electron chemistry depends not only on electron count,but also on whether each accessible redox state is sufficiently stable and kinetically reversible under realistic operating conditions.In nonaqueous electrolytes,the broader electrochemical window can support multiple redox steps,but multi-electron operation also introduces additional constraints: intermediate charged states must avoid disproportionation,radical coupling,and solvent or salt reactions, and the spacing between successive redox couples must remain compatible with efficient full-cell operation [80].

In most reported NRFB systems,multi-electron behavior proceeds through sequential electron transfer,in which two or more one-electron events occur at separated potentials.This pathway is often chemically accessible because intermediate radical ions can be stabilized by resonance delocalization,steric protection,ion pairing,or solvent coordination [8O].At the same time,large potential separation between successive redox steps can broaden the charge-discharge profile,reduce the average discharge voltage,and lower voltage efficiency,especially when one step becomes kinetically sluggish or transport-limited under flow conditions.By contrast,concerted multi-electron transfer would be more attractive from an efficiency standpoint because it could preserve a higher average working voltage and reduce polarization associated with uneven accessibility of intermediate states.In practice,however, concerted transfer is difficult to realize because it requires the system to avoid unstable intermediates,structural rearrangement,and parasitic reactions across a wider redox window [80].

Quinones and anthraquinones remain the most mature platform for multi-electron storage in NRFBs.Their stepwise reductions often enable two-electron storage with distinct redox couples,and careful substitution can stabilize reduced states while maintaining practical solubility. Recent work has further expanded redox accessibility through substituent-assisted redox activity,enabling multiple redox states and high theoretical capacity,although these systems still require careful electrolyte selection to suppress side reactions and preserve reversibility across the full state-of-charge range [81].

Multi-redox behavior can also be engineered by incorporating multiple redox centers into a single molecule,including radical-bearing motifs such as nitroxides or conjugated frameworks that distribute charge across several sites [82].These designs can offer fast kinetics and high theoretical capacity,but they may also increase molecular size, viscosity,ion pairing,or aggregation,thereby reducing the effective gain at deployment-relevant concentration [83].Accordingly,multi-electron ROM design should not be judged solely by the number of accessible redox states.It should instead be evaluated by the combined ability to deliver high electron count,limited potential splitting，chemically persistent intermediates,acceptable transport properties,and similar reversibility across the full redox sequence.

A related integration strategy is bipolar or symmetric ROMdesign, in which a single molecule can serve as both negolyte and posolyte by operating at two separated redox potentials.Symmetric designs can mitigate crossover-driven capacity fade because crossover does not permanently remove active material from the electrochemical system. However,bipolar operation still requires that both the oxidized and reduced states remain chemically stable and that the electrolyte remains compatible across a broader accessible potential range [84]. Thus,bipolar design can reduce the operational penalty of crossover,but it does not remove the fundamental requirement for multistate stability.

# 3.4.Strategies for reversible high-potential redox processes

Reversible cycling at high potential requires suppression of chemical pathways that compete with the desired redox reaction.For highpotential posolytes,common degradation mechanisms include radical coupling, nucleophilic attack at activated positions,and bond cleavage driven by highly oxidizing charged states.For low-potential negolytes, reductive electrolyte decomposition,radical dimerization,and reactions with trace impurities can dominate.Because flow batteries operate through repeated,prolonged exposure of large electrolyte inventories to charged states,reversibility should be evaluated under conditions that approximate practical concentrations, polarization, and exposure times, rather than only under dilute or short-duration static testing [85,86].

Two molecular design strategies recur across successful high-voltage ROM development. First,resonance delocalization reduces the local reactivity of radical intermediates by distributingcharge and spin density overa larger $\pi$ -conjugated framework.Second,steric shielding of chemically vulnerable positions suppresses bimolecular decay pathways such as radical coupling,which is particularly important for oxidized phenothiazine-and carbazole-derived species [87].These strategies are often complementary.Delocalization can reduce intrinsic chemical reactivity,whereas steric protection can block specific follow-up reactions that remain accessible even in electronically stabilized radicals. At the same time,both approaches may increase molecular size or rigidity and thereby reduce solubility,reinforcing the need to co-optimize stability with side-chain design and solvent compatibility [87].

For high-potential posolytes,the most effective designs typically stabilize the radical cation without overexposing strongly electrophilic sites.This balance is often achieved through substituents that delocalize the oxidized-state charge,reduce spin localization,and sterically shield positions prone to coupling or nucleophilic attack.In practice,raising potential without preserving radical-cation persistence rarely yields durable cycling.Accordingly,the most successful high-potential scaffolds combine electronic tuning with structural protection rather than relying on voltage enhancement alone [87].

For low-potential negolytes，the challenge is different.Deeply reduced states must remain reversible while avoiding association, disproportionation,and parasitic reactions with solvent, supporting salt, or trace impurities.Here,reversibility depends not only on the intrinsic structure of the ROM,but also on the extent of ion pairing and the local solvation environment.Molecular designs that suppress aggregation or distribute negative charge more effectively can improve cycling stability,yet these gains remain strongly electrolyte-dependent [88].

Electrolyte co-design is therefore equally decisive for reversible highpotential operation.Weakly coordinating anions such as TFSI and $\mathrm { P F } _ { 6 } ^ { - }$ can reduce strong ion pairing and improve kinetics,but they can also introduce distinct interphase and stability behaviors that depend on the solvent,concentration,and electrode surface [88,89].Solvent choice shapes not only the nominal electrochemical window,but also solvation structure,intermediate lifetimes,and the likelihood of parasitic pathways under sustained polarization.As a result,the stability of a high-voltage ROM should be interpreted as a property of the ROM-electrolyte pair, not of the molecule alone.

Taken together, these examples show that reversible high-potential cycling is not achieved through a single molecular lever. Instead, durable operation emerges from coordinated control of redox energetics, charged-state persistence,steric accessibility,ion-pairing behavior,and solvation environment. # 4. Molecular modifications to improve solubility and stability

Nonaqueous electrolytes provide broader electrochemical windows and enable higher operating voltages than aqueous systems,but they also impose stricter requirements on solubility,transport,and long-term chemical stability.In practice,the realizable energy density of an NRFB is often limited less by redox potential than by the maximum deployable concentration that can be sustained without excessive viscosity,aggregation,precipitation,or accelerated degradation.Molecular modifications that improve solubility while preserving reversibility are therefore as important as potential tuning itself.However,solubility should not be interpreted in isolation. A ROM that is highly soluble under equilibrium conditions may still perform poorly in a working cell if the same structural changes that improve dissolution also raise viscosity,reduce diffusivity,strengthen ion pairing,or increase crossover propensity. Accordingly,the most useful molecular strategies are those that improve concentration and cycling stability together,while retaining acceptable transport properties under deployment-relevant conditions [42].

# 4.1．Solubility-enhancing molecular modifications

Low solubility remains a persistent bottleneck for high-voltage ROMs in organic solvents.Many aromatic cores,including phenothiazines, quinones,and imides,exhibit strong $\pi$ -stacking and favorable crystal packing that limit dissolution in common polar aprotic solvents such as acetonitrile.For unsubstituted or weakly substituted scaffolds,concentrations below $0 . 1 \textbf { M }$ are not uncommon.Solubility enhancement is therefore typically pursued through substituents that disrupt packing, increase solvent affinity,or weaken ion association while minimizing perturbation of the redox-active core.

One of the most widely used approaches is oligoethylene glycol (PEG) substitution.PEG chains increase molecular polarity and conformational flexibility,which improves compatibility with polar aprotic solvents and can stabilize both neutral and charged states.In line with this design principle,PEG-functionalized phenothiazines show markedlyimprovedsolubilityandelectrochemical reversibilityin nonaqueous electrolytes compared with unsubstituted analogues [91, 95]．A similar strategy has also been applied successfully to dimethoxybenzene-based posolytes.In DBMMB and DBBB,the combination of ether-rich side chains and bulky tert-butyl groups disrupts crystallization,weakens intermolecular association,and improves solvent affinity,thereby enabling higher concentration and more stable cycling [96,97].

PEG-type substitution has also proven effective across several other ROM families,including TEMPO derivatives and viologens,where concentrations approaching or exceeding $0 . 5 \mathrm { ~ M ~ }$ have been reported in solvents such as acetonitrile and dimethyl sulfoxide [98,99].These gains are valuable because they can also reduce intermolecular aggregation and,in some cases,weaken ion-pairing effects at moderate concentration [1OO].Yet the benefit is not universal.Extended ether chains can increase molecular size and raise electrolyte viscosity,which lowers diffusion coeficients and increases polarization at practical current density.PEGylation should therefore be regarded as a co-optimization strategy rather thana purely beneficial solubilization handle.

A second approach is branched alkyl substitution, which improves solubility primarily by disrupting solid-state packing rather than by increasing polarity.Branched groups such as tert-butyl weaken intermolecular $\pi$ -stacking and reduce crystallinity in planar aromatic scaffolds,thereby improving dissolution while often preserving the intrinsic redox properties of the core [1Ol,lO2].A representative example is 2-tert-butylanthraquinone,in which steric disruption of anthraquinone packing enhances solubility in binary nonaqueous solvents.Related design logic has also been used in TEMPO derivativesand pyrimidine-based negolytes,where steric and hydrogen-bonding modifications reduce aggregation,improve cycling stability,or suppress crossover [1O3-1O5].At the same time,branched alkyl functionalization is highly solvent-dependent.Excessive hydrophobic substitution can reduce favorable solute-solvent interactionsin acetonitrileor carbonate-based media,which limits the net solubility gain or even introduces phase-behavior complications in mixed-solvent systems [102]. Accordingly, branched alkyl groups are best viewed as a complementary tool whose value depends strongly on the surrounding electrolyte environment.

# 4.2.Stability considerations at high redox potentials

High-voltage operation imposes substantial chemical stress on ROMs because charged intermediates formed during oxidation or reduction are often more reactive than their neutral precursors.Stability is therefore governed by several interacting factors,including charge and spin delocalization,accessbility of reactive sites,and the susceptibility of the charged state to parasitic reactions with solvent or supporting electrolyte.Improving stability is rarely achieved througha single molecular lever.Instead,successful designs usually combine electronic stabilization with steric protection while preserving practical solubility.

Resonance delocalization remains one of the most important stabilizing mechanisms.Extended conjugation distributes unpaired electron density over a larger framework, thereby reducing localized reactivity and lowering the likelihood of bond cleavage or radical coupling.Phenothiazines,phenazines,and perylene diimides benefit from this effect and often show comparatively stable radical cation or radical anion states in nonaqueous media [44,55,112]. Yet extended conjugation can also strengthen intermolecular interactions and promote aggregation, which is why solubilizing and steric substituents are frequently required in parallel.

Energetic alignment relative to the electrolyte is equally important. ROMs designed for high oxidation potential must avoid driving solvent oxidation or salt-derived decomposition even when the nominal solvent window appears wide.Functional-group tuning that shifts oxidation positively can therefore increase voltage whilesimultaneously increasing oxidative stress on the radical cation.This trade-off becomes particularly important for posolytes operating above approximately 0.8 V vs. $\mathrm { F c / F c ^ { + } }$ ，where modest additional increases in potential can disproportionately increase parasitic chemistry unless stabilization and electrolyte compatibility are strengthened [89].

Steric shielding complements resonance stabilization by blocking reactive positions and suppressing bimolecular decay pathways. In phenothiazines,substitution patterns that protect the 3 and 7 positions can suppress radical coupling and extend cycle life during repeated oxidation [113]. Similar principles apply to viologen radicals and other aromatic radicals for which dimerization or coupling represents a major failure route.The most durable high-voltage ROMs,therefore, combine electronic tuning with structural protection rather than relying on voltage enhancement alone.

# 4.3.Mitigating dimerization,crossover, and side reactions

Beyond intrinsic instability of the redox-active core,several practically important failure modes arise from intermolecular processes and cell-level transport. These include radical dimerization at high concentration,crossover through separators or membranes,and chemical side reactions promoted by extreme redox states.

Radical dimerization is a common failure pathway for several negolyte classes,especially viologens and certain quinone-derived radicals.At high concentration,radical species can dimerize or form higher aggregates,thereby reducing the electrochemically active fraction and accelerating apparent capacity loss.Steric hindrance and electronic asymmetry introduced through substitution can reduce orbital overlap and suppress dimer formation, improving long-term reversibility [114]. Increasing solvation strength of the radical state can also reduce the driving force for association,although this often comes with a viscosity penalty.

Crossover remains a major contributor to capacity fade even when the ROM itself is chemically stable [115].Increasing molecular size, introducing charge,or engineering specific interactions can reduce diffusion through porous separators and,in favorable cases, improve membrane selectivity.However, these same changes can reduce diffusivity,raise viscosity,or intensify ion pairing depending on the electrolyte.An alternative strategy is bipolar or symmetric ROM design, in which the same molecule serves as both posolyte and negolyte at different redox states.Because crossover does not permanently remove active material from the electrochemical inventory,symmetric architectures can reduce crossover-driven fade without relying entirely on membrane selectivity [1l6].Their value,however,still depends on multistate chemical stability and acceptable transport.

Side reactions with solvent or supporting salt become increasingly important near the extremes of the electrolyte window.Reduced quinones can show chemical reactivity in certain carbonate or ether electrolytes,while highly oxidizing posolytes can catalyze solvent oxidation on carbon electrodes despite apparently favorable nominal windows. These observations reinforce that durability is an emergent property of the ROM-electrolyte-electrode system rather than of the molecule alone, and they further justify co-optimization across structure,solvent,salt, and separator [50,117].

# 4.4.Electrolyte additives and solvation effects

Electrolyte formulation provides additional levers for improving ROM stability and practical performance.Weakly coordinating anions such as TFSI' can reduce specific ion pairing in some nonaqueous electrolytes and improve the reversibility of charged states,particularlyat high ROM concentration [118].Salt identity can also affect solubility directly through changes in ionic strength,specific interactions,and solvent structure.For example, $\mathrm { L i P F } _ { 6 }$ in carbonate electrolytes has been reported to support high-solubility anthraquinone-based electrolytes in NRFB operation [119].These effects are system-specific,however,and salt selection should therefore be evaluated together with stability, conductivity,and side-reaction risk at the target operating voltage.

Solvation effects are central to both redox energetics and chemical persistence.Highly solvating solvents such as dimethyl sulfoxide can stabilize charged intermediates and improve reversibility,but theyalso tend to increase viscosity and can limit power density at high concentration [l2O].Mixed-solvent systems have therefore been explored to balance solvation strength with transport properties and to mitigate viscosity-driven polarization [l21].In addition, trace water or deliberately introduced protic species can alterredox pathways,especially for quinone-based ROMs where proton-coupled electron transfer and cation association can improve reversibility in some regimes [122]. Controlled proton management in aprotic NRFB electrolytes remains comparatively underexplored for high-voltage operation,but it is likely to become increasingly relevant as multi-electron quinone chemistries are pushed toward higher concentration and longer cycling.

Overall, the most effective improvements in Section 4 do not arise from solubility enhancement, steric protection,or electrolyte tuning in isolation.They arise when these levers are coordinated so that concentration，reversibility，transport，and side-reaction resistanceare improved together.Table 2, therefore,serves not simplyas a catalog of soluble molecules,but as a reminder that practical concentration must be judged from cycling-tested operation, not from equilibrium solubility alone.

# 5. Full-cell applications and integration challenges

Molecular design has produced redox-active organic molecules with redox potentials compatible with high-voltage nonaqueous operation, but full-cell demonstrations remain the decisive filter for practical viability. Compared with half-cell tests,full cells introduce coupled constraints from inventory management,asymmetric transport losses, separator resistance and crossover,electrode wetting and polarization, and durability under continuous circulation.These factors can dominate performance even when both half-reactions appear reversible in isolation.

# 5.1．Coupling high-voltage posolytes and negolytes

The most direct route to high-voltage NRFBs is to pair a highpotential posolyte with a low-potential negolyte within a compatible solvent-salt framework. Common combinations include phenothiazineor carbazole-derived posolytes with quinone-，viologen-,or imidederived negolytes.In principle,the formal redox-potential differences between these posolyte-negolyte pairs,with individual redox potentials commonly reported versus $\mathrm { F c / F c ^ { + } }$ ,cansupport theoretical cell voltages of roughly $2 . 0 { - } 3 . 0 \ \mathrm { V }$ under suitable nonaqueous electrolyte conditions [127].In practice,however， successful pairing requires more than maximizing the potential difference.It requires simultaneous alignment of (i) the chemical stability windows of both charged states,(ii) solubility limits at the operating temperature,(ii) concentration-dependent viscosity,conductivity,and diffusion,and (iv) electrolyte compatibility with the selected separator and electrodes [l28].Recent studies further show that mismatch in any one of these domains can drive rapid capacity imbalance,elevated polarization,oraccelerated fade even when the individual redox couples appear promising in isolation [129].

At the full-cell level, the upper usable voltage is often dictated by posolyte stability and electrolyte oxidation on carbon electrodes, whereas the accessible energy density is frequently capped by negolyte solubility and transport at deployment-relevant concentration.In addition,differences in diffusion coefficient, viscosity,and ionic conductivity between the posolyte and negolyte electrolytes can produce asymmetric mass-transfer losses,concentration overpotentials,and state-of-charge gradients that intensify side reactions during cycling. Pairing should therefore be treated as an electrolyte-system matching problem,not merely as a molecule-selection exercise.Practical workflows increasingly combine redox characterization with solubility mapping,concentration-dependent transport measurements,and short flow-cell screening under realistic polarization before extended cycling is attempted.

Symmetric or bipolar ROM designs provide an alternative integration route.In these systems,a single molecule supports both oxidation and reduction at separated potentials,enabling symmetric electrolytes and reducing the permanent impact of crossover because migrated species remain electrochemically addressable.Symmetric configurations can also simplify balancing and mitigate cross-contamination risks. However,bipolar ROMs typically sacrifice some voltage or capacity to gain these integration benefits,and they impose stricter requirements on multi-state stability across a wider accessible potential range [130]. Their value is therefore strongest in architectures where separator selectivity is limited or where long-duration operation makes crossover-driven imbalance a dominant failure mode.

# 5.2. Trade-offs between voltage,concentration,and current density

Although nonaqueous solvents unlock significantly higher nominal voltages than their aqueous counterparts,practical performance is dictated by a complex interplay between cell voltage,active species concentration,and achievable current density. Maximizing voltage is a primary objective because higher cell potential increases electrolytelevel energy density and can reduce system cost per kilowatt-hour by lowering the electrolyte volume and balance-of-plant requirements fora given energy target [14].This is typically pursued through molecular modifications,such as bulky substituents,ionic functionalization,or resonance-stabilized frameworks,designed to enhance redox potentials and charged-state stability.While effective at the molecular level, these modifications frequently entail hidden costs,including increased electrolyte viscosity and diminished mass-transfer performance,which collectively amplify polarization and constrain operation at higher current densities [128].

Active concentration presents a similar paradox.While high concentrations are indispensable for achieving competitive energy densities,they exacerbate viscosity-related transport losses and intensify ion pairing and aggregation, which can depress diffusivity and conductivity at deployment-relevant compositions. Consistent with this,ROMcontaining nonaqueous electrolytes have been shown to exhibit maxima in conductivity and diffusivity around $0 . 5 { \mathrm { ~ M ~ } }$ ,followed by significant decreases at higher concentrations,contributing to transportlimited utilization under load [111].

Consequently,voltage maximization should not be pursued in isolation.Practical full-cell engineering requires a multi-objective optimization approach that balances discharge voltage,capacity utilization at target current densities,and round-trip energy efficiency.The most significant advances in the field are therefore not defined by peak voltage alone,but by the ability to expand the overall performance envelope across these competing metrics.Fig.4 provides a holistic synthesis of this landscape by mapping representative high-voltage NRFB full-cell demonstrations against the practical benchmarks required for grid-scale relevance.The figure highlights the persistent gap between high cell voltage,deployment-relevant concentration,and long cycle life,and thus emphasizes that progress must be evaluated through co-optimization rather than voltage alone.

# 5.3.Recent high-voltage NRFB demonstrations

Since 2O21,several NRFB full-cell studies have reported sustained operation at cell voltages above $2 \ \mathrm { \nabla V _ { : } }$ ，providing important proof-ofconcept validation for high-voltage organic redox pairs under circulating conditions.Across these reports,however,the recurring lesson is not simply that high voltage is achievable.It is that full-cell durability is most often limited by separator-mediated crossover,inventory imbalance,and transport-driven polarization,rather than by immediate intrinsic instability of the redox-active core alone [131].

Phenothiazine-anthraquinone pairings remain among the most widely cited high-voltage demonstrations.Reported full cells operate at approximately $2 . 2 { - } 2 . 5 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ and can sustain Coulombic efficiencies above $9 5 \ \%$ over ${ \tt > } 1 0 0$ cycles in selected configurations. In these systems, capacity decay is frequently attributed primarily to crossover and electrolyte imbalance,while post-cycling analyses often indicate that direct molecular decomposition is comparatively secondary under the tested conditions [42]. This distinction is important because it separates molecular stability from inventory stability.A chemically robust ROM can still exhibit apparent fade when separator selectivity is insufficient or when the two electrolyte sides age asymmetrically.

Imide-based negolytes paired with high-potential posolytes have shown improved chemical robustness and longer cycle life,including reports exceeding 3oo cycles.However,many of these demonstrations operate at comparatively modest active-species concentrations,often below $\mathbf { 0 . 3 \ M }$ ，This choice helps suppress viscosity growth and polarization, but it also limits delivered energy density.These systems therefore provide strong evidence of intrinsic redox stability,yet they do not fully resolve the concentration and transport requirements needed for deployment-relevant performance [55].The broader implication is that stability gains obtained through rigid,resonance-stabilized scaffolds must be complemented by solubility and transport engineering before they can translate into practically competitive full cells.

Viologen-based negolytes continue to demonstrate highly reversible low-potential redox chemistry,but their integration into high-voltage full cells remains sensitive to radical association,including dimerization or higher-order aggregation,as well as ion pairing and separator compatibility.Recent studies show that steric design and electrolyte selection can mitigate these effects,yet the practical window for simultaneously achieving high concentration,low polarization,and low crossover remains narrow in many viologen-centered systems [31].As a result, viologen platforms often highlight the broader need for co-design of molecule,salt, solvent,and separator,rather than molecule optimization alone.

Taken together,these demonstrations show that the present bottleneck is not the absence of high-voltage chemistry,but the difficulty of sustaining that chemistry under conditions that also deliver practical concentration,acceptable voltage efficiency,and stable electrolyte inventory.Most reported high-voltage NRFB full cells remain laboratoryscale,commonly using electrolyte volumes below $5 0 ~ \mathrm { m L }$ and current densities below those required for commercial relevance.Reported energy efficiencies typically fall in the $7 0 \mathrm { - } 8 5 ~ \%$ range,with voltage efficiency often representing the dominant source of loss because of limited nonaqueous conductivity,viscosity-driven transport losses,and separator resistance. # Benchmarking checklist for full-cell NRFB reports

To enable meaningful cross-study comparison, full-cell NRFB reports should provide the following items in one clear and easily accessible location,either in the main text or in the Supporting Information:

i.Electrolyte formulation: solvent identity,supporting salt identity and concentration,and active-species concentrations on both the posolyte and negolyte sides.
ii.Reference convention: redox potentials reported versus a clearly stated reference,together with the conversion method if values are translated from anotherreference scale.
iii.Cell configuration: flow or static mode,electrode area and electrode type,separator or membrane identity and thickness,and operating temperature.
iv. Operating conditions: current density, flow rate where relevant, cut-off voltages, electrolyte volumes,and any preconditioning or polarity-reversal procedures.
v.Performance definitions: explicit formulas for Coulombic efficiency,voltage eficiency,and energy efficiency,together with polarization breakdown where available.

vi.Energy-density basis: clear statement of whether the reported value is based on total electrolyte volume,active material only,or a broader system-level basis,including all assumptions used in the calculation.

vii.Degradation attribution: evidence that distinguishes crossover- or imbalance-driven loss from chemical decomposition,such as rebalancing tests，crossover measurements,post-cycling spectroscopy,or compositional analysis.

# 5.4.Solvent-system and safety trade-offs in high-voltage nonaqueous RFBs

Many representative high-voltage NRFB demonstrationsuse acetonitrile-based electrolytes,often with supporting salts such as $\mathrm { T B A P F } _ { 6 }$ or TFSI-based salts,because acetonitrile combines low viscosity, high ionic conductivity,and a broad practical electrochemical window that is favorable for proof-of-concept full-cell studies [l3].As reflected by several entries in Table 3,the frequent use of acetonitrile $/ \mathrm { T B A P F } _ { 6 }$ therefore indicates a practical electrochemical advantage rather than a fully resolved deployment choice.

However,this advantage is coupled to important safety and durability trade-offs.Many organic solvents used in NRFBs introduce hazards related to volatility,flammability,and handling sensitivity,and these issues become more important as electrolyte inventory and operating voltage increase [l3]. Solvent choice can also directly influence the chemical persistence of charged states.A clear example is the PDI-TEMPO symmetric flow battery,in which capacity fade was faster in acetonitrile than in DMF;the study attributed this difference to side reactions involving acetonitrile and decomposition centered mainly on the reduced PDI moiety [133].This result shows that a solvent favored for its conductivity and low viscosity can still impose hidden penalties in long-term chemical stability.

Alternative solvents and solvent platforms, including propylene carbonate,ethers,DMF,DMsO,ionic-liquid-based media,and eutectic formulations,can offer advantages in thermal behavior,volatility,solvation strength,or electrochemical stability,but they usually introduce their own constraints [121,135,136].More strongly solvating media can stabilize charged intermediates and improve some aspects of reversibility,yet they often increase viscosity and reduce power capability. Solvent composition can also alter ion pairing,membrane crossover behavior，and overall cell efficiency，asshown in studiesof mixed-solvent nonaqueous flow batteries and deep-eutecticor ionic-liquid-based systems [137].These trade-offs indicate that solvent selection should not be judged only by nominal electrochemical window orhalf-cell reversibility.It should also be evaluated through a broader deployment lens that includes viscosity,conductivity,volatility, flammability,separator compatibility,crossover behavior,and long-term chemical persistence.

# 5.5.Open challenges for scale-up and deployment

Several barriers still prevent high-voltage NRFBs from progressing from laboratory demonstrations to deployment-relevant energy-storage systems.These bottlenecks are not independent. They are tightly coupled,and improvements in one domain often expose hidden constraints in another.Therefore,scale-up should be framed asa systemintegration problem rather than a molecule-only optimization exercise.

Crossover of active species through separators remains the most persistent failure driver.Even when ROMs exhibit high intrinsic chemical stability,crossover causes inventory imbalance,self-discharge,and apparent capacity decay,particularly under large concentration gradients and extended operation [14O].The challenge is amplified by the limited availability of membranes purpose-built for nonaqueous electrolytes.Many aqueous ion-exchange membranes swell or lose selectivity in organic solvents,while porous separators reduce resistance but permit rapid crossover [141].As a result,present-day NRFB designs often operate within a narrow compromise space where acceptable ohmic loss is achieved only at the expense of accelerated imbalance and shortened effective lifetime.

ROM cost and manufacturing scalability represent a second,equally decisive constraint. Many high-performing high-voltage molecules rely on multistep syntheses, specialty precursors,and extensive purification, which can dominate cost at scale and complicate supply-chain robustness.In addition,molecular tunability,while a core advantage of organic systems,introduces compositional diversity that complicates cost modeling，qualification protocols,and long-term procurement. These realities underscore the importance of developing structure,cost, and performance relationships,where voltage and stability gains are evaluated alongside synthetic yield,step count,solvent and reagent burden,and purification intensity [142].

Component compatibility and long-term materials stability remain under-characterized in many NRFB studies.Carbon electrodes,current collectors,seals,and tubing that are benign in aqueous media can exhibit altered wetting,swelling,or chemical reactivity in nonaqueous electrolytes.Moreover，parasitic reactions at high potential are frequently catalyzed by electrode surfaces or trace impurities and can evolve over time as interphases form.Durable operation therefore requires assessing ROM,solvent,and salt compatibility together with electrode polarization behavior,interfacial stability,and corrosion or compatibility of metallic components under realistic circulation, temperature,and impurity control [143].

Finally,scaling exposes transport and thermal-management constraints that are muted in small cells.At higher concentration and higher current density,viscosity-driven pressure drops,mass-transfer limitations,and heat generation can reduce voltage efficiency and accelerate degradation by increasing time spent at extreme potentials.Achieving commercially relevant round-trip efficiency and lifetime will therefore require coordinated advances in electrolyte transport engineering,flowfield and electrode architecture,and separator design,rather than incremental improvements to any single component [144].

Overall,recent full-cell demonstrations confirm the feasibility of high-voltage NRFB operation,but they also reveal a constrained operating window in which voltage,concentration,efficiency,and durability can be optimized simultaneously.The next phase of progress will depend on translating molecular advances into manufacturable, separator-compatible,and transport-eficient electrolyte systems that sustain stable inventory and high energy efficiency under deploymentrelevant conditions.

Key takeaways (Section 5)

i.Full-cell durability is most often limited by crossover,separator resistance,and transport-driven polarization,not only intrinsic ROM stability.
ii.Voltage,concentration，and current density must be cooptimized; gains in one variable frequently create losses in efficiency or lifetime.
iii.Symmetric or bipolar architectures can reduce crossover-driven fade when membrane selectivity is limited,but require multistate stability.
iv.Benchmarking should separate inventory loss from chemical decomposition using crossover measurements,rebalancing,and post-cycling analysis.

# 6. Conclusion and future perspectives

High-voltage NRFBs offer a practical route to surpass the voltage and energy-density ceiling imposed by aqueous chemistries.Continued progress depends on disciplined co-optimization across molecular design and full-cell engineering.Across recent literature,voltage is rarely the limiting variable.Practical performance is set by the coupled balance among redox potential, stable concentration at operating temperature,charged-state persistence,concentration-dependent transport losses,and suppression of crossover-driven inventory imbalance.

Several transferable design principles can be distilled.First,the redox-active core defines the feasible potential window,but the substituent strategy determines whether that window is usable at deployment-relevant concentrations. Phenothiazinesand related electron-rich heterocycles remain strong posolyte candidates because resonance delocalization and steric protection can stabilize oxidized states at relatively high potential. On the negolyte side,quinones and anthraquinones,imide derivatives,and pyridinium or viologen platforms provide robust foundations.Their practical value depends on maintaining solubility of reduced states while suppressing aggregation, radical coupling,and other association pathways.

Second, solubility engineering is a primary lever for energy density, but it must be treated as a transport problem as well as a chemistry problem.PEG-like chains,branched alkyl groups,and ionic side chains can increase concentration by disrupting packing and improving solvation.These same modifications can also raise viscosity,lower diffusivity，and reduce voltage eficiency，especially at higher current density.Third,long-life operation at high-voltage is fundamentally a radical-management challenge.ROMs that distribute charge and spin density over extended conjugation,shield reactive positions,and avoid labile bond motifs show the most consistent reversibility over repeated cycling.Fourth,many reported capacity losses reflect crossover and electrolyte imbalance rather than intrinsic ROM decomposition.This shifts attention toward separator design,electrolyte rebalancing strategies,and the use of symmetric or bipolar architectures when highselectivity membranes are unavailable.

Looking forward,the most competitive NRFB systems will likely emerge from redox-active molecules that simultaneously combine high redox potential,high stable concentration,and intrinsically low crossoverpropensity.Promising research directions include:

i. multi-electron or multi-redox ROMs that increase energy density while retaining clean electrochemical kinetics and avoiding excessive voltage inefficiency;
ii.donor-acceptor molecular architectures that achieve high cell voltagewithina single molecular framework;
iii.oligomeric or tethered molecular motifs that suppress crossover by increasing molecular size while preserving solubility and reversible transport behavior;and
iv.hybrid and electrolyte-engineering strategies,including organic posolytes paired with metallic or intercalation anodes when safety,dendrite suppression,and interfacial stability can be ensured,as well as mixed-solvent systems,advanced supporting salts,and machine-learning-assisted molecular design workflows that accelerate the identification of high-voltage,transport-efficient,and chemically robust ROMs.

Despite these promising opportunities,a predictable and recurring risk remains.Molecules that demonstrate excellent reversibility and stability in cyclic voltammetry may underperform under realistic flow battery conditions if they introduce excessive viscosity,increase polarization losses,or exhibit significant crossover through practical separators.

Machine-learning-assisted molecular design and broader modelassisted screening can accelerate progress when integrated into a deployment-oriented screening loop [145]. Quantum-chemical calculations and data-driven models can guide redox-potential targeting and identify stability-relevant descriptors and likely decomposition routes [146].Molecular dynamics and continuum transport models can then translate solvation structure into viscosity,diffusivity,and conductivity trends that govern power and efficiency [111,147].The highest value will come from integrating these layers into a single workflow that prioritizes not only high potential,but also concentration-dependent transport and chemical resilience under realistic polarization and impurity constraints.

To accelerate discovery and scaling,three recommendations are particularly actionable.First, reporting should be standardized to enable fair benchmarking,including consistent potential references,explicit ROM concentration and viscosity,current density and polarization breakdown,and a clear accounting of whether capacity loss is dominated by crossover,imbalance,or chemical decomposition. Second, molecules and separators should be treated as a co-design pair, because crossover frequently dominates apparent durability and because molecular size,charge,and polarity directly define the separator selectivity required for stable inventory.Third,synthetic scalability should be prioritized early rather than treated as a downstream concern.Highperforming molecules that rely on complex routes,low yields,or expensive reagents are unlikely to meet grid-scale cost targets,so modular synthesis and robust purification should be evaluated as core performance metrics alongside electrochemical properties.

Overall,high-voltage NRFBs have advanced from proof-of-concept chemistry to increasingly credible full-cell demonstrations.The next phase should focus on narrowing the gap between molecular excellence and system practicality.Stable high-voltage operation,high concentration,and low crossover must be achieved simultaneously to establish a compelling path to deployment.
