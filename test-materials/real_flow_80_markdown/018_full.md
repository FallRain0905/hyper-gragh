# 1．Introduction

The global energy landscape is undergoing a fundamental transformation as nations intensify efforts to decarbonize electricity generation and mitigate climate change impacts. Renewable energy sources, particularly wind and solar photovoltaics,have experienced unprecedented growth over the past two decades,driven by technological advancements,declining costs,and supportive policy frameworks.In the United States, wind and solar power collectively supplied approximately $1 4 \%$ of total electricity generation in 2023, with projections indicating continued expansion in the coming years.Similarly,the European Union has set ambitious targets to achieve at least $4 2 . 5 \%$ renewable energy share by 2030,while China has emerged as the world's largest producer of renewable electricity，with installed solar and wind capacity exceeding 1200 GW by the end of 2023.Despite this remarkable progress the inherent variabilityand intermittency of renewable energy sources present significant challenges to grid stability and reliability. Solar irradiance fluctuates with weather conditions and ceases entirely during nighttime hours,while wind power generation varies unpredictably with atmospheric conditions.This temporal mismatch between renewable energy supply and electricity demand creates operational challenges for grid operators,who must maintain continuous balance between generation and consumption to ensure system stability [1]. The integration of large-scale energy storage systems has emerged as a critical enabler for addressing these challenges by decoupling energy generation from consumption, storing excess renewable energy during periods of high production,and dispatching it during peak demand or low generation periods [2]. Among various energy storage technologies, electrochemical battery systems have atracted considerable attention due to their scalability,rapid response times,and location flexibility. Lithium-ion batteries currently dominate the commercial energy storage market, benefiting from decades of development for portable electronics and electric vehicle applications.However， their deployment for grid-scale applications faces several constraints,including high capital costs, limited cycle life, thermal runaway risks, and concerns regarding the sustainability and geopolitical concentration of critical raw materials such as lithium, cobalt,and nickel. These limitations have motivated extensive research into alternative battery chemistries that offer improved safety profiles,longer operational lifetimes,and reduced reliance on scarce materials [3].Redox flow batteries (RFBs) represent a particularly promising class of electrochemical energy storage systems that address many limitations of conventional batteries.Unlike conventional batteries where energy storage and power generation occur within the same electrode materials,RFBs store energy in liquid electrolytes containing dissolved electroactive species housed in external tanks,while power generation occurs in an electrochemical cell stack where oxidation and reduction reactions take place.This fundamental architectural difference enables independent scaling of energy capacity (determined by electrolyte volume and concentration） and power output (determined by electrode area and stack size), providing exceptional design flexibility for diverse applications.Furthermore,RFBs operate at ambient temperature, exhibit minimal capacity degradation over thousands of charge-discharge cycles,present negligible fire hazards due to aqueous electrolytes,and facilitate straightforward capacity restoration through electrolyte replacement rather than entire system disposal [4] (see Tables 5 and 6).

Traditional vanadium redox flow batteries (VRFBs) have achieved commercial maturity and demonstrate excellent electrochemical reversibility，withseveral megawatt-scale installationsoperating worldwide. However, the high cost and limited availability of vanadium, combined with moderate energy density, have stimulated research into alternative redox-active materials.Aqueous organic redox flow batteries (AORFBs) have emerged as an particularly atractive alternative,utilizing organic molecules as electroactive species instead of metal ions. The appeal of AORFBs stems from several advantageous characteristics: organic electroactive materials can be synthesized from earth-abundant elements such as carbon, nitrogen,oxygen,and hydrogen; their molecular structures can be systematically modified through synthetic chemistry to tune electrochemical properties including redox potential, solubility [5],and stability; they generally exhibit lower toxicity and environmental impact compared to metal-based systems; and they potentially ofer significantly reduced material costs through scalable synthesis from biomass-derived precursors or industrial feedstocks. Among various organic molecule families investigated for AORFB applications,dihydroxyphenazine (DHP) derivatives have demonstrated particularly promising characteristics, including appropriate redox potentials,reasonable solubility in alkaline media,and acceptable electrochemical stability during cycling [6]. The development and optimization of AORFBs requires systematic understanding of how molecular-level properties of organic electrolytes translate to cel-level performance under diverse operating conditions and system configurations.This relationship is mediated by complex,coupled transport phenomena including fluid dynamics in flow channels and porous electrodes，species transport via convection and diffusion，charge transport in both solid and liquid phases,and electrochemical kinetics at electrode-electrolyte interfaces. Accurately predicting cell performance necessitates solving the governing conservation equations for mass, momentum, charge, and chemical species, typically formulated as systemsof coupledpartial differential equationsdefinedover three-dimensional (3D) computational domains representing the actual cell geometry [7].High-fidelity numerical simulation approaches, particularly the finite element method (FEM) and finite volume method (FVM), have been extensively employed to model RFB systems and have demonstrated excellent agreement with experimental measurements across various cell designs，from laboratory-scale single cells to multi-kilowatt stacks. These detailed simulations provide comprehensive spatial and temporal resolution of concentration fields,potential distributions,current density profiles,and velocity patterns,yielding invaluable insights into performance-limiting phenomena such as concentrationpolarization， ohmiclosses， andflowdistribution non-uniformities [8].

Despite their accuracy and comprehensiveness,3D multiphysics simulations impose substantial computational demands that severely limit their applicability in scenarios requiring rapid or repeated performance evaluations.Solving coupled nonlinear partial differential equations over computational meshes containing millions of elements typically requires hours to days of computation on high-performance computing clusters,with individual simulations consuming hundreds to thousands of CPU-hours. This computational burden becomes prohibitive for applications such as autonomous materials discovery frameworks that must screen thousands of candidate molecules,technoeconomic optimization studies that evaluate numerous system configurations,parametric sensitivity analyses exploring multi-dimensional design spaces[9],and digital twin implementations requiring real-time performance predictions for monitoring and control purposes. Consequently,there exists a critical need for reduced-order modeling approaches that substantially accelerate computational performance while maintaining acceptable predictive accuracy for practical engineering applications. Zero-dimensional (OD) lumped-parameter models represent the simplest approach to RFB performance prediction, treating the entire electrochemical cell as a single homogeneous unit with uniform properties and neglecting all spatial variations.These models reduce the governing equations to systems of ordinary differential equations oralgebraic equations that can be solved analytically or with minimal computational effort, enabling near-instantaneous predictions. However,the assumption of perfect mixing and uniform concentration throughout the cell becomes increasingly inaccurate as cell dimensions increase,particularly in large-format cells where concentration gradients and non-uniform current distributions significantly impact performance.This limitation motivates the development of models that incorporate essential spatial variations while maintaining computational efficiency substantially superior to full 3D simulations. Physics-based analytical models achieve this balance by introducing strategic simplifications to the governing equations that enable closed-form or semi-analytical solutions while preserving the fundamental physics of transport and reaction processes.Simultaneously, the remarkable recent advances in machine learning methodologies, particularly deep neural networks,have opened new possibilities for data-driven performance prediction that can capture complex nonlinear input-output relationships through training on comprehensive datasets [10].

The present work addresses the critical need for computationally efficient yet accurate RFB performance prediction tools by developing and systematically comparing two distinct modeling approaches:a physics-based analytical framework designated EZBattery and a machine learning operator model based on Deep Operator Network (DeepONet）architecture.These complementary methodologies offer different advantages and trade-offs regarding computational efficiency, physical interpretability,data requirements,and generalizability across different cell designs and chemistries.Rigorous validation and comparative evaluation are conducted using a comprehensive dataset generated from high-fidelity FEM simulations of a large-scale $7 8 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ interdigitated AORFB employing DHP-based organic anolytes,encompassing 1280 distinct material property combinations evaluated across 10 diferent operating conditions for a total of 12,8oo simulation cases. Through systematic analysis of prediction accuracy，computational performance,sensitivity to material properties,and applicability ranges, this study elucidates the respective strengths and limitations of physicsbased and data-driven modeling paradigms for flow battery applications,providing practical guidance for selecting appropriate tools for diverse research and engineering contexts ranging from fundamental materials discovery to commercial system deployment.

# 2.3.EZBattery:physics-based analytical model

The EZBattery modeling framework addresses the computational limitations of full 3D finite element simulations by introducing strategic simplifications that enable analytical or semi-analytical solutions while preserving essential transport and reaction physics. The key innovation is representing the complex porous electrode microstructure as an array of idealized micro-channels with equivalent transport characteristics,as illustrated in Fig. 2(a).

# 2.3.1． Micro-channel approximation

The porous carbon felt electrode is conceptualized as a network of parallel micro-channels oriented along the primary flow direction, with characteristic dimension $^ { b }$ determined by the average pore size.The porosity $\varepsilon$ and specific surface area $a _ { s }$ relate to the micro-channel geometry through:

$$
b = \frac { 4 \varepsilon } { a _ { s } ( 1 - \varepsilon ) }
$$

This representation transforms the complex three-dimensional porous medium into a geometrically simplified system amenable to analytical treatment.Each micro-channel is assumed to have either circular $( d = 1 )$ )orsquare $( d = 0$ )cross-section,where $d$ is a shape factor

appearing in subsequent equations.

# 2.3.2.Species transport in micro-channels

Transport of reduced species within a micro-channel is governed by the convection-diffusion equation in cylindrical coordinates:

$$
\frac { \partial C _ { \mathrm { r e d } } } { \partial t } + u ( r ) \frac { \partial C _ { \mathrm { r e d } } } { \partial z } = D _ { \mathrm { r e d } } \left[ \frac { \partial ^ { 2 } C _ { \mathrm { r e d } } } { \partial z ^ { 2 } } + \frac { d } { r } \frac { \partial C _ { \mathrm { r e d } } } { \partial r } + \frac { \partial ^ { 2 } C _ { \mathrm { r e d } } } { \partial r ^ { 2 } } \right]
$$

where $\mathfrak { z }$ is the axial coordinate along the flow path (O to $H$ ,the electrode height), $r$ is the radial coordinate (O to $b / 2$ ）， $u ( r )$ is the velocity profile, and $D _ { \mathrm { r e d } }$ is the effective diffusion coefficient of reduced species.For laminar flow in circular channels, the velocity profile is parabolic:

$$
u ( r ) = 2 u _ { 0 } \left[ 1 - \left( { \frac { 2 r } { b } } \right) ^ { 2 } \right]
$$

where $u _ { 0 }$ is the average velocity.For square channels,a similar profile applies with appropriate geometric factors.

The concentration field is expanded in a Taylor series about the channel centerline $( r = 0$ )：

$$

# 2.4. DeepONet: machine learning operator model

Deep Operator Networks (DeepONets) represent a class of neural network architectures designed to learn mappings between function spaces,making them particularly suitable for predicting continuous outputs (such as voltage profiles) given diverse input conditions.The DeepONet framework consists of two sub-networks that work in concert, as illustrated in Fig. 2(b).

# 2.4.1．Network architecture

The branch network encodes system-specific parameters including the ten material properties from Table 1 plus the two operating conditions (flow rate and current density),forming a 12-dimensional input vector:

$$
\mathbf { x } _ { \mathrm { b r a n c h } } = \left[ \alpha , a _ { s } , \kappa _ { m } , k _ { 0 } , \beta _ { m } , C _ { 0 } , D , \mu , E ^ { 0 } , \kappa _ { l } , Q , i _ { \mathrm { a p p } } \right]
$$

An additional binary flag $s \in \{ - 1 , + 1 \}$ distinguishes charging $( s = +$ 1) from discharging $( s = - 1 )$ phases,as these exhibit different voltage characteristics at identical SOC values due to hysteresis effects.The complete branch input is therefore 13-dimensional.

The trunk network processes the independent variable, in this case the state of charge:

$$
\mathbf { x } _ { \mathrm { t r u n k } } = [ \mathbf { S } \mathbf { O C } ]
$$

Both networks employ fully connected feedforward architectures with four hidden layers,each containing 2oo neurons.Hyperbolic tangent activation functions are applied at each layer:

$$
\mathbf { h } ^ { ( l + 1 ) } = \operatorname { t a n h } \left( W ^ { ( l ) } h ^ { ( l ) } + b ^ { ( l ) } \right)
$$

where $\mathbf { h } ^ { ( l ) }$ is the hidden state at layer l $\mathbf { W } ^ { ( l ) }$ are weight matrices, and $\mathbf { b } ^ { ( l ) }$ are bias vectors.

# 2.4.2. Output prediction

The outputs of both networks are combined through a dot product operation to produce the voltage prediction:

$$
V ( \mathbf { x } _ { \mathrm { b r a n c h } } , \mathbf { S O C } ) = \sum _ { k = 1 } ^ { n _ { \mathrm { b a s i s } } } \phi _ { k } ( \mathbf { x } _ { \mathrm { b r a n c h } } ) \psi _ { k } ( \mathbf { S O C } )
$$

where $\phi _ { k }$ are the $n _ { \mathrm { b a s i s } } = 2 0 0$ outputs from the branch network and $\psi _ { k }$ are the corresponding outputs from the trunk network.This formulation allows the network to learn a set of basis functions $\psi _ { k } ( S { \mathrm { O C } } )$ that are linearly combined with weights $\phi _ { k } ( { \bf x } _ { \mathrm { b r a n c h } } )$ specific to each material and operating condition combination.

# 2.4.3.Enhanced architecture with encoders

A modified DeepONet architecture was implemented incorporating encoder layers in both branch and trunk networks.These encoders perform nonlinear transformations at each hidden layer before combining information:

$$
\mathbf { h } _ { \mathrm { c o m b i n e d } } ^ { ( l ) } = \lambda ^ { ( l ) } \mathbf { e } _ { \mathrm { b r a n c h } } ^ { ( l ) } \left( \mathbf { h } _ { \mathrm { b r a n c h } } ^ { ( l ) } \right) + \left( 1 - \lambda ^ { ( l ) } \right) \mathbf { e } _ { \mathrm { t r u n k } } ^ { ( l ) } \left( \mathbf { h } _ { \mathrm { t r u n k } } ^ { ( l ) } \right)
$$

where $\mathbf { e } _ { \mathrm { b r a n c h } } ^ { ( l ) }$ and ${ \mathbf e } _ { \mathrm { t r u n k } } ^ { ( l ) }$ are encoder functionsat lyer $l ,$ and $\lambda ^ { ( l ) } \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ are learnable convex combination coeficients.This enhancement improves the network's expressiveness and reduces approximation errors, particularly for complex nonlinear relationships.

# 2.4.4. Training procedure

The network parameters (weights and biases） are optimized by minimizing the mean squared error between predictions and COMSOL simulation results:

$$
\mathcal { L } = \frac { 1 } { N _ { \mathrm { b a t c h } } N _ { \mathrm { S O C } } } \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { N _ { \mathrm { b a t c h } } } \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { N _ { \mathrm { S O C } } } \left[ V _ { \mathrm { p r e d } } \left( \mathbf { x } _ { i } , \mathbf { S } \mathbf { O C } _ { j } \right) - V _ { \mathrm { F E M } } \left( \mathbf { x } _ { i } , \mathbf { S } \mathbf { O C } _ { j } \right) \right] ^ { 2 }
$$

where $N _ { \mathrm { b a t c h } } = 3 0 0$ is the batch size (number of material/operating condition combinations per training step), $N _ { \mathrm { S O C } } = 3 8$ is the number of SOC points per case (19 charging $^ { + 1 9 }$ discharging), $V _ { \mathrm { p r e d } }$ are DeepONet predictions,and $V _ { \mathrm { F E M } }$ are COMSOL reference values.

Optimization employed the Adam algorithm with an exponential learning rate schedule:

$$
\eta ( n ) = \eta _ { 0 } \cdot \gamma ^ { \left\lfloor { \frac { n } { n _ { \mathrm { d e c a y } } } } \right\rfloor }
$$

where $\eta _ { 0 } = 1 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ is the initial learning rate, $\gamma = 0 . 9 9$ is the decay factor, $n$ is the iteration number, and $n _ { \mathrm { d e c a y } } = 2 0 0 0$ is the decay interval. Training proceeded for 6oo,ooo iterations,requiring approximately 8- $^ { 1 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ on an NVIDIA A10O GPU.

# 2.4.6.Implementation details

The DeepONet model was implemented using the JAX framework, which provides automatic differentiation and efficient GPU acceleration.Input features were standardized to zero mean and unit variance based on training set statistics:

$$

$$

where $\mu _ { i }$ and $\sigma _ { i }$ are the mean and standard deviation of feature $_ i$ in the training set. # 2.5.Model evaluation metrics

To comprehensively assess and compare the predictive performance of EZBattery and DeepONet against COMSOL FEM simulations,four quantitative error metrics were defined.

# 2.5.1.Root mean square error (RMSE)

The RMSE quantifies the overall magnitude of voltage prediction deviations across all SOC points during both charging and discharging:

$$
{ \mathrm { R M S E } } = { \sqrt { \frac { 1 } { N } \left[ \sum _ { \mathrm { s o c } } ( V _ { \mathrm { s o c } } ^ { c ^ { \prime } } - { V _ { \mathrm { s o c } } ^ { c } } ) ^ { 2 } + \sum _ { \mathrm { s o c } } ( V _ { \mathrm { s o c } } ^ { d c ^ { \prime } } - V _ { \mathrm { s o c } } ^ { d c } ) ^ { 2 } \right] } }
$$

where $V _ { \mathrm { S O C } } ^ { c ^ { \prime } }$ and $V _ { \mathrm { S O C } } ^ { d c ^ { \prime } }$ are the predicted charging and dischargig voltages from EZBattery or DeepONet, $V _ { \mathrm { S O C } } ^ { c }$ and $V _ { \mathrm { S O C } } ^ { d c }$ are the corresponding COMSOL FEM reference values,and $N$ is the total number of SOC points evaluated (typically 38,but may be less if certain SOC values are not reached due to limiting conditions).Lower RMSE indicates better overall accuracy.

# 2.5.2. OCV error $( e _ { E } \circ \mathrm { c v } )$

This metric captures systematic bias in voltage predictions by averaging signed errors across all SOC points:

$$
e _ { E } \mathrm { o c v } = { \frac { 1 } { N } } \left[ \sum _ { \mathrm { S O C } } \bigl ( V _ { \mathrm { S O C } } ^ { c ^ { \prime } } - V _ { \mathrm { S O C } } ^ { c } \bigr ) + \sum _ { \mathrm { S O C } } \bigl ( V _ { \mathrm { S O C } } ^ { d c ^ { \prime } } - V _ { \mathrm { S O C } } ^ { d c } \bigr ) \right]
$$

Since charging and discharging voltages are symmetric about the equilibrium potential, errors in open-circuit voltage (OcV) prediction manifest as consistent positive or negative bias in both charging and discharging.Thus,large $| e _ { E ^ { \mathrm { { o c v } } } } |$ indicates primarily OCV estimation errors.Values near zero suggest accurate OCV prediction even if other error sources are present.

# 2.5.3. Internal resistance error $( e _ { I R } )$

This metric isolates errors related to internal resistance (ohmic and overpotential losses) by computing the difference between charging and discharging errors:

$$
e _ { I R } = \frac { 1 } { N _ { c } } \sum _ { \mathrm { S O C } } \bigl ( V _ { \mathrm { S O C } } ^ { c ^ { ^ { \prime } } } - V _ { \mathrm { S O C } } ^ { c } \bigr ) - \frac { 1 } { N _ { d c } } \sum _ { \mathrm { S O C } } \bigl ( V _ { \mathrm { S O C } } ^ { d c ^ { ^ { \prime } } } - V _ { \mathrm { S O C } } ^ { d c } \bigr )
$$

where $N _ { c }$ and $N _ { d c }$ are the numbers of SOC points during charging and discharging,respectively. Since OCV contributions cancel in this difference, $e _ { I R }$ primarily reflects inaccuracies in predicting voltage losses due to kinetic overpotentials and ohmic resistances.Large $\displaystyle \left. e _ { I R } \right.$ indicates difficulties in capturing resistance-related phenomena.

# 2.5.4.Initial voltage error $( \Delta V _ { \mathrm { i n i t i a l } } )$

This metric quantifies the deviation in predicted voltage at the start of charging $( \mathsf { S O C } = 0 . 0 2 5$ )：

$$
\Delta V _ { \mathrm { i n i t i a l } } = V _ { \mathrm { S O C = 0 . 0 2 5 } } ^ { c ^ { \prime } } - V _ { \mathrm { S O C = 0 . 0 2 5 } } ^ { c }
$$

This provides insight into model accuracy at the most reduced state, where concentration gradients and kinetic limitations may be most pronounced.

These complementary metrics enable identification of specific error sources and provide a comprehensive comparison of model performance.RMSE gives overall accuracy, $e _ { E } \mathrm { o c v }$ diagnoses equilibrium potential errors, $e _ { I R }$ reveals resistance prediction issues,and $\Delta V _ { \mathrm { i n i t i a l } }$ checks performance at extreme conditions.

# 3.10.Comparative discussion with literature

The results of this study can be contextualized within the broader landscape of flow battery modeling research.Traditional 3D multiphysics models,exemplified by the COMsOL framework employed here, represent the state-of-the-art for accuracy and have been extensively validated for vanadium systems,zinc-bromine chemistries,and organic systems including quinone-based and viologen-based AORFBs[27].reported similar computational demands ( $( 1 - 3 \mathrm { h }$ per case on 64 cores) for detailed 3D simulations of vanadium flow batteries,confirming that our COMSOL performance is representative of the field.Their study achieved excellent agreement with experimental polarization curves (RMSE ${ < } 0 . 0 1 \ \mathrm { V } _ { \cdot }$ acrossa wide current density range,establishing 3D FEMas a reliablereferencestandardwhenproperlyconfigured[28]. Zero-dimensionallumped-parametermodels, widelyusedfor system-level techno-economic analyses，typically achieve $1 0 ^ { 3 } – 1 0 ^ { 4 }$ speedup relative to 3D models but at substantial accuracy cost [29]. developed a OD model for vanadium systems that predicted voltage within $\pm 0 . 0 5  – 0 . 1 0 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ depending on operating conditions-comparable to the best EZBattery performance but significantly worse than Deep-ONet. The limitation is that OD models assume uniform concentration, neglecting spatial gradients that become critical in large-format cells. Our $7 8 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ cell exhibits non-negligible concentration variations $( 5 - 1 5 \%$ across electrode area at high current density according to COMSOL spatial maps), explaining why OD approaches would be inadequate. One-dimensional models offer an intermediate approach [30]. developed 1Dmodelsforvanadiumsystemsincorporating through-plane concentration gradients,achieving errors of $0 . 0 2 \substack { - 0 . 0 4 \mathrm { V } }$ with ${ \sim } 1 0 ^ { 4 }$ speedup.However, these models neglect in-plane variations and require numerical solution of partial differential equations, limiting theirutityforrapidscreening.EZBatery'sinnovationisachieving comparable or better accuracy (0.O15 VRMSE) with analytical solutions and greater speedup $( 1 0 ^ { 6 } )$ ,while still incorporating essential 1D concentration gradients through the micro-channel framework.Machine learning approaches for flow battery modeling are emerging rapidly. He et al.(2022) applied random forest regression to predict vanadium system performance based on 5Oo experimental datasets,achieving $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ $\approx 0 . 9 5$ -good but below the O.998 achieved by DeepONet in this work. Their model required extensive feature engineering and provided no extrapolation guarantees.In contrast, DeepONet's operator learning framework naturally handles functional inputs (SOC) and has theoretical foundations guaranteeing convergence with sufficient data.More closely related, Fortin et al. (2023) employed physics-informed neural networks (PINNs） to solve governing equations for flow batteries, embedding conservation laws as loss function penalties.They achieved impressive accuracy $( \mathrm { R M S E } \approx 0 . 0 0 8 \ : \mathrm { V } )$ without training data by solving equations directly. However,PINNs require minutes of computation per case due to iterative optimization at inference time, negating the speed advantage of neural networks. DeepONet's pure data-driven approach (no physics constraints during inference) enables faster predictions at the cost of requiring training data [31].The hybrid “digital twin” concept is increasingly discussed for battery management systems [32]. implemented a digital twin for lithium-ion batteries using electrochemical models with real-time parameteradaptation， achieving state-of-charge estimation within $\pm 2 \%$ .Extending such concepts to flow batteries requires even faster models due to larger system size and more complex coupled physics.Our results suggest that DeepONet's sub-10-ms inference time makes real-time digital twins feasible,while EZBattery provides a physics-based validation tool requiring minimal computational resources.For materials discovery, high-throughput virtual screening has been successfully applied to organic molecules for batteries [33]. screened 14oo quinone derivatives using density functional theory combined with continuum solvation models,identifying candidates with desirable redox potentials and solubilities.However, they did not evaluate cell-level performance,which depends critically on transport properties and operating conditions. Our framework dem-onstrates that combining molecular property predictions (from quantum chemistry）with rapid cell-level performance models (EZBattery or DeepONet) enables end-to-end screening from molecular structure to system performance.Comparative accuracy assessment reveals that EZBattery's $0 . 0 1 5 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ RMSE and DeepONet's $0 . 0 1 0 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ RMSE are at the state-of-the-art for reduced-order models.For perspective, experimental voltage measurements typically have $\pm 0 . 0 0 5 { \cdot } 0 . 0 1 0 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ uncertainty, suggesting that both models approach the fundamental limit of meaningful accuracy given experimental validation constraints. Further accuracy improvements may not be practically significant unless experimental precision also improves.

# 3.11.Limitations and applicability ranges

Several limitations warrant discussion.First,both reduced-order models were developed and validated using data from a single cell geometry (interdigitated, $7 8 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 } )$ ．Extrapolation to fundamentally differentarchitectures(serpentinechannels,flow-through configurations） would require validation or retraining.EZBattery's physics-based structure makes it more adaptable—changing geometry requires updating geometric parameters in the analytical equation-s—while DeepONet would require generating new training data for the new geometry. Second, the operating condition range explored (1600- $2 4 0 0 \ \mathrm { A / m ^ { 2 } }$ ， $0 . 4 { - } 2 . 0 \ \mathrm { L / m i n } )$ represents typical stationary storage operation but excludes extreme regimes.At very high current densities $\mathrm { ( > 5 0 0 0 ~ A / m ^ { 2 } }$ ）approaching mass-transfer limits，the micro-channel approximation underlying EZBattery may break down as concentration boundary layers become comparable to pore dimensions. Similarly, at very low flow rates $( < 0 . 2 \mathrm { L } / \mathrm { m i n } )$ where residence time exceeds cycle time,batch-mode dynamics not captured in the steady-state formulation may become important. DeepONet's purely empirical approach makes it inherently limited to interpolation within the training envelope. Third, both models assume isothermal operation at $2 9 8 \ \mathrm { K }$ ，Temperature variations affect viscosity,diffusivity，conductivity,and reaction kinetics through Arrhenius relationships. Incorporating temperature as an additional input variable would require expanding the dataset (or for EZBatery,including temperature-dependent property correlations), but the frameworks are conceptually capable of such extensions. Fourth, the models neglect capacity fade mechanisms including crossover through the membrane,side reactions,and electrolyte decomposition.For shortterm performance prediction $_ { \cdot < 1 0 0 }$ cycles), these effects are negligible, but long-term degradation prediction would require incorporating aging models. EZBatery could integrate capacity fade by time-evolving material properties (conductivity decline,active species loss)，while DeepONet would need training data spanning multiple cycle numbers. Finally,the focus on DHP-based anolytes means that validation for other organic chemistries (quinones, viologens,TEMPO derivatives) or inor-ganic systems (vanadium, zinc-bromine) remains future work. However, the EZBattery framework has been successfully applied to vanadium systems in prior publications,and the DeepONet architecture is chemistry-agnostic,requiring only retraining on appropriate datasets. Despite these limitations,the models demonstrate robust performance across the substantial property and operating condition space explored. The 1280-case Latin hypercube sampling ensures statistically repre-sentative coverage,and the systematic validation strategy (including held-out unseen conditions)confirms generalization capability.For the intended applications-rapid screening of novel organic anolytes, parametric optimization of cell designs,and enabling autonomous materials discovery frameworks—the demonstrated accuracy and computational efficiency represent transformative capabilities compared to previous approaches.

# 4.Conclusions

This study presents a comprehensive evaluation of two computationally efficient modeling approaches green finance-driven energy system transformation for predicting the performance of large-scale aqueous organic redox flow batteries based on dihydroxyphenazine electrolytes.The physics-based analytical model EZBattery and the machine learning-based Deep Operator Network (DeepONet) were rigorously validated against high-fidelity three-dimensional finite element simulations across 2630 test cases spanning 1280 distinct material property combinations and 1O operating conditions in a $7 8 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ interdigitated flow cell.Both models demonstrate exceptional predictive accuracy with root mean square errors of $0 . 0 1 5 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ for EZBattery and $0 . 0 1 0 \mathrm { V }$ for DeepONet when trained on adequate data, corresponding to coefficients of determination of 0.998 and representing relative errors below $1 . 5 \%$ across the voltage operating range.Most significantly, both approaches achieve computational speedups exceeding six orders of magnitude compared to conventional finite element simulations,with EZBattery requiring less than $_ { 0 . 1 \textit { s } }$ per prediction and DeepONet completing evaluations in under O.O1 s on standard computing hardware,compared to 55-75 min on 128 parallel CPU cores for the reference COMsOL simulations.This dramatic reduction in computational cost transforms the feasibility of applications requiring millions of performance evaluations,including autonomous materials discovery workflows,multi-objective design optimization,Monte Carlo uncertainty quantification,and real-time digital twin implementations for grid-scale energy storage systems.The detailed error analysis reveals complementary strengths and limitations of each approach. EZBattery excels in predicting internal resistance effects and overpotential losses with physically interpretable results stemming directly from analytical solutions to transport and kinetic equations,requires no training data, and demonstrates excellent adaptability to different cell geometries and electrolyte chemistries through straightforward parameter modifications.However,it exhibits larger systematic errors in equilibrium potential predictions,with correlation analysis identifying initial concentration and standard potential as the primary sources of inaccuracy due to simplifications inherent in the micro-channel representation of porous electrodes. In contrast, DeepONet achieves superior accuracy in capturing equilibrium thermodynamics and demonstrates remarkably consistent performance on both interpolation within trained operating conditions and extrapolation to completely unseen conditions,validating its capability to learn underlying physical relationships rather than merely memorizing training examples. The neural network approach shows that $3 0 \%$ of available training data,approximately 2200 simulation cases，provides suficient coverage for robust predictions with diminishing returns from additional data.However, DeepONet exhibits larger errors in predicting resistance-related phenomena and functions as a black box with limited physical interpretability,making diagnosis of prediction failures more challenging. The ranked importance analysis quantitatively confirms that initial concentration,membrane conductivity,and electrolyte conductivity are the three most influential properties affecting prediction accuracy for both models,while kinetic parameters show minimal influence,indicating that the cell operates ina transport-limited rather than kinetics-limited regime under the conditions explored. Together, these complementary modeling tools provide a practical pathway for accelerating redox flow battery research and deployment.EZBattery offersa flexible,physicsgrounded approach ideal for exploratory studies across diverse chemistries and geometries where training data may be unavailable or expensive to generate,while DeepONet provides unparalleled computational speed and accuracy for high-throughput applications within well-characterized design spaces where initial investment in training data generation is justified by subsequent millions of rapid queries.The integration of both approaches with high-throughput computational chemistry for molecular property prediction and techno-economic analysis frameworks enables comprehensive end-to-end screening from molecular design through cell-level performance to system-level cost optimization，ultimately advancing the development of costeffective,safe,and scalable energy storage technologies essential for supporting the global transition to renewable electricity generation and achieving carbon-neutral energy infrastructure.
