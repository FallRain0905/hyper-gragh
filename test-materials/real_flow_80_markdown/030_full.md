# ABSTRACT

Redox flow batery (RFB) technologies open a new era for large-scale energy storage systems,with the development of a new generation of polyoxometalate clusters-based redox flow battries (POM-RFBs) enabling the high eergy density RFBs to be possible.Looking back at the decade-long journey of POM-RFB research, it is evident that significant progres has been made inenhancing the RFB performance,yet several challenges still require resolution.Herein,firstly,the critical merits of POM clusters including their multi-electron redox properties,strong stability,and high solubility,are emphaticaly summarized as they significantly contribute to the superior performance metrics of POM-RFBs.Furthermore,applications of POM as electrolyte active materials in both aqueous and non-aqueous RFBs are systematically elaborated,considering both symmetric and asymmetric configurations,which highlight theadaptability of POMs across diferent setups and their RFB performance.Looking ahead,future development strategies for POM-RFBs are chiefly elucidated,focusing onadvanced computational efforts,performance improvementand cost reduction of electrolyte-membrane-electrode,as well as forward-looking evaluations in commercial applications.Drawing from these discussions,this review is expected to furnish valuable insights for POM-RFBs both in-depth laboratory research and broad commercial deployment,concurrently laying a solid foundation for achieving the strategic goals in long-duration energy storage.

# 1．Introduction

Amidst the ongoing surge in global energy demand and heightened focus on renewable energy,energy storage technology has assumed an increasingly crucial role as a pivotal component in the modern power system [1,2].However,the inherent instability and intermittency of solar and wind energy have intensified the challenges of managing peak loads in the power grid,resulting in significant issues such as the abandonment of wind and solar power and the over-construction of grid infrastructure [3,4]. Redox flow batteries (RFBs) emerge as a promising solution to the challenges posed by the intermittency of renewable energy sources.Their attributes of high safety levels， long lifespan, excellent cycling stability,expansibility,and renewability,make them well-suited for mitigating the instability of solar and wind energy [5-7].

Furthermore,RFBs offer a flexible and efficient energy storage option, effectively addressing peak load management issues and enhancing the integration of renewable energy into the power grid. Currently,relatively mature All-Vanadium (All-V),Iron-Chromium (Fe-Cr),and Zinc-Bromine (Zn-Br) RFBs have gradually been adopted in the commercial large-scale energy storage projects [8-l2]. Especially,vanadium redox flow batteries (VRFBs) have attracted significant attention due to their abundant and accessible resources,highly controllable electrochemical reactions,and environmentally friendly properties [13,14]. Nevertheless, VRFBs still confront challenges such as low energy density caused by the prevalent single-electron redox capability,phase transition/instability at different operating temperatures,side reactions leading to hydrogen and oxygen evolution,slow redox kinetics,and high cost of electrolyte materials [15-17].These issues have consistently been bottlenecks, hindering the further development of VRFBs.

Indeed,it is for these reasons that the development of polyoxometalate cluster-based redox flow batteries (POM-RFBs）has emerged as one of the hotspots in research over the past decade in Fig.lA,[18-21]. Polyoxometalates (POMs) present unique advantages as charge carriers in electrochemical energy storage compared to traditional electrolyte materials.The multi-electron redox properties, strong stability,high solubility,tunable redox potential,and customizable structure make POMs highly promising for enhancing RFB performance,as well as exhibiting significant potential in addressing challenges related to renewable energy storage [22,23]. POMs are a category of inorganic metal-oxo clusters,comprising high oxidation state transition metals from Groups VB and VIB,along with heteroatoms from the fourth-period transition metals,main-group metals,and non-metals,which are coordinating and bridging with O atoms in specific structure and ratio.Up till now,POMs as active materials in RFBs mainly belong to the Lindqvist type $( [ \mathbf { M } _ { 6 } \mathbf { O } _ { 1 9 } ] ^ { n - }$ ,Fig.1B), Keggin type $( [ ( \mathrm { X O } _ { 4 } ) \mathrm { M } _ { 1 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 6 } ] ^ { n - }$ ，Fig.1C)，and Dawson type $\left( [ ( \mathrm { X O _ { 4 } } ) _ { 2 } \mathrm { M } _ { 1 8 } \mathrm { O } _ { 5 4 } ] ^ { n - } \right.$ ， Fig.1D).Here,X represents the central heteroatom (such as P, Si,As, B, Co,and Fe),and M denotes the skeleton metal atom (such as W,Mo,V, Nb,and Ta).These POMs are specifically composed of $\mathrm { { X O } _ { 4 } }$ tetrahedral units (such as $\mathrm { P O } _ { 4 }$ ， $\mathrm { S i O } _ { 4 }$ ,and $\mathrm { C o O } _ { 4 } \mathrm { , }$ and $\mathbf { M O } _ { 6 }$ octahedral frameworks (e. $g . , W O _ { 6 } , \mathrm { M o O } _ { 6 } ,$ and $\mathrm { V O } _ { 6 } )$ ,offering unique electrochemical properties for

RFB applications [24-28]. To further enhance the energy density of RFBs,aqueous redox flow batteries (ARFBs) with high solubility and non-aqueous redox flow batteries (NARFBs） with a wide potential window have been developed based on POM electrolyte active materials.Additionally,symmetric and asymmetric RFBs have also been derived from these foundations [18,19,29,3O].Thus,POM-ARFBs offer advantages such as simple electrolyte composition,enhanced safety,and reduced cost,whereas side reactions may occur due to the limited electrochemical window or the disintegration of POM clusters [22,31, 32].Additionally,POM-NARFBs mitigate some aqueous solution side reactions,thereby enhancing stability and allowing operation within a higher voltage range.However,POM-NARFBs also face inherent challenges regarding the high cost of organic electrolytes,the low solubility of POM in most organic solvent,low ion conductivity,and safety concerns [25,28,33,34].

This review aims to comprehensively summarize the latest research findings on emerging POM-RFBs,as illustrated in Fig.2.It highlights the unique merits of POMas electrolyte active materials in RFBs,including their multi-electron redox properties,strong stability,and high solubility.These merits significantly contribute to the performance metrics of POM-RFBs related to volumetric capacity and energy,as well as output efficiency and capacity retention. It then elaborates on the latest research applications in employing POMas electrolyte active materials within RFBs,showcasing developments both in symmetric and asymmetric configurations for POM-ARFBs and POM-NARFBs. Special emphasis is placed on the development strategies of advanced computational efforts in understanding reaction mechanisms and guiding POM design，performance improvement and cost reduction of electrolytemembrane-electrode,and comprehensive evaluation of commercial applications,with the target to provide directional guidance for the further advancement of POM-RFBs. The in-depth development and widespread application of POM-RFBs are expected to open new avenues for innovation and sustainable development in energy storage technology.

# 2.Critical merits and performance metrics for POM-RFBs

# 2.1． Critical merits for POM electrolytes

# 2.1.1. Multi-electron redox property

POM clusters are distinguished by their multiple metal centers,each capable of accepting or releasing electrons through redox reactions.The general cyclic voltammogram curves of Keggin and Dawson-type POMs in Fig.3A illustrate this feature.For example,the Keggin-type $\mathrm { H _ { 3 } } [ \mathrm { P W _ { 1 2 } O _ { 4 0 } } ]$ (abbreviated as $\{ \mathrm { P W } _ { 1 2 } \} )$ features twelve redox-active metal sites,which comprise four W(VI) centers with each center consisting of three W atoms.W(VI) shifts to W(V) or even W(IV) by gaining electrons during the charging process.Therefore, $\{ \mathrm { P W } _ { 1 2 } \}$ could accept a total of 24 electrons transforming into the highly reduced state $[ \mathrm { P W _ { 1 2 } O _ { 4 0 } } ] ^ { 2 7 - }$ [36-38].Furthermore,the Dawson-type POM clusters, exemplified by $\mathrm { \Delta [ P _ { 2 } W _ { 1 8 } O _ { 6 2 } ] ^ { 6 - } ( \{ P _ { 2 } W _ { 1 8 } \} ) }$ ,can be structurally regarded as dimers composed of two Keggin-type POMs. The $\{ \mathrm { P } _ { 2 } \mathrm { W } _ { 1 8 } \} _ { \mathrm { : } }$ ，alongwith $[ \mathrm { P _ { 5 } W _ { 3 0 } O _ { 1 1 0 } } ] ^ { \mathrm { 1 5 - } } ~ ( \mathrm { \{ P _ { 5 } W _ { 3 0 } \} } )$ and $[ \bar { \mathrm { P } _ { 8 } } \mathrm { \bar { W } _ { 4 8 } } \mathrm { O } _ { 1 8 4 } ] ^ { 4 0 - } ~ ( \{ \mathrm { P } _ { 8 } \mathrm { W } _ { 4 8 } \} ) _ { \mathrm { : } }$ capable of undergoing redox reactions to store and release more than thirty-six electrons [39,4O].This multi-electron process offers a distinct advantage over conventional redox mediators,marking POMs as superior candidates foradvanced energy storage solutions.Therefore,POMs have been commonly described asan "electron reservoir"[41] oran "electron sponge"[42],and the super-reduced state POMis considered a "semi-- porous molecular capacitor" [36].The potential advantage of using POMs as an active material in RFBs lies in the highly reversible multi-electron redox processes and tunable redox characteristics, allowing POM-RFBs to store more electrical energy per unit mole,which contributes POMs to an ideal material for achieving higher capacity and energy density [18,19,22].Notably, this advantage,pivotal for boosting the performance of RFBs,is conspicuously absent in traditional All-V, Fe-Cr,and Zn-Br RFBs.

# 2.1.2. Strong stability

POMs introduce an innovative dimension to electrolyte stability within RFBs,as illustrated in Fig.3B,demonstrating exceptional resilience in preserving their thermal stability,acid stability,electrochemical stability,photochemical stability,and self-assembly chemical stability [43,44]. This remarkable stability is attributed to intrinsic features of the POM clusters,such as their "cage-like" architecture, the robustness of chemical bonds,and strategic charge distribution,which collectively contribute to maintaining the durable performance of POM electrolytes [45].Furthermore,the "cage-like" molecular framework of POMs is a testament to their robustness, constructed through strong metal/non-metal-oxygen bonds involving coordinating atoms and het-eroatoms,along with oxygen atoms (bridging oxygen and terminal oxygen). Such a resilient structure not only underscores the durability of POMs in electrochemical settings but also serves as a source of endless innovation for the advancement of energy storage systems [46-49]. Moreover,POMs exhibit superior stability in acidic solutions than in neutral or alkaline solutions,a characteristic derived from their synthesis process,which are typically assembled in strongly acidic aqueous or organic solvents.Furthermore, the high concentration of protons $( \mathrm { H } ^ { + } )$ could bond with the oxygen atoms of POMs,forming strong $\mathrm { O - H }$ bonds. This interaction is conducive to maintaining the strength and stability of the metal-oxygen bonds within POMs,thereby enhancing the overall stability and solubility of POMs in acidic conditions.Meanwhile, the inherent acidity effectively prevents the hydrolysis of POMs,thus maintaining their structural integrity [20,43,5O,51]. Conversely,the high concentration of $\mathrm { O H } ^ { - }$ under neutral or alkaline conditions could disrupt the multinuclear structure of POMs,causing them to decompose into simpler oxides or hydrated oxides.Additionally,POMs demonstrate exceptional solvation properties with water molecules due to their low freezing points, ensuring stability and suitability in electrolyte systems under both low-temperature and room-temperature conditions. Most importantly,POM clusters exhibit the unique capability to dissociate into ion states under specific conditions,followed by a reassembly process that restores their stable structures. With a typical size range of 1, 2 nm and bearing a negative charge, POM clusters are less prone to permeate through cation exchange membranes, efectively minimizing electrolyte cross-contamination in RFBs [52,53].Benefiting from the inherent stable structures, POM retains the structural integrity during the process of storing multiple electrons, supporting hundreds, perhaps thousands of charge-discharge cycles without decomposition, ensuring the longevity and reliability of POM-RFBs.

# 2.1.3.High solubility

The widespread applications of POMs,as well as their importance in geochemistry and environmental systems,are typically derived from their high solubility [54].This solubility is facilitatedby the interactions between the metal centers and oxygen atoms of the POMs and water molecules through hydrogen bonding，causing POM clusters to be enveloped by water molecules and form stable hydrates with good solubility in the solution.The high solubility of POMs facilitates the full dissolution and even distribution throughout the electrolyte, ensuring the efficient progresson of charge and discharge reactions,as well as contributing to an increased theoretical energy density in RFBs. Nonetheless,the solubility of POM and the pursuit of more extensive electron redox reactions are contradictory,as the solubility presents a challenge for larger POM clusters [39].Keggin-type POMs,with their relatively compact structure,facilitate easier encapsulation and interaction with water molecules,enhancing their solubility.In contrast, Dawson-type POMs possess a larger and more complex structure,which makes them less prone to solvation, resulting in reduced solubility.Furthermore, the solubility of POMs is influenced by several factors,including the nature of heteroatoms and coordinating atoms,the presence of cations,the solvent used,and the temperature of the solution [45,55].As illustrated in Fig. 3C, the solubilityof POMs is markedly influenced by the "charge effect"and "size effect" associated with cations (or counterions),such as the solubility of M-POM $( M = \mathrm { L i ^ { + } / N a ^ { + } / K ^ { + } / R b ^ { + } / C s ^ { + } ) }$ tends to increase inversely with cation radius,or vice versa [35].The normal solubility trend is observed for most POM clusters including Mo,V,and W,while the abnormal trend is experienced mostly for $\mathbf { N b }$ and Ta POM clusters. Nevertheless,the large-sized cations (such as $\mathrm { K } ^ { + }$ ,and ${ \boldsymbol { \mathrm { C } } } { \boldsymbol { s } } ^ { * }$ ) may lead to a decrease in the solubility of POM or even cause precipitation. Literature reports suggest that the solubility of Keggin and Dawson-type POMs in water and organic solvents typically ranges from $0 . 7 { \mathrm { M } }$ to $\mathbf { 1 . 0 M }$ and 0.3 M to $0 . 5 { \mathrm { M } } { \mathrm { \Omega } }$ ,respectively[18-20,22].For instance,as depicted in Fig. 3D, the solubility of $\{ \mathrm { P } _ { 2 } \mathrm { W } _ { 1 8 } \}$ demonstrates a clear decrease as cation size increases,following the sequence of $\mathrm { H \mathrm { - } P O M } > \mathrm { L i \mathrm { - } P O M } > \mathrm { N a \mathrm { - } P O M } >$ $K$ -POM. Specifically,at $2 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ ,the solubilities of ${ \mathrm { H } } _ { 6 } \{ { \mathrm { P } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { W } } _ { 1 8 } \}$ ， $\mathrm { L i } _ { 6 } \{ \mathrm { P } _ { 2 } \mathrm { W } _ { 1 8 } \}$ ， $\mathrm { N a } _ { 6 } \{ \mathrm { P } _ { 2 } \mathrm { W } _ { 1 8 } \}$ ,and ${ \mathrm { K } } _ { 6 } \{ { \mathrm { P } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { W } } _ { 1 8 } \}$ are measured at $0 . 7 4 \mathrm { m o l / L }$ $( 3 2 3 3 . 1 2  g / \mathrm { L } )$ ， $0 . 6 2 \mathrm { m o l / L }$ $( 2 7 3 0 . 9 0 \ g / \mathrm { L } )$ ， $0 . 5 1 \mathrm { \ m o l / L }$ $( 2 2 9 5 . 4 9 \ { \bf g } / { \bf L } )$ ,and $0 . 1 1 \mathrm { \ m o l / L }$ $( 5 0 5 . 7 4 \ \mathrm { g / L } )$ ,respectively [18].Furthermore,the solubility of POMs is observed to decrease as the ratio of the charge carried by the POManion to the number of metal atoms $( { \bf q } / { \bf M } )$ increases, going from a ${ \bf q } / { \bf M }$ ratio of 0.3 in $\{ \mathrm { P } _ { 2 } \mathrm { W } _ { 1 8 } \}$ ,0.5 in $\{ \mathrm { P } _ { 5 } \mathrm { W } _ { 3 0 } \}$ ,and to 0.83 in $\{ \mathrm { P } _ { 8 } \mathrm { W } _ { 4 8 } \}$ [39]. Therefore, it is crucial to consider factors such as size,cations,and charge to seek a balance in the process of achieving multi-electron storage with POM while ensuring its solubility,which contributes to high capacity and energy density RFBs.

# 2.2.Performance metrics for POM-RFBs

# 2.2.1． Volumetric capacity and energy

POMs are typically dissolved in either inorganic or organic solvents to serve as charge carriers in the negative electrolyte for assembling the POM-RFBs.For symmetric RFBs,both electrolytes utilize the same POM species,while either different POMs or small molecule redox couples are selected to form the positive electrolytes for asymmetric RFBs [21,56, 57].As illustrated in Fig.4A,the electrolyte containing charge carriers is pumped to circulate through the electrodes in the electrochemical cell. The electrical energy and chemical energy are converted back and forth during charging and discharging,involving POM undergoes a reduction/oxidation reaction at the anode while a corresponding oxidation/reduction reaction at the cathode for another redox couple [28,58]. The electrolyte on each side is separated by either a cation-exchange or anion-exchange membrane,which enables counterbalancing ions to pass through to maintain charge equilibrium,while effectively inhibiting the undesired crossover of charge carriers to the opposing half-cell. Such a feature is instrumental in preventing self-discharge phenomena in RFBs.Meanwhile,RFBs are designed with a clear distinction between the reaction zones (within a single cell or galvanic pile) and storage zones (located in tanks for positive and negative electrolytes) to ensure a relatively independent flexibility between power and capacity.

The Qt, $\mathrm { Q } _ { \mathrm { s } } ,$ E,and ED primarily depend on the charge carrier of the number of electrons transferred per molecule (n), the concentration (c),and the volume (v). Furthermore,the capacity can be categorized into negative electrolyte capacity,positive electrolyte capacity,and overall cell capacity,diferentiated by specific volume considerations (Eq.1-4).As for the asymmetric POM-RFBs,it is common practice to maintain an excess of positive electrolyte relative to the negative electrolyte (the POM electrolyte) to fully leverage the multi-electron redox characteristics of the POM for enhancing the capacity [18,21]. # 3．Applications of POM as electrolyte active materials for RFBs

The key parameters and performance outcomes of POM-RFBs that utilize POMs as active materials are listed in Table 2,including POMbased symmetric ARFBs,POM-based asymmetric ARFBs,POM-based symmetric NARFBs,and POM-based asymmetric NARFBs.

# 3.1．POM-based symmetric ARFBs

POM-based symmetric ARFBs entail the utilization of the same POM cluster as both positive and negative electrolytes,mirroring the design principle seen in VRFBs.This arrangement capitalizes on the reversible redox properties inherent to the heteroatoms,coordinating atoms,or substituted atoms within a singular POM cluster.Such a configuration not only simplifies the electrolyte composition but also exploits the multi-electron redox properties of POMs, facilitating efficient energy storage and release.

In 2013,Anderson et al.[66] first introduced an atom substituted Keggin-type $\mathsf { \Gamma } [ \mathsf { S i V } _ { 3 } \mathsf { W } _ { 9 } \mathsf { O } _ { 4 0 } ] ^ { 7 - } \mathrm { \Gamma } ( \{ \mathsf { S i V } _ { 3 } \mathsf { W } _ { 9 } \}$ ,V substitutes W) as active material for symmetric RFB electrolyte,marking the formal opening of a new chapter in POM-RFBs research.As depicted in Fig. 5A,the symmetrical RFB was constructed using $2 0 \mathrm { \ m M \ } \mathrm { \ G i V _ { 3 } W _ { 9 } } \}$ in $0 . 5 \mathrm { M } \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } S \mathrm { O } _ { 4 }$ as electrolyte,which features two distinct three-electron redox couples $\mathrm { ( \left[ S i V _ { 3 } ^ { V } W _ { 9 } ^ { V I } O _ { 4 0 } \right] ^ { 7 - } / \left[ S i V _ { 3 } ^ { I V } W _ { 9 } ^ { V I } O _ { 4 0 } \right] ^ { 1 0 - } }$ and $\left[ \mathrm { S i V _ { 3 } ^ { I V } W _ { 9 } ^ { V I } O _ { 4 0 } } \right] ^ { 1 0 - } / \left[ \mathrm { S i V _ { 3 } ^ { I V } W _ { 3 } ^ { V } } \right]$ $\mathrm { W } _ { 6 } ^ { \mathrm { V I } } \mathrm { O } _ { 4 0 } \big ] ^ { 1 3 - } )$ . The CE exceeds $9 5 ~ \%$ ,and the CRR is approximately 0.1 $\%$ /cycle after 1oo cycles.Moreover,the performance of RFB could be restored for the over discharged (to $- 2 \textsf { V } )$ and reassembling the electrolyte with new membrane after cycles,indicating that $\{ \mathsf { S i V } _ { 3 } \mathsf { W } _ { 9 } \}$ exhibitsstrong self-healing characteristics.Following the initial breakthrough,as illustrated in Fig.5B,the research group expanded their investigation to a series of symmetric RFBs involving atom substituted clusters $\mathrm { A { - } \alpha { - } P V _ { 3 } W _ { 9 } O _ { 4 0 } }$ $\mathrm { \mathbf { B } { \cdot } \boldsymbol { \alpha } { \cdot } \mathbf { P } V _ { 3 } W _ { 9 } O _ { 4 0 } } ;$ and $\mathrm { P _ { 2 } V _ { 3 } W _ { 1 5 } O _ { 6 2 } }$ $\scriptstyle ( \{ \mathsf { A } \cdot \mathsf { P V } _ { 3 } \mathsf { W } _ { 9 } \}$ ， $\{ \mathrm { B } { \cdot } \mathrm { P V } _ { 3 } \mathrm { W } _ { 9 } \}$ ,and $\{ \mathrm { P } _ { 2 } \mathrm { V } _ { 3 } \mathrm { W } _ { 1 5 } \} )$ .These systems contains two three-electron redox couples $\left( \mathrm { \left[ P V _ { 3 } ^ { I V } W _ { 9 } ^ { V I } O _ { 4 0 } \right] ^ { 6 - } / \left[ P V _ { 3 } ^ { V } W _ { 9 } ^ { V I } O _ { 4 0 } \right] ^ { 9 - } } \right.$ and $\mathrm { \big [ P V _ { 3 } ^ { I V } W _ { 9 } ^ { V I } O _ { 4 0 } \big ] ^ { 9 - } } / \mathrm { \big [ P V _ { 3 } ^ { I V } W _ { 3 } ^ { V } W _ { 6 } ^ { V I } O _ { 4 0 } \big ] ^ { 1 2 - } } )$ , where A and B represent angle sharing and edge sharing between adjacent $\mathrm { v }$ atoms, respectively [67]. The results indicate that $\{ \mathrm { A } { \cdot } \mathrm { P V } _ { 3 } \mathrm { W } _ { 9 } \}$ undergoes side reactions during the cycling process to generate $\mathrm { [ P V _ { 2 } W _ { 1 0 } O _ { 4 0 } ] } ^ { 5 - }$ and $\mathrm { [ P V W _ { 1 1 } O _ { 4 0 } ] } ^ { 4 - }$ ,resulting in the CE less than $9 0 ~ \%$ .The electrochemical yield (EY),which represents the measured charge and discharge capacity as a percentage of theoretical capacity,for charge and discharge ranges between $4 0 ~ \%$ and $5 0 \%$ .In contrast, the RFB utilizing $\{ \mathrm { B } { \cdot } \mathrm { P V } _ { 3 } \mathrm { W } _ { 9 } \}$ forms precipitates on the electrode during the redox process,causing the CE levels fluctuating between $5 0 ~ \%$ and $7 0 \ \%$ .Additionally,the charging EY exhibited a decline from $9 0 ~ \%$ to $6 0 ~ \%$ over time,whereas the discharging EY remained consistently around $4 5 ~ \%$ .In addition,after 20 cycles,the charging and discharging EY of $\{ \mathrm { P } _ { 2 } \mathrm { V } _ { 3 } \mathrm { W } _ { 1 5 } \}$ -RFB decreases from approximately $1 0 0 ~ \%$ to $7 5 \ \%$ ,and $3 3 ~ \%$ to $2 4 \ \%$ ,respectively. These results indicate that atom substituted POM clusters present limited reversible stability.Further attention should be directed towards enhancing the redox reversibility of POM clusters following atom substitution，to ensure their viability in energy storage applications. Notably,the researches mentioned were performed on RFBs with a POM concentration of only $2 0 \mathrm { m M }$ ,which is clearly insuficient to support the high-capacity energy storage.

# 4.1.1.Understanding reaction mechanism by first-principles calculations

Advanced computational approaches enable the exploration of multi-scale chemical mechanisms and structural characteristics from the microscopic to mesoscopic levels by facilitating simulations and analyses of electrochemical reactions across atomic,molecular,and mesoscopic scales for POM-RFBs.First-principles calculation methods,also recognized as ab initio methods,are rooted in quantum mechanics and utilize the fundamental interactions between atomic nuclei and electrons,alongside their basic laws of motion.These methods strive to accurately solve the Schrodinger equation precisely,shedding light on the electronic structure and inherent properties of materials.In contrast to empirical and semi-empirical approaches,first-principles calculations depend solely on universal physical parameters such as electron mass $\left( \mathbf { m } _ { 0 } \right)$ ,elementary charge (e),Planck's constant (h), the speed of light (c), and the Boltzmann constant (k) to reasonably predict the physical and chemical properties of a material in its ground state [85,86].Density functional theory (DFT) calculations and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, based on first-principles,have emerged as high-precision tools offering advanced benchmarks in disciplines such as materials science,chemistry,and pharmaceuticals [87-89].The integration of these computational methods with electrochemical and spectroscopic testing techniques,plays a pivotal role in unraveling the intricate reaction mechanisms of cycling processes in POM-RFBs and elucidating the causes of capacity decay associated with POM electrolytes in Fig.11A. Such comprehensive investigations contribute to understanding how smaller-scale structure-activity relationships on the macroscopic performance of POM electrolytes and RFB systems,thus offering the potential to transcend the confines of traditional experimental analysis.

Firstly,DFT calculations are instrumental in dissecting the intricate details of POM clusters and their intermediates,including bond parameters,orbital parameters,and electron distributions [9o,91].These help in identifying central atoms or oxidized groups within POM clusters that exhibit high reactivity toward redox reactions,thereby facilitating a deeper understanding of the pathways for electron transport [19,91]. Such insights are crucial for unraveling the multi-electron oxidation-reduction properties inherent to POM clusters and optimizing their performance in RFBs.Furthermore,DFT calculations equipped with various solvent models like the Solvation Model based on Density (SMD) [92],are employed to facilitate accurate thermodynamic calculations. By simulating the intermediates and transition states involved in the redox reactions,these calculations allow foran in-depth analysis of reaction pathways,energy barriers,and rate-limiting steps,ultimately revealing the detailed mechanism of POM clusters undergoing redox reactions on the electrode [93].Ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations are poised to offer unprecedented insights into the dynamics of POM clusters in solution,including potential aggregation,dissociation,and self-assembly behaviors [94].Complementing this,deep potential molecular dynamics (DPMD) simulations represent a significant advancement by integrating the computational efficiency with the inherent accuracy of first-principles calculations [95,96].This synergy provides a substantial benefit over traditional AIMD by addressing the challenges of expensive computational time and cost typically associated with simulating POM clusters [94,95].Therefore,the integration of multiscale computational simulations with experimental results contributes to a clearer comprehension of the fundamental redox properties of POM clusters. These approaches aid both in gaining a thorough understanding of the reaction mechanisms in POM-RFBs and providing guidance for the designing of POM clusters and optimization of the performance of RFBs.

The optimization of electrolytes for POM-RFBs depends not only on the well-chosen POM clusters as negative electrolytes but also on the strategic collocating with proper redox couples for positive electrolytes, as well as solvents,which are essential for establishing RFB performance with excellent stability,solubility,potential window,safety,and costeffectiveness in Fig.l2C.Here,numerous options exist for selecting appropriate redox couples for the positive electrolyte side,drawing inspiration from established RFBs,including but not limited to All-V,Fe-Cr, Zn-Br/I, $\mathbf { Z n - M n }$ ,and various organic RFBs [16,126-129]. Asymmetric POM-RFB configurations can be achieved by integrating proven redox coupleslike $\mathrm { V O } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { V O } _ { 2 } ^ { + }$ ， $\mathrm { B r _ { 2 } / B r ^ { - } }$ ， $\mathbf { B r C l } _ { 2 } ^ { - } / \mathbf { B r } ^ { - }$ ， $r / \mathrm { I } _ { 3 } ^ { - }$ ，[Fe $\mathrm { ( C N ) _ { 6 } ] ^ { 3 - } / [ F e ( C N ) _ { 6 } ] ^ { 4 - } }$ with POMs,which is advantageous for achieving a synergistic balance between the multi-electron redox capabilities of POMs and the cost-effectiveness and stability of established couples [15, 18,130-132].This innovative approach not only extends the operational voltage window but also significantly enhances the overall battery capacity and energy density per unit volume.It is crucial to prevent cross-contamination to understand the ionic species nature of the redox couples and their interaction with the membrane.Although POM clusters typically exist in an anionic form with dimensions in the range of 1 $\sim 2 \ : \mathrm { n m }$ ,there is still a potential risk of cross-contamination through the cation exchange membrane if the redox couples on the other side involve small-size cations such as $\mathrm { V O } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { V O } _ { 2 } ^ { + }$ [23].To mitigate this risk,a strategic approach involves the configurations of all anion redox couples for POM-RFBs,which leverages both the anionic nature of the POM clusters and compatible anionic redox couples on the positive electrolyte side [2O].Most importantly,the ideal development target for POM-RFBs would be a symmetric RFB system, where both the catholyte and anolyte utilize the same electrolyte active material [21,68].This configuration effectively eliminates the risk of cross-contamination,however,it presents challenges in molecular design as it requires enabling redox reactions on the same POM cluster.Unfortunately,half of the electron transfer may not occur or could be wasted,ultimately leading to limited energy density [66,67].Replacing coordination atoms or heteroatoms could enable redox reactions to occur on different atoms within the same POM cluster,which may help avoid wasting of the transfer electrons [19, 21].

Furthermore,the solvent acts as a medium for the redox processes of active materials,influencing the choice of counterions and construction of reaction conditions,further influencing solubility,the operational voltage window,and potential side reactions of POM-RFBs [19,34,74]. On the one hand,the counterions must be compatible with the solvent to ensure efficient ion exchange and maintain electrical neutrality within the electrolyte.Specifically, $\mathrm { H } ^ { + }$ and alkali metal ions in the specific solvent serve as protons across the cation exchange membrane,facilitating charge balance in both ARFBs and NARFBs [16,18].While $\mathrm { P F } _ { 6 } ^ { - }$ is predominantly employed to shuttle through the anion exchange membrane in NARFBs,leveraging their high solubility in polar organic solvents like the acetonitrile [73,74]. On the other hand,utilizing $\mathrm { H } ^ { + }$ as a proton necessitates the operation of POM-RFBs under strongly acidic conditions similar to the VRFB,while employing small cations (such as $\mathrm { L i } ^ { + }$ )allows for the configuration of milder conditions with weak acidity ornear neutrality，as well as the POM-NARFBs operate under near-neutral and alkaline conditions [18,22,73]．Moreover,smaller cations are generally more cost-effective since they could further improve the conductivity,ion transport property,and redox potential of POM electrolytes.The choice of solvent is also pivotal in determining the properties of electrolytes.Water is favored for its high safety, cost-effectiveness,and environmental friendliness,facilitating active material dissolution yet offering a limited electrochemical window due to water electrolysis potential [21,133].This limitation can be addressed by employing organic solvents,such as acetonitrile and acetic acid, which are known for their wider electrochemical windows [134,135]. However, their adoption in POM-RFBs introduces challnges including reduced solubility,increased flammability and toxicity,as well as environmental concerns [25,74].With the continuous evolution of POM electrolytes, the exploration of innovative solvent systems remains a key area of focus,with the potential to unlock new possibilities for the advancement of POM-RFBs.

# 4.2.3.Developing low-cost proprietary membranes

Ion exchange membranes (IEMs) serve as another pivotal component following the electrolyte in RFBs,not only affecting the ohmic polarization losses of the RFB but also determining the CE and capacity [68, 136,137]．Among these,Nafion membranes (typically N115,N117, NR211,and NR2l2) developed by DuPont,are predominant,characterized by their perfluorosulfonic acid polymer structure with polytetrafluoroethylene backbone and sulfonic acid-terminated side chains, exhibiting excellent cation selectivity [138,l39]. Nafion membranes, despite their extensive applications in fuel cells,RFBs,and chlor-alkali production, exhibit limitations in fully leveraging the distinctive large ion size of POM clusters [57,14O-142].In addition,Nafion IEMs still face several challenges in RFBs,including their nonspecific ion transport, limited ion conductivity,complex manufacturing processes,and high costs $( \sim 5 0 0 \$ 5 m ^ { - 2 } )$ ,as well as environmental and health concerns arise from by-products generated during production [143,144].This shortfall underscores the necessity for alternative IEM solutions and innovative membrane technologies tailored to the unique properties of POMs,enhancing the efficiency and functionality of POM-RFBs.Researchers have investigated affordable commercial dialysis membranes $( \mathrm { M W C O } 1 0 0 , \sim 2 6 \ : \$ 9 \ : \mathrm { m } ^ { - 2 } )$ and the anion exchange membrane AMI-7001 (Membranes-International Ltd.,USA)as IEMs for POM-RFBs [23,74]. These alternatives have demonstrated promising results,delivering performance that rivals Nafion IEMs at a fraction of the cost,but still fall short of achieving the low-cost proprietary characteristics inherent to POM-RFBs.Moreover,an innovative modification to the Nafion membrane involves incorporating fluorinated amphiphilic block copolymers (FBCs)and POMs,acting as molecular stitches as nanoscale buckles respectively [l45].This approach creates a "supramolecular patch" within the Nafion membrane,enhancing proton conduction while simultaneously obstructing vanadium ion permeation, demonstrating a significant improvement in hybrid membrane performance in terms of efficiency and selectivity.

Developing an ideal low-cost proprietary membrane dedicated to POM-RFBs should hinge on several critical features,as presented in Fig.l2D,including propriety,selectivity,stability,sustainability,and cost-effectiveness,to ensure optimal performance and longevity.First of all, the membrane must be versatile enough to operate effectively in strongly acidic,neutral,or alkaline environments,accommodating the diverse conditions of POM-RFBs in both aqueous and non-aqueous systems.Additionally,the membrane must feature high selective permeability for protons,employing cluster size,charge repulsion,and spatial confinement mechanisms to prevent the permeation of POM clusters with varying valence states,thus reducing self-discharge.Meanwhile, enhancing the mechanical strength,chemical resistance,dimensional stability,low intrinsic resistance,and high conductivity of the membrane,contributes to operational efficiency and longer cycling lifespan. Moreover,itis crucial to derive the membrane from abundant materials, with a simple and efficient preparation process,ensuring the IEMs are low-cost,environmentally friendly,renewable,and scalable for largescale production.

# 4.2.4.Electrode optimization and catalyst screening

The optimization of electrode structure and the selection of catalysts are crucial for enhancing the performance of RFBs.Unlike secondary batteries where electrodes are active participants in reactions,RFB electrodes serve as a medium for electron transfer during electrochemical reactions [5,32,146].Therefore,the ideal electrode materials should exhibit excellent conductivity,high surface area for increased reaction sites,chemical stability to withstand corrosive environments, and flexibility to accommodate volume changes without degradation, thus ensuring efficient and stable operation of POM-RFBs [147,148]. Commonly utilized electrode materials in VRFBs and POM-RFBs,such as carbon felt (CF),graphite felt (GF),and carbon paper (CP),often face challenges due to their inherent electrochemical inactivity and poor hydrophilicity [66,147,149,150].This limitation leads to feweractive sites for redox couples and limited electrolyte accessibility,ultimately impeding the advancement of RFB technology. Through surface modification, multi-layer structure design, pore structure control,and thermal or chemical treatments,electrodes could be optimized for enhanced electrochemical performance and stability [151,152].These approaches aim to refine surface or pore characteristics,improving electrolyte diffusion and transport within the electrode. Such enhancements facilitatea higher rate of electrochemical reactions,thereby boosting the efficiency and effectiveness of RFBs.Additionally,the slow kinetics of $\mathrm { V } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { V } ^ { 3 + }$ redox couples in VRFBs result in significant energy losses due to high overpotentials.Introducing catalysts,such as the abundant electron-deficient $\mathrm { T i B } _ { 2 }$ ,helps accelerate these redox reactions [149,153, 154]. $\mathrm { T i B } _ { 2 }$ with a graphite-like layered structure of B atoms and Ti outer electron configuration of enhances theoretical conductivity,facilitating the absorption of electrons from $\mathrm { V } ^ { 2 + }$ to promote the conversion of $\mathrm { V } ^ { 2 + }$ to $\mathsf { V } ^ { 3 + }$ thereby improving the overall reaction kinetics [15]. Despite the promising applications of POM-RFBs,research into the kinetics and rate-determining steps of redox reactions within POM electrolytes remains scant.Furthermore,studies focusing on electrode catalysts specifically for POM-RFBs are limited,indicatinga significant gap in the current understanding and optimization of these systems.

Therefore,as visualized in Fig.12E,research on electrode structure and catalyst screening for POM-RFBs should concentrate on innovative strategies to enhance electrochemical performance.This entails the interface refinementof electrodesand POM electrolytes through surface engineering to promote reaction rates,utilizing highly conductive substrates or additives to minimize electrode resistance and enhance electron conduction efficiency.Designing targeted catalysts tailored to the unique requirements of diverse POM clusters involves the integration of functional groups or modifiers such as carbon-based materials,metals, metal oxides,and carbon-metal hybrids,markedly improving the catalytic activity for redox processes within POM-RFBs.Moreover,developing high-quality model electrodes and catalysts with wide availability of raw materials,easy scalability,and renewability is crucial to reducing the costs and environmental impact of POM-RFBs.Such advancements are instrumental in facilitating the transition of POM-RFB technology from laboratory research to commercial applications.

# 4.3. Comprehensive evaluation of commercial applications

The ultimate goal for the development and innovation of POM-RFBs is to achieve commercialization,providing an effective and sustainable long-duration $( > 1 0 \ \mathrm { ~ h ~ } )$ energy storage (LDES) solution [155,156]. Therefore,with the ongoing efforts to achieve stable operation of RFBs in the laboratory,it is essential to evaluate the safety,economic feasibility,and environmental friendliness of POM-RFBs in advance and deploy scale-up and industrial development.As illustrated in Fig.13, this comprehensive evaluation will guide the scale-up and industrial development processes of POM-RFBs,ensuring they fulfill market demands while adhering to sustainability and safety standards.

# 4.3.1． Appraising of safety measures

POM-RFBs offer inherent safety advantages over the potential safety hazards with lithium-ion batteries,owing to the electrolyte being stored in separate liquid tanks and the battery unit being isolated from the liquid tanks [157,158].Additionally,POM-ARFBs are considered safer compared to POM-NARFBs due to their lower risk of combustion.This configuration makes RFBs a safer alternative for large-scale energy storage applications.Therefore,ensuring the safety of POM-RFBs necessitates a focus on comprehensive monitoring and appraising throughout all stages of their lifecycle,including the design,production, usage,and eventual retirement or disposal stages.This holistic approach to safety management ensures that POM-RFBs are not only eficient in their operation but also adhere to the highest safety standards.In the initial design stage of POM-RFBs,the emphasis should be conducted thorough assessments of the modular battery structures,thermal and electrical management systems,and the electrochemical and chemical stability,thermal stability,and related equipment and materials.This proactive approach in the design phase is essential for laying a foundation that prioritizes safety and minimizes risks throughout the lifecycle of the battery system.During the production and usage stages of POM-RFBs,it's crucial to carefully prepare the POM electrolytes and assemble the batteries,ensuring safe energy storage and release. This involves a thorough examination of reagents,consumables,and the electrochemical, chemical,and thermal stability of both the POM electrolytes and the supporting electrolytes.Comprehensive testing and maintenance,aligned with established safety protocols and emergency response measures,are essential.These steps ensure the stable operation of POM-RFBs and help prevent safety incidents during energy storage and release processes.For the disposal of retired POM-RFBs, developing a pivotal battery disposal plan is imperative.This plan should cover the recovery of valuable materials,including electrolytes,membranes, electrode materials,and battery structures.Additionally,it should outline the harmless and reduction-based treatment methods for waste materials,incorporating the third-party environmental assessment of the disposal site to ensure compliance with relevant regulations and environmental standards,thereby safeguarding the environment and human health.

# 4.3.2.Enhancing of economy feasibility

Levelized cost of energy (LCOE） serves as a crucial metric for assessing the cost-effectiveness of energy projects,encapsulating the average net present cost of electricity delivered over the lifetime [159, 160].As the renewables penetration increases to over $8 0 ~ \%$ ,electricity grids require long-term energy storage to meet demand and help maintain low electricity prices,thereby reducing LCOE is fundamental for attaining the economic viability of POM-RFBs [155].Therefore,it is crucial to focus on reducing the production costs of key materials,such as POM electrolytes,ion exchange membranes,and electrodes at the sources. Simultaneously,enhancing overall performance such as the energy conversion efficiency and the cycle life of POM-RFBs is also beneficial for reducing the lifetime cost (LC),which includes all expenses from construction and operation to retirement.In addition, adopting a standardized design through modularization and system integration simplifies the deployment and maintenance of RFB systems. This approach makes the POM-RFBs more scalable and maintainable, effectively reducing the construction and operational costs [16l].The modularity allows for easier expansion and adaptation to various energy requirements,making POM-RFBs a more versatile and cost-effective solution for renewable energy storage needs.Establishing collaborationswith energy companies,grid operators,and other stakeholders is essential to jointly drive the commercial application of POM-RFBs and overcome market entry barriers.Moreover,the economic viability of POM-RFBs is closely tied to technological advancements,production capacity,and strategic timing.Such partnerships can facilitate the integration of POM-RFBs into the energy market,leveraging collective resources and expertise to enhance the technology's competitiveness and adoption rate.POM-RFBs are expected to exhibit significant cost advantages and cost reduction potential in the field of energy storage, with the technology matures and becomes more refined, the industry chain matching production capacity increasingly,and more research teams and enterprises pushing for market development.This progress will enhance the competitivenessand appeal of POM-RFBs in the energy storage market,making thema more viable and attractive option for large-scale deployment and commercial application.

# 4.3.3.Assessing of environmental friendliness

To achieve $1 0 0 ~ \%$ carbon-free grid goals,eco-efficiency could be used to evaluate the effectiveness of POM-RFBs in terms of their capability to facilitate deep decarbonization [158,162]. More importantly, the environmental friendliness assessment is fundamental for the sustainable development of POM-RFBs,encompassing the entire lifecycle from raw material sourcing to waste disposal [l63].This holistic approach ensures that POM-RFBs contribute positively to environmental sustainability during the energy storage processes,meeting current needs without compromising future resources and environmental health.Assessing the environmental friendliness of POM-RFBs involves multiple aspects,including the sourcing of raw materials,production processes,battery operation,and waste disposal. Ensuring that every stage minimizes environmental impact is crucial for aligning with sustainable development goals and safeguarding future resources and the environment.It should emphasize the utilization of renewable and alternative rawmaterials fromwidely available sources,optimization of production processes to minimize environmental impact,and enhance resource utilization efficiency to reduce the environment deterioration in terms of the carbon footprint [164]. Optimizing the battery system, reducing energy waste,extending the cycle life,and minimizing maintenance needs are crucial during the operation of POM-RFBs to ensure enhancing the energy conversion efficiency and environmental benefits. Forretired RFBs,developing an environmentally friendly disposal plan is vital.This plan should aim to facilitate the timely recovery of valuable materials,reduce the demand for new raw materials,and ensure the disposal process complies with environmental regulations. Moreover, a lifecycle assessment of POM-RFBs is essential to quantify and evaluate their environmental impacts at all stages,encompassing material acquisition, processing,manufacturing,operation,and disposal, which helps in identifying areas for improvement.Furthermore,establishing a transparent and regular environmental monitoring mechanism is critical for ensuring that POM-RFBs track environmental performance,further ensuring compliance with environmental standards,and contributing to sustainable development.

# 5.Conclusions and prospects

This article systematically reviews the advancements of emerging POM-RFBs over the past decade.It conducts an in-depth and prospective evaluation on the critical merits of POMs,the performance metrics and applications of POM-RFBs in order to demonstrate why and how the electron-sponge-like POMs is beneficial to design next-generation RFBs. Future challenges and promising development strategies for POM-RFBs are also emphasized. Overall, POMs,as a new generation charge carrier for RFBs, have demonstrated excellent electrochemical performance and achieved significant foundational research achievements.They offer solutions to issues like low energy density,electrolyte crossover,and slow kinetics found in traditional RFBs such as All-V,Fe-Cr,and Zn-Br systems.Despite these advancements,challenges remain in further enhancing the energy density,power density,and cycle stability,as well as avoiding electrolyte side reactions and self-decomposition, reducing costs,and facilitating the commercialization of POM-RFBs.Considerable attention is dedicated to development strategies for the next-generation POM-RFBs,incorporating advanced computational efforts in understanding reaction mechanisms and guiding POM design,performance improvement and cost reduction of electrolyte-membrane-electrode, and comprehensive evaluation of commercial applications.

Future research on POM-RFBs should concentrate on diversifying POM synthesis raw materials,improving POM electrolyte formulations, creating unique membrane technologies,optimizing electrode materials,and developing advanced catalysts. These require a profound understanding of energy storage mechanisms,integrating theoretical calculations with data-driven machine learning approaches to reveal new insights and drive technology innovations in POM-RFBs.It is a crucial step in establishing the structure-performance relationship of POM electrolytes that integrates advanced material characterization techniques such as X-ray absorption fine structure spectroscopy,in situ-Fourier Transform infrared spectroscopy,in situ Raman spectroscopy, and in situ electrochemical mass spectrometry with POM-RFBs.Moreover,establishing comprehensive evaluation criteria and standard systems is crucial for the advancement of POM-RFBs.Increasing the number of electrons transferred per molecule is indeed a valuable strategy for improving the energy density of RFBs.However，this parameter should not be evaluated in isolation,but rather considered alongside solubility,stability,cost,and sustainability.The extent to which the advantages of POM and the overall performance of POM-RFBs could be fully realized and amplified hinges on various factors,including compatibility with membranes,electrodes,and flow fields,as well as testing conditions.Emphasizing the long-duration energy storage strategy is pivotal in the development and application of POMs in RFBs. This focus shifts away from the small-scale, high-purity POM development traditionally aimed at catalytic purposes,ensuring that POM-RFBs are developed with the need for sustainability and eficiency in largescale energy storage contexts.The overarching goal for POM-RFBs is to significantly enhance the energy density,achieve strong stability and long cycle life,and exponentially reduce costs,thereby boosting the competitiveness within the realm of RFBs and propelling energy storage technology to new heights.In-depth research and development of POM-RFBs hold the potential to make substantial contributions to renewable energy storage,peak shaving and valley filling in power systems,and supporting microgrids.
