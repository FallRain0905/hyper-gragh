# 1．Introduction

With the rapid advancement of industrialization and the growing urgency of carbon neutrality,there is rising global demand for efficient, scalable,and clean energy systems.Renewable energy sources such as solar,wind,and tidal power are playing an increasingly important role in the global energy system,but their inherent intermittency and weather dependence pose challenges to grid stability[1-6].Researchers have developed a variety of energy storage technologies to temporally and spatially regulate electricity,mitigate the imbalance caused by renewable energy integration,and enhance grid stability and operational efciency [7-11].Among them,physical methods such as pumped hydro and compressed air energy storage are well-suited for large-scale applications,yet their deployment is often limited by geographical constraints and substantial capital investment [12,13].For electromagnetic storage systems, the advantages of rapid response and high efficiency are offset by challenges such as high costs and moderate energy density [14]. Thermal energy storage,while providing considerable energy density,must overcome issues related to thermal losses and system complexity [15]. Similarly,hydrogen-based storage promises high energydensityand versatility butis hampered by inefficiencies in production,storage,and transport [16].These limitations have led researchers and engineers to place increasing emphasis on electrochemical energy storage systems, which offer advantages such as strong geographic adaptability,high energy density,long cycle life,lower costs,and greater efficiency,making them a key alternative solution [17-20].

Among various electrochemical energy storage technologies,RFBs stand out due to their decoupled energy and power capacities,fast response time,long cycle life,high safety,structural flexibility,and suitability for long-duration,large-scale grid storage [21-25].RFBs operate by circulating redox-active electrolytes stored in external tanks through an electrochemical cell.The key components of RFB system include electrolyte，electrode，membrane，etc.During operation, redox-active species are pumped to electrodes,where electron transfer occurs,enabling the redox process.This architecture allows energy to be stored and released on demand to meet electricity requirements [26-29]. The energy-power decoupling design in RFBs enables independent scaling of energy (via the size of storage tanks) and power (via electrode area),resulting in long cycle life,deep discharge capability, and easy maintenance [3O,31].However, the energy density of RFBs is inherently limited by the solubility of the redox active materials [29,32].

Compared with lithium-ion batteries,which currently dominate the commercial energy storage market,RFBs suffer from significantly lower energy density due to the liquid phase energy storage nature.

To enhance the energy density of RFBs,researchers have explored several strategies. One direction involves the design of multi-electron transfer systems,where a single redox-active molecule stores multiple charges [33-4O].Although this approach offers the potential to increase capacity,it often suffers from asynchronous redox kineticsand complicated reaction mechanisms,which limit practical feasibility. Another strategy aims to improve the solubility of redox-active species in liquid electrolytes [41-48].While higher concentrations can enhance charge storage,they also increase viscosity, promote molecular aggregation,and reduce long-term stability.Additionally,semi-solid RFBs suspend solid active materials in slurry electrolytes to combine solidand liquid-phase storage,but their high viscosity leads to increased flow resistance,pumping energy demand,and polarization losses,reducing overall efficiency [49-51]. In response to these fundamental limitations, RTFBs have emerged as a fundamentally distinct strategy offering a more practical and eficient route toward high-energy-density storage. This conceptis first proposed by Wang in 2oo6,introducing solid-phase energy storage materials into electrolyte storage tanks in conventional RFBs [52].Specifically,redox-active species undergo electrochemical reactions at electrode and then are pumped to external electrolyte storage tanks where they act as mediator molecules to engage in chemical reactions with solid materials.This architecture significantly enhances the system's energy storage capacity by breaking the boundary between solid-phase and liquid-phase energy storage without increasing electrolyte viscosity or system complexity [53].

# 2. Concepts and principles of redox-targeting flow batterie:

RTFBs represent a significant extension of flow battery technology, offering a promising pathway to overcome the inherent energy density limitations of conventional systems.Unlike traditional RFBs that rely solely on dissolved electroactive species,RTFBs utilize insoluble solid material in electrolyte storage tanks to enhance the energy density of RFBs,which is mediated by soluble RM. This unique design breaks the boundary between liquid-phase and solid-phase energy storage. This section aims to establish a fundamental understanding of principles and concepts of RTFBs.The discussion first introduces the fundamental concepts of RTFBs,with a multi-faceted comparison between conventional RFBs and single-molecule RTFBs,which represent the most streamlined RTFB system.Subsequently,the principles of dual-molecule and single-molecule RTFBs are elaborated,emphasizing how their reaction pathways couple electrochemical processes at the electrodes with chemical redox-targeting reactions in the reservoirs to achieve high energy density storage.

# 2.1.Fundamental concepts of redox-targeting flow bateries

RTFBs represent an important extension of conventional RFB architectures [3O,66].Their basic structure remains similar to that of traditional RFBs,consisting of an electrochemical cell,two electrolyte reservoirs,and a recirculation loop.The key innovation lies in the incorporation of solid state energy storage materials into the storage tanks [67].These solid materials do not directly contact the electrodes; instead, they store and release energy through chemical redox reactions with soluble RMs.Within this closed cycle,RM undergoes electrochemical oxidation or reduction at the electrode surface and is subsequently pumped into the reservoir,where it chemically reacts with solid material,completing an“electrochemical-chemical”coupled process [53].The driving force for redox-targeting reactions arises from the redox potential difference between RM and solid material [54].When RM's potential is more positive than solid material,it can oxidize the solid;when more negative,it can reduce it.This thermodynamic gradient determines whether interfacial charge transfer can occur spontaneously. On this basis,interfacial properties such as the accessibility of the solid surface,the chemical reaction rate,and the transport path further govern whether the RM-solid pair can form an efficient redox-targeting couple.Based on the number of RMs involved in each redox-targeting reaction,systems are classified into Dual-Molecule RTFBs and Single-Molecule RTFBs [58].Ina Dual-Molecule RTFB,two different RMs participate in redox reactions with solid material, providing sufficient thermodynamic driving force but introducing greater system complexity and voltage losses. In contrast，a Single-Molecule RTFB employs one RM to react with solid material, enabling a simpler design and potentially higher overall efficiency. These two configurational categories will be elaborated in Sections 2.2 and 2.3.

The SMRT flow battery,which represents the most streamlined and potentially eficient architecture among RTFBs,differs fundamentally from conventional RFBs in multiple aspects.In conventional RFBs, redox-active species function solely as the energy-storage medium,and the capacity is therefore limited by their concentration [32].Most redox molecules exhibit low solubility,and increasing concentration often leads to elevated viscosity,hindered mass transport,and compromised stability,resulting in intrinsically low practical energy density for liquid-phase systems.In SMRT flow batteries,these functions are redistributed: RM primarily acts as an electron shuttle between the electrodes and solid material,while energy storage is dominated by the solid phase in the reservoir,preserving the characteristic decoupling between power and capacity in RFBs [68].The introduction of redox-targeting chemistry shifts the primary energy-storage function from the liquid-phase to high-capacity solids,enabling energy densities well beyond those of conventional RFBs. Traditional systems such as all-vanadium,iron-chromium,and zinc-iron RFBs typically exhibit energy density ${ < } 4 0$ Wh $\mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 } [ 6 9 - 7 1 ]$ ,whereas SMRT flow batteries have demonstrated significantly higher energy densities [59-6l].For example,an energy density of 118.3 Wh $\mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ has been achieved when $\mathrm { L i M n } _ { 0 . 2 } \mathrm { F e } _ { 0 . 8 } \mathrm { P O } _ { 4 }$ is used as solid material [63]. The use of high-capacity solids enables substantial energy storage without requiring large electrolyte volumes,which allows for smaller reservoir sizes and more compact system design,ultimately leading to significant reductions in system footprint and tank material costs.For instance,an investment of $\$ 1.13$ in $\mathrm { L i M n } _ { 0 . 2 } \mathrm { F e } _ { 0 . 8 } \mathrm { P O } _ { 4 }$ (corresponding to $_ { 1 \ g }$ of material) can translate into an incremental capacity gain of $8 7 . 3 \mathrm { m A h }$ in Zn-Fe RTFB [63]. If solid materials with low cost and high specific capacity can be developed and employed,the overall cost of RTFBs can be further reduced.While this solid-dominated storage strategy offers clear advantages in energy density,it also introduces new kinetic considerations.Unlike conventional systems,where power density is governed solelyby electrochemical kineticsat the electrode interface,SMRTflow batteries introduce an additional chemical redox-targeting step between RMs and solid materials.Because chemical reactions are generally slower than electrochemical processes,this step may impose kinetic limitations at high current densities, while simultaneously ofering opportunities for kinetic enhancement through solid composition tuning, surface chemistry modification,and particle morphology control.

# 2.2.Principles of dual-molecule redox-targeting flow batteries

The concept of redox-targeting reactions,which enables indirect charge transfer between electronically insulating solids and solutionphase RMs,was first formalized in pioneering studies by Wang and colleagues [52].As illustrated in Fig.2a, in this process,a reduced RM (denoted as S) undergoes electrochemical oxidation at the electrode, forming an oxidized species $( S ^ { + } )$ ,which subsequently diffuses to solid material (e.g., $\mathrm { L i F e P O } _ { 4 } )$ ). If the redox potential of $s ^ { + }$ exceeds that of the solid,it can oxidize $\mathrm { L i F e P O } _ { 4 }$ to ${ \mathrm { F e P O } } _ { 4 }$ while itself being reduced back to S.The regenerated S then returns to the electrode,closing the redox cycle.Conversely,during reduction,a RMwitha lower redox potential than the solid material is used.In the presence of $\mathrm { L i ^ { + } }$ ,the reduced RM(S) diffuses to the oxidized solid $\mathrm { ( F e P O _ { 4 } ) }$ ,reducing it back to ${ \mathrm { L i F e P O } } _ { 4 }$ while being oxidized to $s ^ { + }$ This $s ^ { + }$ then diffuses back to the electrode,where it is electrochemically reduced.By employing two RMs with redox potentials bracketing that of solid material,the interfacial charge transfer in both directions can proceed with suficient thermodynamic driving force.

Building upon the fundamental concept of redox-targeting reactions discussed above,researchers began integrating this strategy into RFB architectures [53,54].This led to the development of dual-molecule RTFBs,which combine the excellent scalability of RFBs with the high energy density of solid-state materials [55]. The working principle of dual-molecule RTFBs involves electrochemical reactions occurring at the electrodes and chemical redox-targeting reactions between RMs and solid materials in the reservoirs,enabled by employing two distinct RMs $\mathrm { ( R M _ { 1 } }$ and $\mathrm { R M } _ { 2 } )$ whose standard redox potentials straddle that of solid material.Specifically, the electrochemical oxidation and reduction of $\mathrm { R M } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathrm { R M } _ { 2 }$ take place at the electrode,while their redox-targeting reactions with the solid material occur in the storage tank.The standard potentials follow the order: $\mathrm { R M _ { 1 } ~ } <$ solid material $< \mathsf { R M } _ { 2 }$ The electrochemical reactions at the electrode proceed in an electrochemical sequence, such that $\mathrm { R M _ { 1 } }$ ,which has a lower redox potential,is the first to react during charging and the last to react during discharging.In this case, $\mathrm { R M _ { 1 } }$ is oxidized first at the electrode during charging,followed by the oxidation of $\mathrm { R M } _ { 2 }$ with a higher redox potential as the electrode potential increases.During discharging，the reaction sequence is reversed: $\mathrm { R M } _ { 2 }$ is reduced first,followed by the reduction of $\mathrm { R M _ { 1 } }$ Meanwhile,RMs undergo chemical redox-targeting reactions with solid material in the electrolyte reservoirs. Taking the redox-targeting reac-tion at the positive electrode side as an example, During the charging process,the oxidized form of $\mathrm { R M } _ { 2 }$ oxidizes the solid material from its reduced state to its oxidized state,while $\mathrm { R M } _ { 2 }$ is reduced.Conversely, during discharging,the charged form of $\mathrm { R M _ { 1 } }$ reduces the oxidized solid material back to its reduced state and is simultaneously oxidized.Based on this principle,Yu et al. selected $\mathrm { F c } { \cdot } S 0 _ { 3 } \mathrm { N a }$ and $\mathbf { B r F c - S O _ { 3 } N a }$ as RMs, whose potentials span that of solid material,to construct a pH-neutral aqueous RTFB (as shown in Fig.2b),representing the state-of-the-art in dual-molecule RTFB systems [65].As illustrated in Fig.2c,the introduction of solid material significantly extends the charge-discharge plateau and enhances the releasable capacity.This design achieves a high volumetric capacity of $2 9 3 . 5 \mathrm { A h } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ ,highlighting the critical role of redox-targeting reactions in advancing the energy storage performance of dual-molecule RTFBs.

However, such dual-molecule systems still face several critical limitations.First,each half-cell requires two RMs,resulting in a more complex electrolyte composition,increased costs,and challenges related to chemical stability and compatibility. Second,the large potential difference between two RMs leads to significant voltage losses,thereby reducing the overall energy efficiency of the battery system.These issues have driven researchers to further explore strategies for optimizing mediator selection and reaction mechanisms.In 2O16,Huang et al. developed a simplified RTFB using iodine as the sole RM,leveraging its ability to undergo two redox reactions spanning the delithiation and lithiation potentials of $\mathrm { L i F e P O } _ { 4 }$ [56].Although this system ostensibly used only one mediator,it still involved multiple redox steps,failing to resolve the inherent reaction complexity and low voltage efficiency.Pan et al. improved the dual-molecule redox-targeting system by selecting $\mathrm { C o C p } _ { 2 }$ and $\mathrm { C r C p } _ { 2 }$ ,two molecules with a narrow potential difference of just $0 . 1 5 \mathrm { V }$ ,as the redox pair [57].Compared to previous systems that used mediators with larger potential diferences,such as Fc and $\mathrm { F c B r } _ { 2 }$ with $\mathrm { L i F e P O } _ { 4 }$ which caused a voltage loss of approximately $3 0 0 \mathrm { m V }$ ,or $\mathrm { C o C p } _ { 2 }$ and $\mathrm { C o C p } ^ { * } { } _ { 2 }$ with $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ which resulted in about $5 4 0 \mathrm { m V }$ of voltage loss,this configuration significantly reduced voltage loss and increased the voltage efficiency to $8 4 ~ \%$ [53,54].However, the persistence of a dual-molecule configuration indicates that issues such as complex electrolyte composition and multiple redox steps remain unresolved, constraining the advancement of overall system performance. These challenges were not fundamentally overcome until the advent of SMRT flow battery systems,which use a single RM to mediate charge transfer between electrolyte and solid material based on Nernst potential driving force [58].SMRT system significantly simplifies electrolyte composition,reduces reaction complexity,and enhances voltage efficiency. The details of this approach will be discussed in the following section.

# 2.3.Principles of single-molecule redox-targeting flow batteries

While dual-molecule RTFB systems have demonstrated the feasibility of combining soluble RMs with solid materials,they also exhibit several inherent limitations that hinder their practical implementation.In fullcell configurations,such systems typically involve four distinct RMs and four redox reactions between RM and solid material [55,57,67]. This multi-step process increases operational complexity and compromises cycling stability [68].Moreover, the large potential difference between mediators results in relatively low voltage efficiency [53,54]. To address these challenges,a simplified strategy was developed that employs only a single RM to indirectly charge and discharge solid material in the reservoir, forming the basis of single-molecule RTFBs.A schematic illustration of the operating principle of a typical single-molecule RTFB is shown in Fig.3,covering both the electrochemical reactions at the electrode and the redox-targeting reactions between RM and solid material in the reservoir.In single-molecule RTFBs,the standard redox potential of RM is closely matched with that of solid material. Rather than relying on two distinct RMs to impose afixed thermodynamic driving force,this system exploits the redox potential shift of a single RM induced by concentration changes arising from electrochemical reactions at the electrode during charge and discharge.As the RM undergoes electrochemical oxidation and reduction at the electrode,the relative concentrations of its oxidized and reduced forms varyaccordingly,leading toa dynamic redox potential evolution described by the Nernst equation (Eq.(1)),where the activities of redox species are represented by $\alpha .$ This potential shift provides the driving force for the redox-targeting reactions during charging and discharging.Taking the redox-targeting reaction on the positive side as an example,during charging,RMis electrochemically oxidized at the electrode,resulting in the accumulation of its oxidized form in the electrolyte.This compositional change shifts the RM redox potential in the positive direction,as described by the Nernst equation,allowing it to thermodynamically oxidize solid material in the reservoir.During discharging,the oxidized RMis electrochemically reduced at the electrode, decreasing the ratio of its oxidized to reduced forms and shifting its redox potential in the negative direction.This reversed potential shift enables RM to reduce solid material, completing the discharge process. Through this reversible redox potential evolution governed by the Nernst equation,the redox-targeting reactions can proceed bidirectionally, ensuring sustained and repeatable charge-discharge operation of Single-Molecule RTFBs.

Compared with dual-molecule systems, single-molecule RTFBs preserve the core advantage of redox-targeting chemistry，namely, enhancing the volumetric capacity of RFBs through the introduction of solid-state energy storage.Meanwhile,the use of a single RM significantly simplifies the electrolyte composition,thereby reducing the likelihood of side reactions.In addition,this strategy effectively mitigates the substantial voltage losses commonly observed in dualmolecule RTFBs arising from large potential gaps between RMs,resulting in a marked improvement in voltage efficiency.Following the general description of single-molecule RTFB principles,the first singlemolecule RTFB system proposed by Zhou et al. serves as a representative example for illustration [58].By employing only one RM with a redox potential precisely matched to that of the solid material,the system achieved a voltage eficiency as_high as $9 5 \ \%$ along with a volumetric energy density of 330 Wh $\mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ .These results highlight the superior efficiency and scalability of single-molecule RTFB as an energy storage strategy.As illustrated in Fig.2d, the FcIL exhibits an identical standard redox potential to that of $\mathrm { L i F e P O } _ { 4 }$ . According to Nernst equation,during charging and discharging,FcIL undergoes reversible electrochemical oxidation and reduction at the electrode surface,and its potential dynamically shifts with the activities of the oxidized and reduced species.This concentration-dependent potential shift establishes the thermodynamic driving force required for the redox-targeting reaction between FcIL and $\mathrm { L i F e P O } _ { 4 }$ .Fig.2e provides direct mechanistic evidence through a dual-layer electrode configuration in which an $\mathrm { { A l } _ { 2 } \mathrm { { O } _ { 3 } } }$ insulating layer blocks direct electron transfer,while the mobile FcIL mediator shuttles between the electrode and solid material.Cyclic voltammetry measurements (green curve) show pronounced redox peaks, with much higher current response than in the control groups lacking either solid material (orange) or FcIL (gray).After successfully realizing the SMRT reaction on the positive side of the flow battery,Yu et al. extended this concept to the full-cell configuration by simultaneously applying the SMRT strategy to both the positive and negative sides [72]. Specifically, $\mathrm { L i F e P O } _ { 4 }$ and $\mathrm { L i T i _ { 2 } ( P O _ { 4 } ) _ { 3 } }$ were employed as the solid materials for the positive and negative sides,respectively.RMs with redox potentials matched to those of the solid materials were selected to enable the SMRT reactions,enabling the successful construction of a full Single-Molecule RTFB,as illustrated in Fig.2f.This system achieved high volumetric capacities of $3 0 5 \mathrm { A h } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ for the positive and $2 0 7 \mathrm { A h } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ for the negative,meanwhile demonstrating excellent cycling stability with $9 9 . 1 \ \%$ capacity retention after 55 cycles.Subsequent studies have continued to advance the development of SMRT flow batteries,with a variety of novel redox-targeting systems being reported.These developments will be systematically discussed in Sections 3 and 4.

# 3. Non-Aqueous single-molecule redox-targeting flow batterie!

As one of the fundamental components of RFBs,the electrolyte has been extensively developed in both aqueous and non-aqueous systems. Aqueous and non-aqueous RFBs exhibit distinct advantages in terms of electrochemical performance,material selection,and safety.Aqueous RFBsare generally regarded as safer and more stable,like all-vanadium RFBs having reached a mature stage of commercial deployment. Meanwhile,non-aqueous RFBs have garnered significant attention due to their enhanced electrochemical windows and energy densities,utilizing organic solvent as electrolyte.Specifically，systems such as lithium-ion flow batteries have,by synergistically combining metal ion chemistrywith flow batteryarchitecture,aiming to achieve desirable operating voltages and energy storage capacities for grid-scale applications [51,77].

EarlyRTFB systems typically employed two distinct RMs paired with appropriate solid materials to facilitate bidirectional redox-targeting reactions[54],further research revealed that dual-molecule systems often suffer from substantial voltage losses due to the large potential differences between the two mediators.The SMRT system simplifies the system by utilizing a single RM with a redox potential matching that of the solid material, thereby enhancing efficiency.

# 3.2. Non-Aqueous lithium-oxygen single-molecule redox-targeting batteries

Redox-targeting reactions have been successfully applied in metalair batteries,which use metals as the negative electrode and oxygen from the air as the positive active materials [79,8O],offering high energy density,low cost,and environmental benefits.However,challenges such as electrode deformation and limited energy storage and conversion capabilities due to hydrogen evolution reactions have been encountered. Taking Lithium-oxygen flow batteries as representatives, recent advances in lithium-oxygen batteries show that their performance is fundamentally limited by two major challenges.First, the cathodic discharge product $\mathrm { L i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ is electrically insulating，which severely impedes interfacial electron transport and ion diffusion. Second,oxygen-derived radical intermediates at the cathode trigger multiple parasitic reactions,generating undesirable species such as $\mathrm { L i _ { 2 } C O _ { 3 } }$ and LiOH that negatively affect long-term stability.Compared with costly catalytic additives,a series of studies have demonstrated that incorporating redox-targeting reactions can effectively improve reaction kinetics and energy efficiency.In such systems,RMs function by reducing $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ and oxidizing $\mathrm { L i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ at potentials near $2 . 9 6 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ vs. $\mathrm { L i / L i ^ { + } }$ During discharge,RM is reduced prior to $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ and subsequently chemically reduces $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ to form $\mathrm { L i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 } ;$ during charge,RMis electrochemically oxidized at the cathode and then chemically oxidizes $\mathrm { L i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ back to $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ Therefore,the mediators must possess high solubility in the solvent and good electrochemical reversibility over repeated cycling. Tetrathiafulvalene (TTF）is a representative RM,acting as an electron-hole transfer agent [81].During charging,TTF is oxidized to $\mathrm { T T F ^ { + } }$ ，which further oxidizes $\mathrm { L i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ to release $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ .Importantly, TTF circumvents the intrinsic insulating nature of $\mathrm { L i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ without storing charge directly. Similarly, Under ideal redox-targeting potential matching conditions (Fig.5b),in low-polarity solvent (DMsO),2,5-di-tert-butyl-1,4-benzoquinone (DBBQ) promotes the solution-phase formation of $\mathrm { L i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ away from the electrode surface,[82] thus mitigating cathode passivation. However,these approaches do not fundamentally address the issue of parasitic reactions.Multifunctional molecular mediators offer a more ideal RM design. One example is a hybrid molecule constructed by anchoringtworedox-active2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-1-piperidinyloxy (TEMPO) units onto a quenching-active perylene diimide (PDI) backbone.These functional units are covalently connected, enabling them to operate synergistically without mutual interference: the PDI moiety acts as a quenching mediator to suppress oxygen-radical-induced side reactions,while the TEMPO groups serve as reversible redox mediators [83],as schematic shown in Fig.5a. Uniquely,this mediator does not rely on traditional redox potential matching; instead,it directly reacts with oxygen radicals and $\mathrm { L i } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ ，transforming the solid interfacial pathway into a more stable and controllable liquid-phase-dominated process. The resulting performance improvement is remarkable, yielding a discharge capacity 46 times higher than that of the control group.Redox-targeting strategies have also been extended to other metal-oxygen systems such as sodium-oxygen batteries [84]．Researchers have incorporated redox mediators including sodium iodide, ferrocene,and ethyl viologen into the electrolyte，successfully enhancing the cycling stability and overall electrochemical performance of these batteries.These advances underscore the versatility of redox-targeting chemistry in metal-air systems,offering a promising pathway to overcome kinetic and morphological limitations and to advance the practical realization of high-energy,long-lifetime metal-air flow batteries.Despite the promising role of RMs in improving discharge-charge efficiency and mitigating electrode passivation in metal-airsystems, onecritical challengeremainsunresolved. Mediator-induced reaction pathways often alter the stability window of the electrolyte and promote decomposition of solvent molecules or oxygen intermediates.Addressing these issues will require developing mediators with higher redox selectivity to suppress the formation of undesired by-products,constructing protective interphases.Further exploration will be essential for translating redox-mediated metal-air batteries from lab-scale demonstrations toward practical energy storage.

# 4. Aqueous single-molecule redox-targeting flow batteries

Compared to non-aqueous systems,aqueous SMRT flow batteries offer obvious advantages in safety,cost control,and environmental compatibility.Although non-aqueous SMRT batteries benefit from a broader electrochemical stability window, their practical deployment is hindered by several intrinsic limitations,including high electrolyte costs,low dielectric constants,reduced ion conductivity of commonly used ion-exchange membranes,as well as safety concerns arising from flammability and toxicity.As a result, research efforts have increasingly shifted toward aqueous systems,which provide higher ionic conductivity,improved thermal safety,and enhanced environmental sustainability,making them promising candidates for next-generation energy storage.Based on the chemical nature of RM and solid material,current aqueous SMRT batteries can be broadly classfied into three categories: organic,inorganic,and hybrid organic-inorganic systems.This section provides a comprehensive review of these three systems,focusing on theirredox reaction mechanisms,interfacial charge-transfer characteristics，and recent advances in material design and performance optimization.

# 4.1.Aqueous organic single-molecule redox-targeting flow batteries

Aqueous organic SMRT flow batteries have attracted significant attention due to their tunable molecular structures,low cost,and synthetic versatility.Through rational functional group modification, the solubility,redox potential,and electrochemical stability of organic molecules can be systematically tailored,making them highlyadaptable for SMRT applications.Notably,systems where RMand solid material share similar molecular structures often benefit from simplified electron-transfer pathways and accelerated redox kinetics. One representative example is the AQDS-PI system developed by Zhou etal. [61]. While AQDS offers low cost and fast kinetics,its poor solubility in neutral electrolytes has hindered broader application [91]. This limitation was addressed by pairing it with polyimide (PI)，whose wel-matched redox potential enabled stable SMRT operation under neutral-buffered conditions. The hydrogen-bond-mediated charge transfer mechanism of this AQDS-PI system under neutral pH is illustrated in Fig.6a. Cyclic voltammetry (Fig.6b) further confirms the excellent redox potential alignment between AQDS and PI,which, together with their structural similarity，facilitates rapid electron transfer.As a result of this compatibility,the aqueous organic SMRT battery achieved an eight-fold increase in capacity,reaching a solid material utilization rate of $8 3 ~ \%$ and a discharge capacity of 97 Ah $\mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ ， demonstrating promising practical potential. A similar strategy, leveraging structural similarity and redox potential matching,was later applied to radical-functionalized polymers by Schroter et al.,using TMATEMPO and PTMA[92].This system is based on the TEMPO radicals,where RM and solid material share an identical molecular backbone and electronic structure,exhibit similar redox potentials,and are readily synthesized via scalable and well-established procedures [93, 94].As shown in Fig. 6c,the introduction of PTMA led to a sharp increase in discharge capacity from approximately $3 . 7 \mathrm { m A h }$ to $1 1 . 4 ~ \mathrm { { m A h } }$ ， corresponding to a solid material utilization rate of about $7 8 \%$ .Meanwhile,the battery maintains a consistently high coulombic efficiency, confirmingthefavorablereversibilityandstabilityofthe redox-targeting process. Fig. 6d further supports the redox-targeting mechanism:PTMA introduction stabilized the voltage plateau and extended the discharge duration across multiple cycles,confirming the sustained electrochemical contribution of solid material.

A key challenge in SMRT systems is achieving precise redox potential matching between RM and solid material,as naturally matched redox pairs are rare [95,96].Most studies still rely on empirical RM-solid material selection,which is inefficient and poorly scalable.To address this,Rong et al. proposed a DFT-based strategy to rationally design SMRT systems by predicting redox potentials [64]. They systematically screened AQDs derivatives and constructed a system using poly (N-anthraquinoyl pyrrole) (PAQPy)as solid material and1,8-dihydroxyanthraquinone-2,7-disulfonic acid (1,8-DHAQDS) as RM.Fig.6e shows the calculated half-wave potentials of various AQDS derivatives. By combining Gibbs free energy，electrostatic potential (ESP)，and HOMO-LUMO gap analyses,1,8-DHAQDS was identified as the optimal match for PAQPy under $\mathrm { N H } _ { 4 } ^ { + }$ conditions.Fig.6f presents the cyclic voltammetry of DHAQDS and PAQPy,confirming the redox potential alignment through overlapping peaks.Based on this pairing，the resulting SMRT battery achieved significantly enhanced performance. As shown in Fig.6g，the introduction of PAQPy extended the charge-discharge duration and increased the volumetric capacity from $5 6 2 . 6 2 \ \mathrm { m A h } \ \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ to $2 1 6 9 . 3 2 \ \mathrm { m A h } \ \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ ,a 3.86-fold improvement.The system also demonstrated excellent cycling stability.This represents a competitive performance among reported aqueous organic SMRT Flow Batteries [97].This study provides not only a high-capacity SMRT configuration but also a theoretical framework for systematically identifying well-matched RM-solid material pairs, thereby advancing aqueous organic SMRT technology toward a more efficient and predictable stage of development.

Their study revealed the critical role of KOH concentration in modulating the driving force for charge transfer.This design effectively overcame the limited solubility of $\mathrm { [ F e ( C N ) _ { 6 } ] ^ { 3 - / 4 - } }$ in alkaline electrolytes and increased the catholyte volumetric capacity to $2 9 \mathrm { A h } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ .Paéz and Zhang extended this concept into a more complex battery architecture [62].Although the negative side design employed a dual-molecule system (beyond the scope of this section),the negative side retained the SMRT structure based on inorganic aqueous chemistry.This integration successfully combined a nickel-metal hydride battery with an aqueous RTFB,achieving an impressive energy density of $1 2 8 \mathrm { W h } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ .The design demonstrates the compatibility and integration potential of $\mathrm { N i ( O H ) _ { 2 } }$ -based SMRT reactions within hybrid system architectures.This study further broadens the application scope of inorganic SMRT technology in practical energy storage systems and provides a viable pathway for developing hybridized energy storage strategies in the future.

To date,most SMRT systems have focused primarily on selecting naturally matched redox pairs or adjusting mediator potentials via solvent engineering to achieve solid-liquid redox potential alignment [67]. However, these approaches often overlook the intrinsic tunability of solid materials themselves,which play an equally crucial role in interfacial reaction kinetics and electrochemical reversibility.Building upon previous emphasis on electrolyte engineering and solid-liquid synergy, Cai et al.proposed a novel design strategy based on compositional tuningof $\mathrm { L i M n _ { x } F e _ { 1 - x } P O _ { 4 } }$ to align the redox potential with [Fe $\left( \mathrm { C N } \right) _ { 6 } ] ^ { 3 - / 4 - }$ ,marking a shift from passive material selection to active interfacial regulation [63].As shown in Fig.7e,increasing Mn content raised the redox potential from $0 . 2 9 1 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ to $0 . 3 9 0 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ (vs.Ag/AgCl), demonstrating tunability through solid composition. $\mathrm { L i M n } _ { 0 . 2 } \mathrm { F e } _ { 0 . 8 } \mathrm { P O } _ { 4 }$ exhibited the highest peak current and optimal electrochemical performance,and its redox potential (O.333 V) closely matched that of [Fe $( \mathrm { C N } ) _ { 6 } ] ^ { 3 - / 4 - } ( 0 . 3 3 7 \ : \mathrm { V } )$ .Fig. 7f shows that moderate Mn doping enhanced Li+ diffusion,whereas excessive Mn induced lattice distortion,reducing electrochemical activity and impairing the SMRT reaction [113-115]. When paired with a zinc negative electrode,the full cell achieved an energy density of $1 1 8 . 3 \mathrm { W h } \mathrm { L } ^ { - 1 }$ ,5.6 times higher than that of the system without a solid component,with a capacity retention of $9 9 . 8 ~ \%$ per cycle,demonstrating excellent reversibility and interfacial stability.This work highlights the value of potential tuning in optimizing interfacial kinetics and long-term performance in aqueous inorganic SMRT systems.

Although aqueous inorganic SMRT flow batteries offer excellent electrochemical stability,low cost,and strong structural robustness, several critical challenges remain for practical implementation. One major constraint lies in the solubility limits of commonly used inorganic redoxmediators such as ferrocyanides and sulfides under specific pH or ionic conditions,which reduces the flexibility in electrolyte design.In addition,many inorganic solid materials rely on ion insertion and extraction mechanisms for energy storage,and the resulting solid-state ion diffusion can become the rate-limiting step.Under high current densities,the sluggish transport of ions within the solid phase may significantly hinder redox-targeting kinetics,leading to increased polarization and deteriorated power performance.Another key issue is the interfacial stability of inorganic solids in aqueous environments. Without careful control over surface chemistry and lattice integrity, surface reconstruction or dissolution of active metal species may occur, compromising cycling stability and Coulombic efficiency.To address these limitations,future efforts should emphasize electrolyte optimization,interface engineering,and compositional tuning of solid materials to enhance the scalability and long-term durability of aqueous inorganic SMRT flow batteries.

# 4.3.Aqueous hybrid single-molecule redox-targeting flow batterie:

Since SMRT systems often encounter difficulties in identifying RM and solid material with well-matched potentials within either an allorganic or all-inorganic component domain[67],in addition to the aforementioned fully organic or fully inorganic SMRT systems, some studies have also developed hybrid redox-targeting configurations composed of both organic and inorganic components as an alternative pathway to realize the SMRT mechanism.These hybrid systems integrate the structural stability and conductivity of inorganic materials with the tunable molecular properties of organic compounds [116], providing new strategic directions for regulating interfacial charge transfer kinetics and enhancing electrochemical performance.Researchers have conducted exploratory studies around such systems, demonstrating the feasibility of redox-targeting reactions between organic and inorganic species in aqueous environments, thus offering valuable insights for the diversified construction of aqueous SMRT systems.Against this backdrop, Zanzola et al. proposed two representative hybrid strategies: one coupling an inorganic RM with an organic solid material,and the other combining an organic RM with an inorganic solid material,thereby validating the practical feasibility of hybrid architectures in aqueous SMRT applications [117,118].For the first configuration,Zanzola et al. focused on polyaniline (PANI) as solid material [117].Given the three redox states of polyaniline (PANI),this study evaluated the redox matching of Fe(III/I) and V(IV/III) mediators [119]. Using the setup in Fig.8a，Fe(Il/II) enabled stable redox-targeting of PANI with an average capacity of $4 5 . 7 \ : \mathrm { m A h } \ : \mathrm { g } ^ { - 1 }$ over 25 cycles,while V(IV/II) showed inferior kinetics.To address the poor conductivity and capacity fading of pristine PANI in full cells,a PANI/carbon-black composite was introduced.As shown in Fig.8b, the composite reduced overpotentials and extended the voltage-capacity profile,reflecting improved voltage and energy efficiencies as well as buffering of electrolyte charge fluctuations.For the second configuration,the researchers constructed an aqueous SMRT flow battery by coupling the water-soluble organic radical TEMPTMA with the Prussian-blue analogue CuHCF [118].As illustrated in Figs.8c-d, the SMRT mechanism leverages the matched redox potentials of the two components,with Fe redox transitions in CuHCF accompanied by $\mathrm { K } ^ { + }$ intercalation/deintercalation.As shown in Figs.8e-f,compositing with carbon nanotubes significantly enhanced the electrochemical activity of CuHCF,enabling efficient redox-targeting electron transfer mediated by TEMPTMA. The resulting hybrid positive exhibited improved capacity and redox kinetics,achieving a volumetric capacity of $3 5 0 \mathrm { C } \mathrm { m L } ^ { - 1 }$ and a coulombic efficiency of approximately $9 5 ~ \%$ when paired with a zinc negative electrode. These results highlight the critical role of conductive framework design and interfacial charge-transfer synergy in hybrid SMRT systems.

Following the strategy of integrating organic RMs with inorganic solid materials,hybrid SMRT strategies have been successfully extended to high-energy-density metal-air systems,demonstrating their potential for scalable power generation.A representative example is the hybrid redox-mediated zinc-air fuel cell developed by Song et al.,which delivers a peak power density of $0 . 5 1 ~ \mathrm { W ~ c m } ^ { - 2 }$ and a discharge capacity of 48.83Ah [12O].In this system,an organic phenazine derivative,7, 8-dihydroxyphenazine-2-sulfonic acid (DHPS),is employed as the sole RM.DHPS shuttles between the electrode and the fuel reservoir, undergoing redox-targeting reactions with metallic Zn stored in the reservoir. Compared with conventional zinc-air batteries in which Zn is directly loaded onto the electrode,this design effectively mitigates electrode passivation and limited depth of discharge,while confining ZnO byproducts to the energy-storage reservoir，thereby avoiding blockage of the electrochemical cell.

Although aqueous hybrid organic-inorganic SMRT flow batteries expand the material design space for single-molecule RTFB systems, they still face several inherent limitations.A central challenge lies in the interfacial charge-transfer mismatch between organic species and inorganic species.Due to differences in electronic structure,ionic coordination environment,and surface chemical properties,the redoxtargeting reactions often proceed with sluggish kinetics,leading to increased polarization and low utilization of solid materials.In addition, the long-term chemical compatibility between organic molecules and inorganic solids in aqueous electrolytes remains poorly understood. Over extended cycling,species degradation, surface passivation of the solid material,or irreversible interfacial side reactions may occur.These issues collectively limit the rate performance,cycling stability,and scalability of aqueous hybrid SMRT systems,highlighting the need for further research on interfacial engineering and stability enhancement.

The preceding sections have highlighted the major advances achieved in both nonaqueous and aqueous SMRT flow batteries. Nevertheless,a number of SMRT systems still suffer from performance degradation or relatively low energy eficiency during long term operation.This behavior arises from the combined effects of multiple factors, with the fundamental origin lying in the transition from the single step electrochemical reaction in conventional RFBs to the more complex two step process in RTFBs that involves electrode reactions coupled with redox-targeting reactions occurring in the electrolyte reservoir.As a consequence， the intrinsically slower heterogeneous redox-targeting reaction in the reservoir becomes a key bottleneck governing the overall system performance.A core and widely encountered issue is the inherent kinetic limitation of the heterogeneous redox-targeting reaction. Regardless of whether the process takes place in organic electrolytes or aqueous media,electron transfer between dissolved redox mediators and solid energy storage materials is commonly constrained by the low electronic conductivity of the solid phase, such as ${ \mathrm { L i F e P O } } _ { 4 }$ PBAs,and polyimides,together with the sluggish ion diffusion of charge compensating ions including $\mathrm { L i ^ { + } }$ ， $\mathrm { N a ^ { + } }$ ,and $\mathrm { H } ^ { + }$ .This leads to chemical targeting reaction rates in the reservoir that are significantly lower than the electrochemical reaction rates at the electrode interface.The resulting kinetic mismatch first manifests as an increased requirement for activation overpotential to sustain continuous charge transfer,which directly lowers voltage efficiency and limits achievable power output.At the same time,incomplete conversion between solid materials and redox mediators within a single charge discharge cycle is frequently observed, giving rise to insufficient utilization of solid phase capacity even in the absence of pronounced structural degradation. With continued cycling, incompletely reacted solid regions progressively accumulate and evolve into chemically isolated domains,thereby further aggravating polarization and accelerating performance decay. It should be noted that such chemical isolation is typically induced by multiple factors that develop gradually during operation, including particle aggregation or partial detachment under cycling and flow conditions,the formation of surface passivation layers,and interfacial side reactions between the solid phase and the electrolyte that hinder electronic or ionic transport.As a result, these regions gradually lose effective contact with redox mediators during practical operation,leading to irreversible loss of accessible capacity.On this basis,insufficient chemical and structural stability of redox mediators and solid storage materials during battery cycling further amplifies the aforementioned limitations. Dissolved RMs may undergo slow yet cumulative chemical degradation, side reactions, or solubility changes during repeated redox conversion，resulting in decreased effective mediator concentration and reduced reaction reversibility,while solid materials subjected to sustained targeting reactions and repeated ion insertion and extraction may experience lattice strain accumulation,local structural rearrangement,or interfacial chemical evolution. These processes weaken the reaction matching betweenredox mediators and solid materials and further intensify system polarization.Meanwhile,the design and operating characteristics of the electrolyte reservoir also exert a significant influence on system performance.Nonuniform flow distribution, local dead zones,and inappropriate packing or stacking of solid particles can restrict effective mass transport of redox mediators to solid surfaces,rendering part of the solid material inaccessible for redox-targeting reactions within a given charge discharge cycle.The resulting spatial nonuniformity of reactions further reduces overall solid material utilization and forces the system to operate under higher polarization conditions,ultimately manifesting as cumulative capacity loss,declining voltage efficiency,and limited energy efficiency during prolonged cycling.

# 5.Characterization techniques for redox-targeting reaction mechanism study

The advancement of RTFB performance is intrinsically linked to a comprehensive understanding of its energy storage mechanisms and the evolutionary behavior of materials.Utilizing advanced characterization techniques,researchers can precisely elucidate the structural and compositional evolution of key components,during charge-discharge processes.This provides critical theoretical insights for the optimization and design of essential materials,helping people better study the mechanism of battery reactions [12l-l25].In order to monitor the evolution of RM and solid material in SMRT reactions,researchers have employed various in-situ and ex-situ characterization techniques.

# 5.1．In-situ characterizations

In-situ characterization techniques allow for the real-time monitoring of the composition, structure,or state of materials in RTFB systems without disrupting their operation [126,l27].These techniques have become indispensable tools for elucidating the dynamic processes in SMRT flow batteries.By coupling real-time spectroscopic methods with electrochemical measurements,researchers can directly monitor redox reactions,interfacial charge transfer,and the evolution of active species under operating conditions.The integration of spectroscopic and diffraction-based in-situ methods has profoundly expanded the understanding of redox processes in flow batteries.While XAS resolves local electronic and coordination structures, Raman and UV-Vis spectroscopy reveal dynamic changes in molecular vibrations and chromophore states.Complementary synchrotron XRD measurements trace crystalline and amorphous phase transformations under operating conditions.This convergence of techniques provides a comprehensive mechanistic picture spanning electronic, molecular, and structural dimensions, laying the groundwork for performance-driven material design.These techniques provide time-resolved insight of SMRT systems and,more importantly,the solid-liquid interfacial kinetics that directly determine the reaction rate.In the system where Mn-doped LFP is paired with the [Fe(CN)6]3-/4- redox couple, the reaction mechanism was clarified through Fe and Mn K-edge XANES/EXAFS analysis. $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 2 + }$ in their SMRT system undergoes oxidation to $\mathrm { F e } ^ { 3 + }$ during charging and is reversibly reduced during discharging,whereas Mn acts as a spectator throughout the process (Fig.9a, 9b) [63]. This result pinpointed Fe as the true redox center;subsequent ex-situ XPS of the Fe 2p region further validated these reversible valence changes.Complementary operando Fe K-edge XANES further traced the transformation between $\mathrm { L i F e P O } _ { 4 }$ and FePO4(Fig.9c),confirming edge shifts associated with Fe oxidation and subtle spectral shortening at high voltage that signaled mediator degradation [74].

Operando kinetic measurements consistently show that the SMRT reaction rate is governed not by bulk mediator diffusion but by the solid-liquid interfacial electron-transfer step.This highlights that optimizing surface accessibility,electronic pathways,and mediator-surface affinity is essential for accelerating SMRT kinetics and improving practical power density.

# 5.2.Ex-situ characterizations

Ex-situ diffraction and spectroscopic methods deliver high-precision insights into phase transformations and chemical states once cycling is paused.For instance,Pan et al.employed XPS to confirm the emergence of $\mathrm { T i } ^ { 3 + }$ in $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ following lithiation,which was evidenced by a $2 . 0 \ \mathrm { e V }$ shift in binding energy [54].In parallel, Raman spectroscopy revealed a structural transitionfromanatase,characterized bythe $\overline { { 1 4 3 } } \mathrm { ~ c m } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { ~ E _ { g } ~ }$ mode,to orthorhombic $\mathrm { L i } _ { 0 . 2 7 } \mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ . (Fig.10b).Similarly，combining ex-situ XRD and XPS on both electrode materials in NVP cells to pinpoint the origin of capacity expansion, revealing a downward shift in the $\mathtt { V 2 p }$ peak at the negative electrode consistent with chemical sodiation of FL (Fig.10a) [78]. High-resolution imaging and elemental mapping techniques then clarify how these structural and chemical changes manifest in particle morphology and composition. SEM-EDX mapping confirmed that Mn and Fe remain uniformly distributed in $\mathrm { L i M n } _ { 0 . 2 } \mathrm { F e } _ { 0 . 8 } \mathrm { P O } _ { 4 }$ even after extended cycling,ruling out element segregation as the cause of capacity fade [63].TEM shows the enduring integrity of its carbon coating layer.In all-organic system,SEM comparison before and after long-term cycling revealed polymer expansion and pore collapse in PTMA composites [92] (Fig.1Oc),linking morphological degradation to hindered ion transport Fig.1Od shows FTIR spectra of NiHCF before and after interaction with $\mathrm { N a I } / \mathrm { N a I } _ { 3 }$ ，where the emergence and disappearance of the Fe-CN stretching band at $2 1 6 6 ~ \mathrm { { c m } ^ { - 1 } }$ directly evidences the reversible SMRT reaction. Together,these ex-situ techniques corroborate the structural and electronic changes in NiHCF,validating its role as a solid-state capacity booster in the SMRT process.Collectively,these ex-situ diffraction，imaging，and spectroscopic results converge to validate the structural and electronic evolution of NiHCF and related electrodes, confirming their effectiveness as solid-state capacity boosters in SMRT systems.To investigate interfacial reaction rates as deeplyas possible, early-stage studies usually use electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS)and cyclic voltammetry (CV) to directly reflect parameters such as the interfacial resistance and diffusion coefficients of the battery materials themselves.Although most ex-situ techniques do not directly quantify interfacial kinetics,the observed morphological degradation and surface-state evolution strongly suggest secondary impacts on solid-liquid charge-transfer rates,which ultimately manifest in capacity decay and power loss.Chen et al.pointed out that sterics is one of the important factors affecting interfacial reaction rates [l28],and systems with higher reaction rates are more likely to achieve lower charging voltages,smaller overpotentials,and fewer side reactions.In addition to using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and cyclic voltammetry (CV)in the early stage to directly reflect the interfacial resistance,diffusion coeficients,and other parameters of the battery materials themselves,ex-situ evidence such as SEM revealing pore collapse and polymer swelling in PTMA that lead to a decrease in effective surface area provides proof of interfacial kinetic decay [92], and TEM characterization of carbon coating is also conducted to ensure interfacial electronic conductivity and reduce charge-transfer resistance.Ex-situ observations provide indirect but essential evidence for understanding long-term kinetic degradation. To further explore the reaction kinetics at the solid-liquid interface during the SMRT process, Scanning Electrochemical Microscopy (SECM) can be used to quantitatively characterize kinetic parameters such as electron-transfer rates and exchange current density,and techniques such as the Electrochemical Quartz Crystal Microbalance (EQCM),which monitors in real time the correlation between the corresponding current and the mass change on the material surface,can also be employed to support the development of more high-performance SMRT systems.

# 6.Conclusion and outlook

Redox-targeting flow batteries have emerged as promising system for high-energy-density redox flow batteries by combining liquid and solid energy storage mode.This article comprehensively reviews SMRT flow batteries, focusing on recent advances and developments. Both aqueous and non-aqueous SMRT flow batteries are systematically discussed in terms of matching between solid material and RM,electrochemical property and battery performance.Inorganic,organic and hybrid SMRT systems are also well analyzed.We further review various ex-situ and insitu characterizations to elucidate SMRT reaction mechanism, providing essential insights for optimizing system design,and enhancing battery performance.Although significant progress has been made in SMRT flow batteries,several key challenges remain unresolved.The following outlines the main challenges and possible solutions of SMRT flow batteries,presenting plenty of opportunities for further exploration and advancements.

# (1） Strategies for selecting solid materials and redox mediators

The selection methods of matchable solid materials and redox mediators require further optimization.Most reported SMRT flow batteries choose solid materials and redox mediators with matched redox potentials based on experience,existing randomness and trial-and-error process.The primary challenge lies in the precise matching of solid material-RM pair.A multi-stage screening strategy is proposed.First, computational screening using density functional theory (DFT） can predict key properties such as redox potentials,solubility,and thermodynamic stability,narrowing down candidate pools from material databases. Second,high-throughput experimental methods should be employed to rapidly test the electrochemical behavior of candidate pairs (solid materials and RMs). This combined computational and experimental workflow can systematically identify the most promising SMRT systems.

In future SMRT systems,several classes of solid materials and mediators are poised to offer substantial performance gains.Twodimensional conductive MOFs/COFs,featuring continuous $\pi { - } \mathrm { d }$ transport pathways and exceptionally high surface areas,represent ideal candidates for maximizing solid-liquid interfacial contactand accelerating electron transport. Conductive polymer-based hybrid frameworks (for example,synthesizing conductive polymers with redoxactive molecular side chains） combine flexible multivalent redox chemistry with tunable doping,enabling highly reversible charge storage and strong interfacial affinity in near-neutral aqueous media. Organic redox solids,including polyimides and quinone-based polymers,provide multi-electron transfer capability and intrinsic chemical stability while circumventing the dissolution issues that limit conventional organic active materials.Meanwhile,defect-engineered metal oxides such as vacancy-rich $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ ）offerabundant surface defect sites that facilitate rapid interfacial charge transfer, making them promising kinetic enhancers for SMRT applications.As for RMs,multi-electron redox mediators are expected to dramatically increase electron throughput per cycle, thereby improving both the rate capability and energy efficiency of SMRT reactions.Surface-afinitive mediators, capable of selective adsorption onto solid interfaces,present a complementary strategy for lowering interfacial activation barriers and enhancing charge-transfer kinetics.More advanced SMRT systems are likely to evolve around high-voltage aqueous redox flow batteries and multi-step,multi-electron redox couples,in which rapid multi-electron targeting reactions can be efectively coupled with high-power battery operation. Collectively,these emerging materials and system designs highlight a broad and versatile landscape for next-generation SMRT technologies.

(2)Regulation of solid-liquid interface reactions and kinetics enhancement

The key of SMRT reaction is the complex heterogeneous process at the solid-liquid interface in electrolyte storage tank,which often sufers from high energy barriers and sluggish kinetics,leading to significant voltage polarization and poor rate capability.The challenge is to understand and control the interfacial reaction pathway.To enhance the solid-liquid reaction interface,advanced interface engineering is required. One key strategy is surface modification of the solid materials. This can be achieved by coating them with a thin layer of conductive carbon or by creating nanostructured composites to enhance electronic conductivity and increase the active surface area for the RMs to react. This can also enhance the utilization of solid materials.Another approach involves molecular engineering of the RMs to tailor their adsorption energy on the solid surface,thereby facilitating the electron transfer step.Furthermore,employing advanced in-situ characterization techniques,such as electrochemical quartz crystal microbalance (EQCM) or Raman spectroscopy,is vital to observe the dynamic oxidation and reduction of the solid materials in real-time,providing direct insights for designing interfaces with fast kinetics and high stability.

(3) Reactor design in electrolyte storage tank

Unlike conventional flow batteries,SMRT systems must manage a solid-liquid interface in the electrolyte storage tank, introducing unique challenges for tank design.A major issue is the insufficient contact between solid materials and redox mediators.The proposed solution involves an integrated multiscale reactor design.At the macro-scale,the tank's geometry and the placement of flow distributors must be optimized, potentially using computational fluid dynamics (CFD),to ensure the electrolyte permeates the entire solid bed uniformly,avoiding dead zones.At the micro-scale,the internal architecture of the solid materials, as mentioned previously,must be engineered as a porous monolith or a packed bed with controlled porosity to facilitate mass transfer while maintaining good electronic contact. Concepts from chemical reactor design,such as fixed-bed or trickle-bed reactors,can be highly instructive.The ultimate goal is to create a tank where the solid is thoroughly connected and the liquid electrolyte is evenly distributed,enabling efficient and scalable solid-liquid contact for SMRT reaction process.

(4) Establishing evaluation system for SMRT flow batteries

The distinctive design of SMRT flow batteries,with its static solid energy storage and redox mediators,necessitates a bespoke set of evaluation metrics beyond those used for conventional redox flow batteries.Standard metrics like cycle life and efficiency are insufficient to capture the unique challenges and advantages.A new evaluation framework is supposed to be established,featuring metrics such as solid utilization (the percentage of the solid material actually reacted over a full cycle),redox-targeting round-trip eficiency (quantifying the efficiency loss specifically in the charge shuttling process between the electrode and the solid),reactor tank areal capacity (the total capacity stored per geometric area of the tank's cross-section),and volumetric energy density (including both the solid and electrolyte).Furthermore, specific durability tests need to be developed to assess the stability of the solid-liquid interface over long cycles.This comprehensive evaluation system is crucial for guiding R&D, benchmarking different SMRT systems,and demonstrating their practical value for large-scale, stationary energy storage.
