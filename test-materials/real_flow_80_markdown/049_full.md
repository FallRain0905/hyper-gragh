ABSTRACT: Phenylethylammonium hydrohalide-based passivation layers mitigate trap-asisted recombination in perovskite solar cells by neutralizing defect states and optimizing band bending. However, these layers sufer from thermal instability due to ammonium salt decomposition and bulk migration. To address this，a thermally stable phenylethyl-grafted guanidinium $( \mathrm { P E G } \bar { \mathbf { A } } ^ { + } )$ interfacial layer is introduced. Its delocalized charge distribution， low adsorption energy， and hydrogen-bonding network prevent bulk migration, enhancing stability at elevated temperatures. $\mathrm { P E G A ^ { + } }$ -modified perovskite solar cells $\left( 1 . 5 5 ~ \mathrm { e V } \right)$ achieved a power conversion efficiency (PCE） exceeding $2 6 . 2 \%$ ，retaining over $9 6 \%$ of their maximum power after $1 1 0 0 \ \mathrm { h }$ and maintaining $8 9 \%$ of their initial performance for $^ { 8 0 0 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ at $8 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . This strategy is applicable across various device architectures， ofering a new molecular design paradigm for long-term interfacial stability in perovskite solar cells.

O rganic-inorganic metal hybrid perovskites，as a new class of optoelectronic semiconductor materials,exhibit broad structural tunability and versatile property adjustability. Interface engineering plays a pivotal role in optimizing the performance of optoelectronic devices such as solar cells.1- 4

Specifically, interface modification in perovskites enables defect passivation，surface potential regulation，and enhanced mechanical and chemical stability. Aromatic group-modified ammonium salt compounds are often used as interfacial layers deposited on three-dimensional perovskite surfaces to passivate surface defects,owing to their structural compatibility,thermal stability,and hydrophobicity.Among them,phenylethylammonium salts and their derivatives are the most well-known.5,6

You et al. demonstrate that phenethylammonium iodide (PEAI) passivates surface defects and suppresses nonradiative recombination in FA/MA mixed perovskite films, leading to highly efficient solar cells.7

Nevertheless,accumulating evidence has revealed inherent limitations of arylammonium-based passivating_agents, including bulk-phase migration, 8-15 deprotonation,I6-18 and facetselectiveinteractioallofwchudermieteoe stability of perovskite optoelectronic devices. For instance, modifying guanidine groups to increase the acid dissociation constant $\left( \mathsf { p } K _ { \mathsf { a } } \right)$ can enhance the photothermal stability of the interfacial layer;20,21 weakening the interaction between the passivation layer and the lead iodide octahedra can reduce inward migration of the passivating species； and employing multiple passivating agents can achieve polyfacet passivation. However, it is challenging to simultaneously fulfill all of these requirements. Guanidinium halides are compounds with a high $\mathsf { p } K _ { \mathsf { a } }$ and excellent thermal stability. In addition， the guanidinium group exhibits diverse hydrogen bonding modes, which endow it with pronounced adhesive properties2-24 They have been widely used forbulk dopingand surface passivation of perovskites to improve their optoelectronic performanceandehancestability52 Suetrt that guanidinium iodide (GAI） acts as a multifunctional interface passivator，effectively suppressing nonradiative recombination and enhancing the efficiency and stability of perovskite solar cells.33 Due to their rich coordination modes and diverse crystal structures with lead halides,guanidinium halides are also considered_promising candidates for multifaceted surface modification.34 The relatively large ionic radius of guanidinium halides often leads to collapse of the threedimensional perovskite structure, resulting in phase transitions. To employ the GAI interface modification,it is essential to restrict its migration into the perovskite bulk.To date,studies of guanidinium-based derivatives functionalized with aromatic groups as interfacial modifers for perovskite surface passivation remain scarce. Interfacial materials obtained by grafting aromatic groups onto $\mathrm { G A ^ { + } }$ are expected to reduce the driving force for $\mathrm { G A ^ { + } }$ incorporation into the perovskite lattice while also lowering the moisture affinity of the perovskite interface and offering the potential to balance the interfacial polarity distribution.

In this work, a phenylethyl group was chemically bonded with a guanidinium group to synthesize phenylethylguanidinium $\left( \mathrm { P E G A } ^ { + } \right)$ for perovskite interfacial passivation. Owing to its higher $\mathsf { p } K _ { \mathrm { a } }$ than $\mathrm { P E A } ^ { + }$ ，PEGA+ exhibits enhanced thermal stability. Strong hydrogen bonding and the hydrophobic phenylethyl group suppress its migration into the perovskite bulk,enabling stable surface adsorption and formation of a robust passvation layer.At ${ 8 5 } ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ the interfacial layer shows negligible low-dimensional phase migration for over $9 0 0 \ \mathrm { m i n } ,$ far outperforming the ammonium-salt control, which degrades within $6 0 ~ \mathrm { \ m i n }$ .Meanwhile, $\mathrm { P E G A ^ { + } }$ effectively passivates surface defects,delivering $1 . 5 5 ~ \mathrm { e V }$ perovskite solar cells with a PCE of $2 6 . 2 \%$ . The devices retain over $9 6 \%$ of initial efficiency after $1 1 0 0 \mathrm { { h } }$ MPP tracking and over $89 \%$ after $^ { 8 0 0 \mathrm { h } }$ at $8 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ 、This strategy is also applicable to n-i-p and widebandgap devices，achieving PCEs above $2 5 . 7 \%$ and $2 3 . 1 \%$ respectively，and offers guidance for designing stable guanidinium-based passivation molecules

First,we synthesized and analyzed the ammonium cation compounds featuring phenyl-group-linked structural motifs (Figures S1-S4). The compounds studied include phenylethylammonium cation $\left( \mathrm { P E A } ^ { + } \right)$ and phenylethylguanidinium cation $\left( \mathrm { P E G A } ^ { + } \right)$ (Figure la). These compounds share the same phenyl group but difer in $\mathsf { p } K _ { \mathrm { a } }$ and cation size, following the order: guanidinium $>$ formamidinium $>$ ammonium. When the cation is directly linked to the phenyl ring,conjugation induces a more rigid structure and lowers the $\mathsf { p } K _ { \mathsf { a } }$ . In contrast, introducing an alkyl chain increases conformational flexibility and strengthens interactions with counteranions. The $\mathsf { p } K _ { \mathrm { a } }$ values of these molecules are shown in Figure lb,where $\mathrm { P E G A ^ { + } }$ exhibits a higher $\mathsf { p } K _ { \mathrm { a } }$ 、Zeta potential measurements in aqueous solution reveal that the guanidinium-substituted structures possess higher zeta potentials ( $2 0 . 6 8  { \mathrm { ~ m V } } _ { \cdot }$ ，Figure 1c).The thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) curves show that the decomposition temperatures of the iodide salts of $\mathrm { P E G A ^ { + } }$ and $\mathrm { P E A } ^ { + }$ are 276.7 and $1 7 8 . 1 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ respectively. The higher $\mathsf { p } K _ { \mathrm { a } }$ of $\mathrm { P E G A ^ { + } }$ indicates a stronger binding affinity with HI resulting in improved thermal stability (Figure S5).

We conducted a comparative analysis of the spatial conformations of $\mathrm { P E A } ^ { + }$ and $\mathrm { P E G A } ^ { + }$ to better understand the interfacial interactions between $\mathrm { P E G A } ^ { + }$ ， $\mathrm { [ P b X _ { 6 } ] ^ { 4 - } }$ ，and the perovskite，as well as the resulting product structures.We compared the conformations of $\mathrm { P E A } ^ { + }$ and $\mathrm { P E G A } ^ { + }$ within the dielectric environment of the perovskite lattice,as shown in Figure ld, where the distance between the two amino groups on the guanidinium cation is $0 . 2 6 \ \mathrm { n m }$ and the distance from the amino group to the methylene group is $0 . 3 7 ~ \mathrm { n m } , ^ { 5 }$ Using density functional theory (DFT),we obtained the conformational distribution of $\mathrm { P E G A } ^ { + }$ under the dielectric constant of perovskite (33),as shown in Figure S6.The results reveal that the folded conformation of $\mathrm { P E G A } ^ { + }$ exhibits a lower energy of $3 8 . 9 \mathrm { ~ k J ~ m o l ^ { - 1 } }$ . This supports our hypothesis that the cationic salt of $\mathrm { P E G A ^ { + } }$ is more likely to adopt a folded conformation when it is incorporated into the perovskite structure. The guanidinium group can effectively delocalize the positive charge over its entire functional plane，and the maximum electrostatic potential (ESP） of $\mathrm { P E G A ^ { + } }$ $( 6 . 0 \ \mathrm { e V } )$ is lower than that of $\mathrm { P E A } ^ { + }$ $\left( 6 . 6 \ \mathrm { e V } \right)$ .The distribution of the electrostatic potential energy is shown in Figure S7. This indicates that $\mathrm { P E G A ^ { + } }$ possesses a stronger charge delocalization capability and a weaker local positive potential,leading to more moderate and distributed electrostatic interactions with anions or the inorganic framework.

The reaction products of PEGACl with the perovskite surface were further investigated.A higher concentration of PEGACl salt was spin-coated onto the perovskite film. By observing the lattice fringes in the film using TEM (Figure 2a), $d$ -spacings of 0.57 and $0 . 3 1 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ were identified. The spacing of $0 . 3 1 \ \mathrm { n m }$ originates from the $\left[ \mathrm { P b I } _ { 6 } \right] ^ { 4 - }$ octahedron，while the $0 . 5 7 ~ \mathrm { \ n m }$ spacing is attributed to the (161）plane of the $\mathrm { P E G A } _ { 2 } \mathrm { P b I } _ { 4 }$ model. Grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS） measurements revealed the emergence of new diffraction ringsat $q \ = \ 1 . 1$ $\mathring { \mathbf { A } } ^ { - 1 }$ and $1 . 6 \ \mathrm { ~ \AA ~ } ^ { - 1 }$ ， corresponding to the（i61）and（231）planes，respectively (Figure 2b).In addition, these scattering rings were observed to be isotropically distributed，and the crystallinity was relatively weak，in contrast to the preferential orientation observed for surface-passivated $\mathrm { P E A } ^ { + }$ (Figure 2c). The averaged charge distribution in $\mathrm { P E G A } ^ { + }$ ，together with its multiple self-hydrogen-bonding interactions，weakens its interaction with the $[ \mathrm { P b } _ { 6 } ] ^ { 4 - }$ octahedra. The randomly oriented phenethyl groups further reduce the crystallinity of the low-dimensional perovskite phase at grain boundaries, resulting in weaker diffraction signals.This also indicates that $\mathrm { P E G A ^ { + } }$ exhibits lower reactivity toward the 3D perovskite bulk phase.As a result, $\mathrm { P E G A ^ { + } }$ tends to encapsulate the surface of the 3D perovskite rather than infiltrate into its interior. To further confirm this finding,we carried out DFT calculations to determine the binding energy $\left( E _ { \mathrm { b } } \right)$ between the perovskite and the two salts,as shown in Figures 2d and 2e.To fabricate the low-dimensional/3D perovskite films，we separately spincoated equimolar solutions of PEACl and PEGACl onto the underlying $\mathrm { F A P b I } _ { 3 }$ films.XRD was then used to evaluate the competitive reactivity of $\mathrm { P E A } ^ { + }$ and $\mathrm { P E G A } ^ { + }$ with $\mathrm { F A P b I } _ { 3 }$ (Figure 2f).We heated the passivated films to $8 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for aging and monitored the formation of two-dimensional perovskite phases at the film surface by small-angle $\mathrm { X }$ -ray diffraction (SXRD).For perovskite films modifed with PEACl, additional diffraction peaks at lower angles gradually emerged with increasing aging time,indicating the formation of quasi-2D perovskites with higher n values.36 This suggests that PEACl migrates into the interior of the perovskite crystals. In contrast, for perovskite films with PEGACl surface modification，only very weak low-dimensional perovskite diffraction peaks can be observed, even after prolonged aging. Owing to its localized charge,weaker self-interaction, and smaller steric hindrance, $\mathrm { P E A } ^ { + }$ readily reacts with $\left[ \mathrm { P b I } _ { 6 } \right] ^ { 4 - }$ to form a 2D phase and tends to diffuse into the bulk during aging or annealing，generating defects. In summary，we developed a phenylethylguanidinium $\left( \mathrm { P E G A } ^ { + } \right)$ interfacial modifier that combines the high $\mathsf { p } K _ { \mathrm { a } }$ and thermal stability of guanidinium with the hydrophobicity of phenylethyl groups. $\mathrm { P E G A } ^ { + }$ effectively passivates surface defects，suppresses bulk-phase migration，and stabilizes the perovskite structure, leading to significantly enhanced device performance and durability.Perovskite solar cells treated with $\bar { \mathrm { P E G A } } ^ { + }$ achieved a power conversion efficiency exceeding $2 6 . 2 \%$ and demonstrated remarkable operational stability, retaining over $9 6 \%$ of their initial efficiency after $1 1 0 0 \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ of continuous MPP tracking and maintaining $8 9 \%$ efficiency after $^ { 8 0 0 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ at $8 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ .This strategy is simultaneously applicable to multiple device architectures,including $\mathtt { n - i - p }$ configurations and wide-bandgap perovskite solar cells，delivering PCEs exceeding $2 5 . 7 \%$ and $2 3 . 1 \%$ ,respectively.This work highlights the potential of guanidinium-based aromatic derivatives as promising candidates for durable interfacial engineering in perovskite optoelectronics.
