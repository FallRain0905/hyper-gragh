# ABSTRACT

Depositing bismuth (Bi) catalysts on negative electrodes of iron-chromium redox flow bateries enhances $\mathrm { C r } ^ { 2 + } /$ $\mathrm { C r } ^ { \bar { 3 } + }$ redox reaction activity and suppresses hydrogen evolution. Bi remains stable within the conventional battery voltage range (not below $0 . 7 \ \mathrm { V } )$ . However, under abnormal conditions such as deep discharge during operation or continuous self-ischarge during prolonged batery shutdown,the negative electrode potential may increase,triggering Bi disolution.Subsequent uncontrolled redeposition concentrates Bi at the electrode inlet, causing flow blockage and performance degradation.This study quantitatively investigates the triggering conditions for Bi disslution under such abnormal conditions and the uniformity of uncontrolled redeposition heterogeneity.Ina single-cell experiment,negative electrode flow resistance serves as a quantitative indicator for thereal-time diagnosis of Bi distribution uniformity.The electrode flow resistance and ICP-MS tests of negolyte collectively show that Bi remains stable during charge-discharge cycling within $0 . 6 { - } 1 . 1 \mathrm { ~ V } .$ ，Discharging the battery to $0 . 5 5 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ $\mathrm { 1 0 0 ~ m A ~ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ ）initiates partial dissolution,with significant dissolution occurring at lower voltages.Notably,low current densities exacerbate dissolution at the same cutoff voltage.Toaddress Bi dissolution during pump-off (shutdown),a direct charging strategy under pump-off conditions enables controlled redeposition,maintaining distribution uniformity.Furthermore,for severe inhomogeneity,we innovatively propose a “pump-off dissolution and pump-on redeposition” method,significantly improving Bi distribution uniformity.

# 1． Introduction

Redox flow batteries (RFBs), first proposed by NASA in the 1970s [1],ofer significant advantages for large-scale energy storage,including intrinsic safety,scalable capacity,long cycle life,and design flexibility [2,3].Unlike conventional rechargeable batteries (e.g.,lithium-ion), RFBS store active species in external tanks,decoupling power and energy capacity for flexible system design [4,5].Among RFB technologies, the iron-chromium redox flow battery (ICRFB) stands out as an early and commercially promising system due to its low cost,abundant raw materials,and environmental friendliness [6,7].

However,ICRFBs face challenges at the negative electrode: sluggish kinetics of the $\mathrm { C r } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { C r } ^ { 3 + }$ redox couple result in significant polarization losses [8,9],and the reaction's equilibrium potential is considerably lower than that of the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), leading to severe parasitic hydrogen evolution especially during the last stages of charging,which in turn causes capacity decay and reduced Coulombic efficiency [1O-l2].Bismuth (Bi) catalyst deposition effectively addresses these issues by enhancing $\mathrm { C r } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { C r } ^ { 3 + }$ kinetics and suppressing hydrogen evolution.Hang-xin Che et al.demonstrated that bismuth nanoparticles anchored on nitrogen-doped graphite felt exhibit excellent catalytic performance.This enhancement arises from two synergistic effects:(1） the bismuth nanoparticles form intermediates with $\mathrm { H } ^ { + }$ ， suppressing hydrogen evolution side reactions during charging;and (2) the nitrogen dopants provide adsorption sites for chromium ions, accelerating the $\mathrm { C r } ^ { 3 + } / \mathrm { C r } ^ { 2 + }$ redox kinetics [13]. Studies demonstrate substantial performance improvement with Bi, such as increased voltage efficiency (8 $5 . 5 ~ \ \% - 9 0 . 4 ~ \ \% )$ [6],enhanced energy efficiency with reduced capacity decay [14]，and synergistic kinetics enhancement/HER suppression via Bi-C composites $( 8 6 . 5 4 \%$ energy efficiency) [15].Meanwhile,Bi catalysts also show efficacy in vanadium RFBs (VRFBs) for $\mathrm { V } ^ { 3 + } / \mathrm { V } ^ { 2 + }$ kinetics [16-20].

Uniform Bi deposition is critical for performance.Homogeneous Bi distribution across the electrode surface maximizes active sites and kinetics,while non-uniformity causes localized flow blockage.In redox flow battery stacks,increased flow resistance in electrodes reduces electrolyte flow rate,elevating mass transport losses and side reactions. While ex-situ deposition methods like impregnation achieve uniform Bi catalyst distribution,in-situ deposition offers greater operational flexibility and simplified maintenance.Bismuth deposition occurs spontaneously by adding $\mathbf { B i } ^ { 3 + }$ ions to the negolyte before charging，and maintenance requires no battery disassembly. Studies show Bi tends to concentrate near the electrode inlet during in-situ deposition,but strategies like higher flow rates [21],intermittent electrodeposition [22], and pulsed current deposition (maintaining liquid-phase relative permeability ${ \sim } 1$ VS. $< 0 . 2$ for continuous current） [23] improve uniformity.

Durable Bi adhesion is essential for sustained catalysis.Dissolution not only compromises catalytic efficacy but also leads to the occurrence of non-uniform redeposition,resulting in flow blockage within the electrode.Weak binding from traditional electrodeposition can cause detachment during cycling；solutions like carbon thermal reactions enhance adhesion and stability [24].Bi dissolution can occur under specific conditions (e.g.VRFB open-circuit at a state of charge (SOC) of O),with redeposition favoring the inlet region during charging [21]. However,Bi demonstrates excellent stability within typical operating voltage ranges $( 0 . 7 \mathrm { - } 1 . 2 \ V $ for ICRFB and $0 . 8 \mathrm { - } 1 . 6 5 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ for VRFB) during cycling,as shown by stable performance over thousands of cycles [21, 25,26].

Despite stability under normal operation，atypical conditions threaten Bi integrity.Although Bi dissolution under open-circuit conditionsat $S \mathrm { { O C } } = 0$ is established [21],dissolution can also be triggered during unexpected deep discharge events by excessive elevation of the negative electrode potential.The critical discharge voltage at which this dissolution initiates remains unexplored.Furthermore,during scheduled pump-off shutdowns (to reduce energy loss),self-discharge persists due to crossover currents [27,28] and shunt currents [29-31].This gradually decays open-circuit voltage (OcV) and raises the negative electrode potential,ultimately causing Bi dissolution. Upon restart, the detached $\mathbf { B i } ^ { 3 + }$ ions can be flushed out of the carbon felt by electrolyte flow,disrupting uniformity and degrading performance.Crucially,Bi dissolution and subsequent redeposition under these atypical operating conditions remain systematically uninvestigated.Notably,low-rate continuous pumping can effectively prevent a rise in negative electrode potential, but continuous pumping leads to significant pumping power loss.Therefore,a more rational approach is needed to maintain uniform Bi distribution during pump-of periods.

Severe Bi non-uniformity causes flow blockage [21-23],manifesting as reduced electrolyte flow rates in stacks.Abnormal stack voltages (excessively high during charging or excessively low during discharging)indicate significant mass transfer losses.In this case,monitoring the electrolyte flow rate through the stack is recommended.If the flowrate is abnormally low,it suggests severe uneven distribution of Bi catalysts, leading to flow blockage.Restoring uniformity typically requires energy-intensive full dissolution (near-zero SOc discharge) followed by lengthy redeposition.A cost-effective,convenient method to improve Bi distribution uniformity is therefore vital for stable ICRFB stack operation.

To address these gaps,we systematically investigate Bi dissolution and redeposition under atypical conditions (deep discharge,pump-off self-discharge) and their impact on ICRFB performance.Furthermore, we develop methods to improve Bi distribution uniformity.Using a custom single-cell ICRFB,we characterize Bi uniformity dynamics via flow resistance measurements across a $1 5 \ \mathrm { c m }$ long electrode. Specifically,we: (1) Determine the critical discharge cut-of voltage causing Bi dissolution during cycling;(2) Investigate Bi dissolution effects under pump-off conditions and propose a pump-off redeposition strategy; and (3) Introduce an innovative “pump-off dissolution/pump-on redeposition”method to rectify severe distribution inhomogeneity.

# 3．Results and discussion

# 3.1.Significant impact of bismuth deposition uniformity on electrolyte flow resistance

Under conventional in-situ charging conditions, $\mathbf { B i } ^ { 3 + }$ ions are almost completely consumed near the electrode inlet. This occurs because the low negative electrode potential favors rapid deposition at the inlet, where the ion concentration is highest, thereby preventing significant ion transport downstream.Consequently, bismuth deposits are primarily localized at the electrode inlet. Our recent study found that for a Bi loading of $0 . 1 \mathrm { m g } \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ ,deposition using a continuous current of $6 0 \mathrm { m A }$ $\mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ resulted in significant flow resistance increase due to localized Bi accumulation at the electrode inlet,as confirmed by SEM characterization [23].In contrast,in-situ pulsed current deposition achieved uniform distribution without increasing flow resistance,and no significant Biagglomerates were observed at the electrode inlet. The above study has verified that the variation in flow resistance can serve as an indicator of bismuth deposition uniformity.The present study uses the same Bi loading but under different electrolyte volumes and feeding conditions. In this section,we compare both continuous and pulsed current deposition methods by monitoring the total pressure drop $( \Delta P _ { t o t a l } )$ of the electrolyte flowing through the negative electrode,thereby establishing a baseline for subsequent experiments.

# 3.2.Bismuth dissolution caused by deep discharge

During the discharge process of redox flow batteries,the cell voltage gradually decreases while the negative electrode potential correspondingly increases.Within the typical operating voltage range (0.7-1.2 V) of ICRFBs, Bi catalysts remain stable without dissolution.However,during the operational lifespan of battery stacks,accidental over-discharge may occur.If the negative electrode potential exceeds a critical threshold, deposited Bi dissolves into the electrolyte as $\mathbf { B i } ^ { 3 + }$ ions. During subsequent charging,these ions redeposit onto the electrode.However,under standard charging current densities, $\mathbf { B i } ^ { 3 + }$ ions preferentially deposit near the inlet since the stoichiometric number of the $\mathbf { B i } ^ { 3 + }$ ion reduction reaction is much smaller than 1 [23].Therefore,although the total amount of Bi remains unchanged,the initially uniform Bi distribution is disrupted. It is therefore essential to investigate effects of the discharge cut-off voltages on Bi dissolution to prevent such issues during battery operation.

First,we employ the pulsed current deposition method (Table 1) to uniformly deposit Bi catalysts $( 0 . 1 \mathrm { \ m g \ c m ^ { - 2 } } )$ on the negative electrode surface at initial electrolyte $S \mathrm { O C } = 0$ .Subsequently,charge-discharge cycling tests are conducted at a current density of $1 0 0 \mathrm { \ m A \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ Deep discharge below the critical voltage dissolves Bi from the electrode surface, generating $\mathbf { B i } ^ { 3 + }$ ions.During subsequent charging, these ions redeposit preferentially near the electrode inlet.This localized deposition progressively increases flow resistance over repeated cycles.We observe that this blocking effect becomes readily measurable after 10 charge-discharge cycles,establishing flow resistance change as a clear indicator of significant Bi dissolution.The results are presented in Fig.3. Across the four tests labeled (a) to (d),the charge cut-off voltage is consistently set to $1 . 1 \ V$ ,while the discharge cut-off voltages are set to $0 . 6 \mathrm { ~ V } , 0 . 5 5 \mathrm { ~ V } , 0 . 5 \mathrm { ~ V } ,$ and $0 . 4 5 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ ,respectively.As shown in Fig.3(a), during 10 cycles between $_ { 0 . 6 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } }$ and $1 . 1 \ V _ { \cdot }$ ， $\Delta P _ { t o t a l }$ exhibits minor fluctuations that synchronize with periodic cell voltage changes,primarily due to variations in electrolyte viscosity related to the state of charge. Notably, the $\Delta P _ { t o t a l }$ values at identical SOC remain stable across different cycles without an increasing trend, indicating no obvious change in flow resistance within the electrode.This confirms that discharging to $_ { 0 . 6 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } }$ at $1 0 0 \mathrm { \ m A \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ does not induce Bi dissolution.

In contrast, Fig. 3(b) shows a gradual increase in $\Delta P _ { t o t a l }$ with cycle number.This is atributed to partial dissolution of Bi during discharging to $0 . 5 5 \mathrm { V }$ ,releasing $\mathbf { B i } ^ { 3 + }$ ions that are carried by the electrolyte into the negative storage tank.Subsequent charging causes preferential redeposition of these ions near the carbon felt inlet. Over multiple cycles, accumulation of Bi in this region leads to a continuous rise in flow resistance.In Fig.3(c) and (d),where the discharge cut-off voltage is further reduced to $0 . 5 \mathrm { V }$ and $0 . 4 5 \mathrm { V }$ ,larger amounts of Bi dissolve from the electrode during each discharge.As a result, $\Delta P _ { t o t a l }$ increases more rapidlyand toa greater extent.

During the shutdown of flow battery stacks,cells enter an opencircuit state and pumps are typically deactivated to minimize power losses.However,self-discharge occurs due to shunt and crossover currents,causing the negative electrode potential to gradually rise and trigger Bi dissolution.To simulate this prolonged self-discharge process, we deeply discharge the battery under pump-off conditions to induce Bi dissolution and subsequently propose a remedial redeposition method under pump-off conditions.Comparative experiments confirm this method efectively preserves Bi distribution homogeneity.

# 3.4.Eliminating Bi-induced blockage through disolution and redeposition

As discussed in Sections 3.2 and 3.3,even with initially uniform deposition of Bi catalysts,severe non-uniform Bi distribution may still develop over the operational life of flow battery stacks.To address this issue,the section proposes a convenient “pump-off dissolution and pump-on redeposition”method to effectively improve Bi distribution uniformity.The method involves discharging the battery under pumpoff conditions to induce Bi dissolution,followed by resuming electrolyte flow to flush the $\mathbf { B i } ^ { 3 + }$ ions concentrated at the electrode inlet toward the outlet. This allows $\mathbf { B i } ^ { 3 + }$ ions to redeposit while flowing through the electrode,thereby improving Bi distribution uniformity.The effects of electrolyte state of charge and flushing velocity on Bi uniformity are also investigated.

Unlike in Sections 3.2 and 3.3,after the three preparation stages in Table1,pulsed current deposition for Bi catalyst is not employed in this section.Instead, a constant current of $1 0 0 \mathrm { \ m A \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ is applied to charge the battery to $_ { 1 . 1 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } }$ During this process,Bi deposits predominantly near the electrode inlet, causing flow blockage as described in Section 3.1. Subsequent experimental stages follow Table 3,with results shown in Fig.7.Fig.7(a)-(d) correspond to different electrolyte states of charge, determined by the charge/discharge process in Stage 1.Taking Fig.7(a) as an example: in Stage 1,after discharging at $1 0 0 \mathrm { \ m A c m ^ { - 2 } }$ to $0 . 7 \mathrm { V }$ ,the cell is switched to open-circuit.In Stage 2, the open-circuit condition is maintained for 5 min with the electrolyte flow stopped.After discharge in Stage 3,Bi dissolves into $\mathbf { B i } ^ { 3 + }$ ions.In Stage 4,the cell remains at open-circuit while the peristaltic pump is restarted at a liquid velocity of $6 \mathrm { m m } s ^ { - 1 }$ ,flushing the $\mathbf { B i } ^ { 3 + }$ ions concentrated at the electrode inlet toward the outlet. A decrease in $\Delta P _ { t o t a l }$ from approximately $6 5 \mathrm { k P a }$ (before pump shutdown in Stage 2) to about $4 5 \mathrm { k P a }$ is observed.In Stage 5,the open-circuit condition and $6 \ : \mathrm { m m } \ : s ^ { - 1 }$ flow are maintained for $3 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ During this period, $\Delta P _ { t o t a l }$ gradually increases to around $5 4 ~ \mathrm { k P a }$ ,indicating that some dissolved $\mathbf { B i } ^ { 3 + }$ ions fail to redeposit on the carbon felt and instead return to the storage tank.When these ions flow back to the electrode inlet, they deposit predominantly near the inlet, increasing flow resistance.Nevertheless,the final $\Delta P _ { t o t a l }$ of $5 4 \mathrm { k P a }$ remains below the initial $6 5 ~ \mathrm { k P a }$ ,indicating an overall reduction in flow resistance. During the two charge-discharge cycles between O.7 and $1 . 1 \mathrm { ~ V ~ }$ in Stage 6,flow resistance remains stable,as shown in Fig. S6.

Based on the above analysis,the increase in $\Delta P _ { t o t a l }$ during Stage 5 $( 5 0 - 5 3 ~ \mathrm { m i n }$ ,Fig.7(a)) primarily stems from incomplete bismuth deposition: a fraction of $\mathbf { B i } ^ { 3 + }$ ions fails to deposit on the carbon felt in Stage 4 and is instead carried into the electrolyte tank by the electrolyte flow.To mitigate this,increasing the electrolyte's SOC enhances the negolyte's reducing potential,enabling timely redeposition of most $\mathbf { B i } ^ { 3 + }$ ions onto the mid-and downstream electrode sections.Therefore,three higher electrolyte SOC conditions are designed: discharging to $_ { 0 . 8 \mathrm { ~ V ~ } }$ ,charging to $1 . 1 \mathrm { V }$ ,and charging to $1 . 2 \mathrm { V _ { \it \mathrm { i } } }$ all at $1 0 0 \mathrm { \ m A \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ The corresponding results are presented in Fig.7(b)-(d).As electrolyte SOC increases,a significant reduction in $\Delta P _ { t o t a l }$ is observed in Stages 4 and 5.Taking Fig. 7(c) as an example, $\Delta P _ { t o t a l }$ during the $3 0 { \cdot } \mathrm { { m i n } }$ flow period in Stage 5 stabilizes atapproximately $4 3 \mathrm { k P a }$ ,which is close to the initial value of $3 8 \ \mathrm { k P a }$ before Bi deposition at $t = 0$ .In summary,the electrolyte SOC significantly influences the effectiveness of the pump-off dissolution and pump-on redeposition method.

# 4.Conclusions

This study investigates the dissolution and redeposition of Bi catalysts in ICRFBs under abnormal operating conditions,including deep discharge and prolonged shutdown.The findings contribute to maintaining a uniform Bi distribution within the electrode and preventing an increase in flow resistance,providing insights for designing highefficiency battery stacks.While this study focuses on ICRFBs,both proposed methods- the remedial redeposition technique for Bi dissolution under pump-off conditions and the “pump-off dissolution/pumpon redeposition”approach to eliminate Bi-induced blockage -are also applicable to VRFBs.The main conclusions are summarized as follows:

1.Bismuth catalysts exhibit high deposition stability within the typical charge-discharge voltage range (O.7-1.2 V) of iron-chromium redox flow batteries.However,when discharged to $0 . 5 5 ~ \mathrm { V }$ at a current density of $1 0 0 \mathrm { \ m A \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ ，partial dissolution of Bi occurs.Further discharge to lower voltages results in substantial Bi dissolution. Moreover,for the same discharge cutoff voltage,lower current densities result in more extensive Bi dissolution.This conclusion is jointly confirmed by observed increases in flow resistance and ICP-MS test results.Furthermore,repeated deposition/dissolution cycling of the Bi catalyst during long-term operation may induce material degradation.This degradation could adversely affect catalyst adhesion and stability,presenting a significant challenge that merits dedicated investigation in future work.

2.Under shutdown conditions of flow battery stacks,the batteries enter an open-circuit state and the pumps are turned off. Continuous selfdischarge gradually increases the negative electrode potential,ultimately causing dissolution of Bi catalysts.Restoring electrolyte flow at this stage disrupts the originally uniform distribution of Bi.A conventional mitigation strategy is to maintain low-flow-rate electrolyte pumping during stack shutdown conditions to prevent a rapid rise in negative electrode potential, though this incurs additional energy losses due to continuous pumping. This paper proposes an alternative method: charging the battery under pump-off conditions to redeposit $\mathbf { B i } ^ { 3 + }$ ions at their original sites.Experiments confirm that this approach effectively preserves a uniform Bi distribution.

3.When Bi catalyst distribution becomes severely non-uniform,it must be dissolved and redeposited uniformly. The conventional approach involves fully discharging the battery to dissolve Bi, followed by redeposition using a pulsed-current strategy-a process that is both time-consuming and energetically costly.This paper introduces a more convenient and eficient technique:“pump-off dissolution and pump-on redeposition",which requires only a few minutes, providing significant convenience for the maintenance process of battery stacks.Experimental results demonstrate that this method significantly mitigates clogging issues caused by non-uniform Bi deposition,particularly under conditions of higher electrolyte SOC and elevated flushing velocities.During flow battery stack operation, stack voltages and electrolyte flow rates are monitored to detect flow blockage.For a blocked stack,applying the“pump-off dissolution and pump-on redeposition”method resolves the obstruction,significantly simplifying maintenance.
