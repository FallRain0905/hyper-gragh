Vanadium flow batery (VFB) is promising for grid-scale energy storage, yet the negative electrode remains a major bottleneck because sluggish $\mathrm { V } ^ { 3 + } / \mathrm { V } ^ { \bar { 2 } + }$ kinetics and the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) jointly limit efficiency,usable capacity,and cycling stability.Existing reviews mainly compare catalyst identities and electrochemical metrics,whereas the catalyst-loading route itself governs catalyst distribution in thre-dimensional felts,manufacturability,maintenance mode,and cost-scaling from laboratory cels to commercial stacks.This Review uses metal-based multifunctional catalytic layers as a representative platform to compare two route for catalytic negative electrodes: preloading before cell assembly and in-situ construction from electrolyte-borne metal ions during operation.We first organize an evidence framework for electrode evaluation, highlighting operando and spatially resolved diagnostics that distinguish catalyst-related improvements from changes in wetting and masstransport and directly detect HER in porous felts.We then analyze representative preloaded and in-situ-constructed metal systems and show that the two routes folow fundamentally different technoeconomic logics:preloading is constrained by area-dependent fabrication,uniformity control,and refurbishment burdens,whereas in-situ construction shifts the intervention toward low-dose electrolyte additives,electrochemical conditioning,and operation-integrated maintenance.On this basis,we proposean applicationoriented framework spanningactivity,HERsuppression,stabilityost-effectiveness,and scalability,andargue that in-situ construction is currently the more deployment-oriented pathway,while preloading remains indispensable for catalyst discovery and mechanistic validation.

# 1．Introduction

Among various large-scale energy storage technologies, the vanadium flow battery(VFB) shows broad application potential across a wide range of scenarios [1-3]. It is deemed to be one of the most promising energy storage devices due to its high safety,potential for long-duration energy storage,and excellent cycling stability [4-8]. The three key components of a VFB are the graphite felt (GF) or carbon felt (CF) electrode,the ion-exchange membrane,and the vanadium electrolyte, the ion-exchange membrane that separates the two half-cells,and the vanadium electrolyte that supplies the reactive substances (Fig.1) [9, 10].During VFB operation,electrolyte is pumped into the battery interior by a peristaltic pump,where it contacts the electrodes and undergoes reactions. During charging, $\mathrm { V O ^ { 2 + } }$ in the positive electrolyte is oxidized to $\mathrm { V O } _ { 2 } ^ { + }$ ,generating $\mathrm { H } ^ { + }$ ,while $\mathsf { V } ^ { 3 + }$ in the negative electrolyte is reduced to $\mathrm { V } ^ { 2 + }$ [11]. Concurrently, electrons and $\mathrm { H } ^ { + }$ are transported from the positive electrolyte to the negative electrolyte through the external circuit and ion-exchange membrane, respectively [l2]. During discharging, the $\mathrm { V O } _ { 2 } ^ { + }$ and $\mathrm { V } ^ { 2 + }$ species in the two electrolytes convert chemical energy into electrical energy to power the external load [13]. Despite these merits,the deployment of VFB is still constrained by system cost,which is strongly coupled to achievable power density and stack footprint.

The optimization of electrolytes and membrane materials are hindered by their high costs and limited compatibility,making them unsuitable for large-scale applications [14].By comparison,electrode upgrading is a manufacturable lever,because optimized electrodes can be integrated into existing stack fabrication and commercial felt supply chains,allowing performance improvements without fundamentally redesigning the balance-of-plant.Nevertheless,commercial electrodes still exhibit major drawbacks,including solid-liquid interfacial incompatibility,side reactions,and poor catalytic activity/kinetics (Fig.1) [15].To address these issues,extensive efforts have been devoted to electrode modification. Table 1 compiles a representative set of recently published review articles on VFB electrode materials and shows that the literature has largely converged on four recurring “focuses"(Focus A-D) [12,14-44].

Here,some reviews (A)aim to establish general and transferable understanding,such as reaction mechanisms,rate-limiting steps,interfacial processes,and the common physical/chemical constraints; for example, Skyllas-Kazacos et al. synthesize electrode designs together with vanadium redox mechanisms and practical operating limitations, thereby clarifying why electrode engineering remains decisive for real devices [16]. Other reviews (B) argue that progress depends on how performance is evaluated and compared,and therefore concentrate on parameter identification,kinetic/transport decoupling,and standardizedmetricsthatenablecross-studycomparability；the methodology-oriented synthesis by M Buriak et al.is a typical example in this category [2O]. Building on these foundations,the largest body of reviews fallsunder electrodes directly；Roth et al.organize carbon-structure regulation strategies and connect property relationships to power density improvements under operational constraints [24].Finally,several reviews treat electrocatalysis as a dedicated lever, summarizing catalyst concepts in terms of activity enhancement and side reaction suppression; Amini et al. provide a representative catalyst-centered perspective that complements other optimization [21].Taken together,these perspectives have substantially advanced our understanding of“what works"; however, they also reveal a practical gap that motivates this Review: beyond identifying promising materials,comparatively fewer syntheses interrogate whether the modification route itself can be implemented with an acceptable processing burden and a scalable cost structure. Importantly, most existing reviews compare which materials,structures,or catalysts deliver better electrochemical metrics,but less often examine which loading route is more compatible with practical manufacturing and scale-up.For metal-based catalytic layers on the VFB negative electrode, the loading routeitselfdirectlyinfluencescatalystdistributionwithin three-dimensional felts,the balance between upfront processing and operational maintenance,and the associated cost-scaling logic [14,39, 40,42]

Among cell-level limiting factors,the negative electrode is particularly critical because sluggish $\mathsf { V } ^ { 2 + } / \mathsf { V } ^ { 3 + }$ kinetics and competitive HER jointly penalize efficiency and long-term capacity retention.Researchers have extensively developed various negative electrode modifications, including intrinsic treatment, introduction of catalysts,and construction of novel electrodes [45].These methods have largely or partially addressed the issues with negative electrodes.Nevertheless,the perspective of scalability and economic benefits of the electrode optimization methods themselves is still lacking when considering the goal of large-scale commercial production.In the laboratory scale,electrode sizes usually fall within the range of 2 to $4 8 \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ [46-51]. In contrast, for the large-scale commercial applications that we can expect in the future, stacks will typically require electrodes larger than $9 0 0 ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ (Fig. 2) [52-54].Under these circumstances,it is imperative to put the scalability of electrode modification methods in our research.

Loading metal-based multifunctional catalytic layer represents a distinctive approach to electrode modification [28].On the one hand, their unique physicochemical properties enable them to exhibit excellent performance in enhancing reaction kinetics [55].Moreover,certain metals can achieve dual functions of catalyzing $\mathrm { V } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { V } ^ { 3 + }$ redox reaction and inhibiting HER[56].Meanwhile,researchers have endowed them with special morphological structures to improve wettability and mass transfer [57].More importantly,while being able to address the shortcomings of commercial electrodes,a unique aspect of metal-based multifunctional catalytic layer is that metal ions can be directly electrodeposited on the negative electrode at low potentials to achieve in-situ construction of the catalyst [58]． Among the various negative-electrode modification strategies,metal-based multifunctional catalytic layers provide the most suitable platform for this Review because both preloading and electrolyte-mediated in-situ construction have been most directly developed in this class.This makes them particularly useful for comparing how the loading route,rather than catalystidentityalone,governscatalystdistributionin three-dimensional felts,apparent activity toward the $\mathrm { V } ^ { 3 + } / \mathrm { V } ^ { 2 + }$ reaction, HER selectivity,durability,manufacturability,and system-level cost scaling [33,40].

Accordingly,this Review summarizes recent advances in metalbased multifunctional catalytic layers (Bi, Sn, Cu, Sb,and Ag) for VFB negative electrodes from the perspective of catalyst-loading strategy. We first examine which diagnostic methods can distinguish genuine catalyst-related improvements from gains arising from wettability or mass transport and can directly detect HER,with particular emphasis on operando and spatially resolved diagnostics as well as practical HER detection.We then compare representative preloaded and in-situconstructed systems in terms of activity,HER suppression,stability, cost-effectiveness,and scalability,while also highlighting their different manufacturability, maintenance,and cost-scaling logics from laboratory cells to commercial stacks.On this basis,we provide an applicationoriented framework showing that preloading remains especially valuable for catalyst discovery and mechanistic validation,whereas in-situ construction is more attractive for deployment-oriented VFB systems when activity,HER suppression,stability,manufacturability,costeffectiveness,and scalability are considered together.

# 2．Evaluation of electrodes

# 2.1．Methodologies and comparability

The development of more effective modification methods for electrodes relies on the adoption of comprehensive and accurate electrode evaluation techniques.To this end,a variety of evaluation methods have been proposed,encompassing four key dimensions: electrochemical performance,battery performance,morphology，and compositional structure (Fig.3) [59-62].Electrochemical performance evaluation includes techniques such as electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS),cyclic voltammetry (CV),and linear sweep voltammetry (LSV),all of which provide crucial insights into electrochemical performance such as reaction kinetics [2O'63].Nevertheless,for porous felt electrodes in flow batteries,many measured “kinetic”signals are convoluted with ohmic loss,wetting,and mass-transport limitations; therefore,evaluation should explicitly distinguish intrinsic electrocatalytic activity from transport/accessibility effects wherever possible. Battery performance, on the other hand,focuses on assessing the behavior in applications, evaluating parameters like long-term performance and power density [26'45].However,battery and stack-level metrics are strongly coupled to engineering variables such as flow rate,pressure drop,flow-field design and electrode compression.

Morphological characterization is performed using atomic force microscopy (AFM)，scanning electron microscopy (SEM),and transmission electron microscopy(TEM) and so on,providing valuable information about microstructural changes and their influence on electrochemical performance.Meanwhile, compositional structure evaluation involves techniques such as X-ray diffraction (XRD), Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area analysis,and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS),which offer detailed insights into the crystalline phases,surface chemistry,and elemental composition of the electrode materials.Conventional electrochemical and battery-level tests are typically performed in-situ during operation,but they provide bulk-averaged responses and cannot directly resolve dynamic interfacial processes in porous felts.Moreover,most structural/chemical characterizations are conducted afterdisassembly; therefore,operando/ in-situ imaging and spectroscopy are increasingly needed to monitor interfacial reactions,wetting evolution,and gas/bubble behavior under realistic conditions.In the following，we highlight representative operando/in-situ methods for assessing activity/surface reactivity and HER/selectivity.

Accordingly，EIS,CV,LSV，and battery-level metrics should be treated primarily as baseline comparability tools,because apparent gains in peak current,polarization,EE,or power density may also reflect differences in flow rate,electrode compression, flow-field design, wetting,and reactant accessibility within the felt.The diagnostics discussed in the following subsections are therefore valuable because they provide the spatially or chemically resolved evidence needed to determine where the catalyst is active,whether the reaction distribution becomes more uniform,whether selectivity is genuinely improved,and how local deactivation or interfacial reconstruction develops during cycling.

# 2.2.Detection of electrode activity and surface reactivity

Advanced operando/in-situ methods can provide more direct evidence on what the reactive electrode surface is and how the reaction proceeds during operation. Such information is particularly valuable for porous felt electrodes.Therefore,researchers have increasingly developed and adopted operando,spatially resolved,and mechanismoriented methodologies to strengthen activity assessment and mechanistic understanding.

Stevenson et al.employed in-situ Raman spectroscopy coupled with CV and Raman mapping to resolve spatial heterogeneity of reaction processes.Such operando mapping highlights that “activity"can be nonuniformly distributed within the electrode and may follow distinct local reaction pathways (Fig.4a)[64]．Mench et al.implemented a printed-circuit-board (PCB)-based shunt-resistor array to measure two-dimensional in-plane current distribution,and emphasized that a fully segmented flow-field plate is necessary to avoid artifacts from lateral current spreading.Moreover,operating and structural factors directly connected “electrode activity"to utilization and transportunder engineering boundary conditions (Fig.4b) [65].

For comparing preloading and in-situ construction,these studies show why activity diagnostics must be spatially resolved: operando Raman can test whether a catalyst is only locally effective,whereas current mapping can show whether the gain from a loading strategy is translated into a more homogeneous reaction distribution across the electrode rather than merely a higher cell-averaged response.

To attribute performance evolution during cycling,Kim et al.used a dynamic hydrogen electrode (DHE) to separate positive/negative halfcell contributions and track impedance evolution,enabling decomposition of degradation into ohmic resistance and charge-transfer resistance changes and linking the rise of resistive components to electrode deterioration.Such half-cell-resolved diagnostics provide a practical “bridge”between local/operando insights and the macroscopic manifestation of activity loss over long-term operation (Fig.4c) [66]. Lee etal.combined carbon K-edge X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) with density functional theory (DFT) calculations to link functional groups and local carbon bonding environments.This approach enables deconvolution of averaged spectra into chemically meaningful surface descriptors,and helps distinguish changes associated with acid-induced surface reconstruction from those induced by interactions with vanadium aqua complexes,thereby providing quantifiable variables for correlating surface chemistry with electrochemical activity (Fig.4d) [67].In the same logic,DHE-type measurements are particularly useful for separating genuine negative-half-cell improvement from ful-cell engineering effects,whereas coupled XAS/DFT is more suitable for judging whether performance gains originate from electronic-structure regulation of catalytic sites or from changes in surface functional groups and interfacial reconstruction. Together, these methods turn activity evaluation from a collection of techniques into evidence for attributing where,why,and for how long a catalytic layer is effective.

# 2.3.Detection of the hydrogen evolution side reaction

During long-term cycling of VFB,battery performance gradually degrades, primarily manifested in capacity decay,with some laboratory scale VFB experiencing capacity decay to below $2 0 { - } 6 0 \%$ after extended cycling tests [68]. The capacity decay mainly stems from three aspects: volume imbalance,concentration imbalance,and charge imbalance [69-71].Volume and concentration imbalances primarily arise from the imperfect selectivity of ion-exchange membranes [72]. Simple strategies like electrolyte reflow can address volume imbalance,while remixing and redistributing the electrolyte can simultaneously resolve both concentration and volume imbalances [73].In contrast, charge imbalance is more challenging to address.Charge imbalance originates from HER at the negative electrode and oxygen evolution reaction (OER） at the positive electrode [74]. The oxygen generated at the positive electrode can be managed by connecting the positive and negative electrolyte storage tanks,allowing oxygen to react with vanadium ions at the negative electrode to maintain overall charge conservation [75].However,the hydrogen gas produced by HER accumulates in the storage tanks,elevating the average chemical valence of vanadium ions in the electrolyte and causing irreversible capacity loss [76].In such a case, the remixing method fails to produce the desired 50:50 ratio of v3+ to V4+, and thus cannot achieve the formal oxidation state of $\mathsf { V } ^ { 3 . 5 + }$ [77]. Although online electrolysis can resolve this issue,it is overly complex and introduces additional costs [78].Therefore,comparing with the positive electrode,the HER in the negative electrode is an important factor leading to capacity decay,and inhibiting the HER requires special attention.

Therefore,there is a pressing need to develop effective strategies for detecting HER in the negative electrode. Zeis et al. visualize hydrogen gas evolution in 3D via X-ray tomography,and correlate average saturation of CF with CV current response (Fig.5a) [79].Liu et al. use a weak measurement imaging(WMi) method to characterize the spatial distribution of surface activity and HER onset potential on negative electrode (Fig.5b) [8O].The law of total reflection links light intensity changes to refractive index shifts,enabling HER detection with high resolution [81].Recently,Roswitha et al.analyze bubble distribution in thick, porous electrodes of VFB for the first time using deep learning-based image segmentation of synchrotron X-ray micro-tomograms further (Fig.5c) [82].It finds that more negative electrode potentials and certain electrode materials cause more substantial bubble formation, and suggests that larger electrodes with a higher bulk-to-border ratio might reduce HER-related losses.Zhao et al.design a three-electrode electrochemical cell and transparent VFB to in-situ study HER during battery operation (Fig.5d) [83].The diverse relevant detection techniques indirectly highlight the importance of achieving high selectivity in catalyzing vanadium redox reactions while suppressing HER.Beyond instrument-intensive imaging,HER can also be screened by straightforward visual inspection of the electrolyte reservoir and tubing during operation: persistent bubble streams or gas accumulation in the tank/- lines provide an immediate qualitative indication of $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 }$ generation at the negative electrode (Fig.5e and f) [84].Wei et al. further used a three dimensional model to describe HER-driven bubble growth,detachment, and transport within porous felt electrodes,providing a direct view of how bubble behavior evolves under different current density conditions (Fig. 5g-i) [85].

These HER diagnostics are valuable because they can show whether HER suppression is spatially uniform or whether local gas evolution hotspots persist within the felt.This point is especially important when a strategy appears to improve cycling performance or enable a higher charging cut-off voltage,because such gains are only convincing if accompanied by direct evidence of reduced bubble formation under relevant operating conditions.More broadly,HER detection is not only a selectivity metric,but also a durability and techno-economic one: for preloading,persistent HER erodes the long-term value of a fixed catalytic layer,whereas for in-situ construction it determines whether continuous metal-salt addition is justified and whether extra replenishment or maintenance burdens are introduced during long-term operation.Taken together,the diagnostics summarized in this section are intended to function as an evidence framework for later strategy comparison.

# 3.Strategies for metal-based multifunctional catalytic layer fabrication

In recent years,there have been many reports on metal-based multifunctional catalytic layers in VFB.Fig.6 illustrates representative milestones involving Bi, Sb,Ag,Sn and Cu,showing a clear progression from early screening of different metals to recent structure-engineered catalysts [84,86-9O] As research into metal-based multifunctional catalytic layers deepens,various metal elements and loading structures have emerged,such as single-atom Bi, which has enabled the battery to achieve an EE of $8 3 . 5 \%$ ata current density of $3 0 0 \mathrm { \ m A \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ [91]. Meanwhile,bimetallic combinations are being actively pursued to leverage synergistic effects.Building on established in-situ deposition routes,aparticularlyrecent interest isin-situ multimetallic co-deposition to generate new alloy phases and tailored architectures directly on carbon felts [84].Motivated by these developments,the following sections compare two loading approaches: preloading and in-situ construction.

# 3.1.Preloading metal-based multifunctional catalytic layer

By adjusting the microstructure and employing unique loading techniques,researchers integrate metal-based multifunctional catalytic layerinto electrodes to achieve enhanced battery performance.A broad range of metals and architectures has been explored,with Bi-based catalysts appearing most frequently and generally showing effective performance gains (Table 2).Notably,synthesis complexity does not necessarily correlate with battery metrics,and cross-study benchmarking based solely on EE/power density is intrinsically limited because operating conditions and VFB configurations vary substantially. One reason is that full-cell performance in thick porous felts reflects not only intrinsic catalytic activity, but also loading uniformity, wettability, accessible surface area,pore connectivity,and reactant transport under flow.Consequently,a structurally sophisticated catalyst may show favorable nanoscale descriptors yet deliver only modest full-cell gains if its additional active sites are not effectively utilized throughout the felt, if the coating perturbs transport pathways,or if the apparent improvement mainly originates from morphology-driven wetting effects rather than a genuine reduction in the $\mathrm { V } ^ { 3 + } / \mathrm { V } ^ { 2 + }$ reaction barrier. This is why operando and spatially resolved diagnostics are essential for separating true catalytic effects from morphology-assisted improvements.Therefore,the superiority of complex preloaded architectures should be judged against simpler controls under matched flow-cell conditions, rather than by nanostructural sophistication alone.

Ag nanoparticles possess high electrical conductivity and high electrocatalytic activity,which can significantly reduce the activation energy of redox reactions and accelerate the reaction kinetics.Li et al. achieved uniform deposition of $3 0 { - } 8 0 \mathrm { n m }$ silver nanoparticles on carbon fibers by adjusting the deposition current density and the loading amount of silver (Fig.7a)[99]. Under a current density of $2 0 0 \mathrm { \ m A c m ^ { - 2 } }$ ， the EE can reach $8 2 \%$ (Fig.7b).To address the trade-off that highly active Ag sites may also aggravate hydrogen evolution,Xing et al.reported an $\mathbf { A g } / \mathbf { S } \mathbf { n }$ dual-atom electrocatalyst $( \mathrm { A g / S n / \mathrm { \ell } } \mathrm { D A s } )$ that enables synergistic electrocatalysis for the $\mathrm { V } ^ { 3 + } / \mathrm { V } ^ { 2 + }$ reaction while suppressing HER.Adjacent Sn tunes the electronic structure of $\mathbf { A } \mathbf { g }$ (including the D-band center)and moves the preferred H-adsorption site to Sn, thereby lowering the dehydration barrier while increasing HER overpotential (Fig.7c) [92].Additionally,Fang et al.uniformly embedded Sb-based electrocatalystsinto electrospuncarbon nanofibers, thereby increasing the effective functional groups and surface defects of the electrode (Fig.7d)[1Oo].As a result, the hydrophilicity,specific surface area,and electrocatalytic activity of the electrode were significantly enhanced.

Bi,as the most extensively studied metal catalyst,boasts several advantages such as relatively low cost,favorable catalytic activity,and high hydrogen evolution overpotential [93]． Researchershave employed various methods to load Bi in different structures onto carbon fibers. The same electrolyte-borne nature that makes in-situ construction attractive also defines its main failure pathways at the materials and interface levels.Dissolution-redeposition can become uncontrolled, leading to particle coarsening,nonuniform through-plane distribution, and loss of accessible surface area.Meanwhile,detached particles or crossover metal ions can contaminate the positive electrolyte and create compatibility risks for the membrane,positive electrode,and long-term electrolyte management.Moreover,repeated additive replenishment may introduce impurity accumulation and gradually alter interfacial chemistry.

# 4. Summary and perspectives

Achieving an electrode with comprehensive benefits requires a focus on five dimensions: cost-effectiveness,activity,selectivity,stability,and scalability (Fig.11).Considerable progress has been made on the first three dimensions,whereas the last two remain less systematically addressed in many catalyst-centered studies [111-113]. Currently, the global vanadium organization VANITEC has listed installed plants worldwide with a total capacity exceeding 1.5 GWh [113].As a batery technology that has been initially commercialized, the high cost of VFB limits itscompetitiveness[1l4]．Moreover，like other already commercialized energy storage batteries,large-scale development is a necessary path for improving technological maturity and is also an essential criterion for evaluating electrodes [115].From this broader deployment perspective,in-situ construction is the promising pathway for practical VFB negative electrodes.This conclusion does not diminish the scientific value of preloading; instead,it reflects how the two paradigms scale differently once laboratory demonstrations are translated to large-area felts and commercial stacks.

# 4.1.Common-basis comparison across five dimensions

Preloading offers strong control over catalyst identity, loading amount, local coordination environment,and hierarchical architecture before cell assembly.This makes it highly valuable for catalyst discovery,mechanistic interrogation,and proof-of-concept demonstrations. However,once the target shifts from small laboratory electrodes to stack-relevant felts,the practical burden of preloading rises sharply.The challenge is not only catalyst material cost,but also the area-dependent overhead associated with large-electrode handling,multistep fabrication,solvent processing,drying or thermal treatment, uniformity control through the felt thickness,and batch-to-batch quality assurance.Superior laboratory performance of preloaded electrodes therefore does not necessarily translate linearly into stack-level benefit,because the apparent gain may reflect not only intrinsic catalytic activity but also altered wetting,accessible surface area,pore architecture,and local mass transfer.

In contrast, in-situ construction exploits the negative electrode potential window to build the catalytic layer directly inside the battery, under the same electrochemical and hydrodynamic environment in which it finally operates.This route sacrifices some degree of structural precision,but it offers several decisive practical advantages.First,it avoids the need for dedicated catalyst-loading treatment on very large electrodes before stack assembly. Second, the flowing electrolyte can access the three-dimensional felt framework more naturally than many ex-situ routes,making catalyst formation more compatible with thick porous electrodes.Third,implementation is strongly compatible with existing stack hardware and felt supply chains,because the main intervention shifts from electrode-side fabrication to electrolyte-side engineering.

A more useful common-basis synthesis is therefore scenario-based rather than a strict ranking across inherently non-identical studies. Interpreted through the diagnostics summarized in Section 2,the key question is not only whether a catalyst improves cell-level effciency,but whether it produces spatially uniform activity,suppresses HER under relevant operating conditions,and remains effective throughout the 3D felt during prolonged flow-assisted cycling.In terms of activity,both preloading and in-situ construction can markedly accelerate the $\mathrm { V } ^ { 3 + } /$ $\scriptstyle { \bar { \mathbf { V } } } ^ { 2 + }$ reaction,but preloading is beter suited to isolating intrinsic site effects under deliberately designed structures，whereas in-situ construction is judged more by whether the deposited catalyst penetrates, reconstructs,and remains active across the felt thickness under practical operation.In terms of selectivity, the most convincing evidence is not a higher EE alone,but a concurrent reduction in HER signatures.In terms of stability，preloading benefits from predefined interfaces but may suffer irreversible performance loss once detachment, coarsening, delamination，or pore blockage occurs,while in-situ layers may continuously reconstruct yet retain the practical advantage that catalytic function can in principle be replenished and redistributed during operation. In terms of cost-effectiveness and scalability, preloading remains constrained by area-dependent fabrication burden,whereas insitu construction shifts the intervention from electrode-side processing to electrolyte-side conditioning and is therefore intrinsically more compatible with large-area felts and stack manufacturing. Accordingly, preloading is most valuable as a platform for catalyst discovery and mechanistic validation,while in-situ construction is presently more attractive for deployment-oriented VFB when activity,selectivity,stability,cost-effectiveness,and scalability are considered together.

Under the common system-level normalization adopted above，such area-dependent overheads are only competitive with the Bi additive near the absolute low end of the range, whereas across most of the plausible range preloading remains substantially more expensive than in-situ construction on a system-energy basis.Importantly,this comparison is still conservative for in-situ construction because it mainly counts additive cost and a limited conditioning penalty，whereaspreloading becomes substantially less favorable once large-area processing equipment, yield loss,and quality assurance are fully included (Fig. 12).

# 4.3.Durability-linked economics and operation-integrated maintenance

The principal caveat preventing in-situ construction from being an unqualified winner is not its first-order cost,but the need to control longterm deposition and reconstruction behavior.In particular,cross-over and positive-side compatibility,impurity accumulation,and the stability of the in-situ formed layer under continuous cycling remain important deployment challenges.At the same time,a more meaningful comparison must distinguish intrinsic deposit stability from system-level maintainability.For preloading,common degradation pathways include catalyst detachment, coarsening,delamination,pore blockage,and loss of interfacial accessibility. Once these occur,remediation often requires ex-situ intervention,electrode refurbishment,or even replacement, which becomes increasingly disruptive and expensive at stack scale.In contrast,the characteristic risks of in-situ construction are additive depletion,dissolution or redeposition drift,cross-over,and morphology evolution of the deposited phase.

Although these in-situ phenomena must be carefully managed, they are inherently more compatible with electrolyte-side and operation-side maintenance,such as replenishment,intermittent reconstruction, deposition-protocol optimization,and electrolyte rebalancing,all of which can in principle be implemented without dismantling the stack or reloading the electrode externally.For this reason，future technoeconomic assessments should move beyond one-off material cost and adopta durability-linked,system-normalized metric such as:

# 4.4.Scope and outlook

The present Review focuses on metal-based catalytic layers because the central methodological contrast emphasized here,namely preloading versus electrolyte-mediated in-situ construction,is currently most directly developed for metal-ion precursors and electrodeposited metalcontaining phases.By comparison,non-metal-based catalytic systems, suchasheteroatom-/defect-engineeredcarbonandsurfacefunctionalized carbon—have demonstrated clear value for improving $\mathrm { V } ^ { 2 + } / \mathrm { V } ^ { 3 + }$ kinetics and suppressing HER, and they remain an essential reference framework for negative-electrode design [27,32-34,42]. However,these systems are more often realized as pre-fabricated electrode modifications than as electrolyte-mediated in-situ deposited phases，so a like-for-like comparison of preloading versus in-situ construction，maintenance mode,and cost-scaling is presently less direct. This focus therefore defines the scope of this Review around a class of systems for which those questions can currently be compared on a common basis.

Moreover,the extension of in-situ construction to other aqueous flow-battery chemistries is especially encouraging.Pb-based in-situ deposition has already shown benefits in iron-chromium flow batteries and zinc-bromine systems,indicating that the electrolyte-as-precursor concept is not unique to VFB [1O7,108]. At the same time, cross-chemistry transferability should be regarded as conditional rather than universal. It depends on at least four factors: precursor solubility andchemical stability in the target electrolyte,whether the accessible negative-electrode potential window can drive selective deposition, compatibility with membranes and the positive electrolyte,and whether the deposited phase remains sufficiently stable under repeated flow-assisted cycling.These factors define the design boundary of in-situ construction, but they do not weaken its strategic significance.Rather, they suggest that in-situ construction should be developed as a chemistry-aware platform technology for aqueous flow batteries.

Within VFB,this also means that future in-situ design should shift from empirical metal-salt addition to mechanism-guided control of deposition sequence，precursor coordination，pulse or conditioning protocols,and electrolyte composition,especially for multi-metal systems in which alloy or core-shell evolution governs the balance between activity and HER suppression.

Overall,while preloading will remain indispensable for catalyst screening,mechanistic analysis,and minimal seed design,the current body of evidence points more strongly toward in-situ construction as the main practical route for future VFB negative electrodes. By embedding catalyst formation into routine battery operation,this strategy minimizes additional manufacturing steps,avoids dedicated large-area catalyst fabrication on thick porous felts,and shifts the dominant cost driver from area-based processing overhead to low-dose electrolyte additives.More importantly，because catalyst replenishment and reconstruction can in principle be carried out inside the battery,in-situ approaches offer a realistic pathway to combine kinetics enhancement, HER suppression,scalable implementation,and operation-integrated maintenance without major hardware replacement. Looking forward, a particularly promising next step is an in-situ-dominant hybrid paradigm, in which only a minimal seed architecture is introduced ex situ, while the functional catalyst phase is largely built and renewed electrochemically during operation. Such a route would preserve the practical advantages of in-situ construction while selectively borrowing the structural guidance of preloading.In this sense,the future of catalytic negative electrodes is unlikely to be defined by increasingly complex pre-fabrication alone;rather,it will be defined by how effectively catalyst construction can be internalized into battery operation itself.
